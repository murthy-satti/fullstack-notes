    Compliance and Audit Readiness¶

    Compliance means showing, to customers, auditors, and regulators, that agreed security and privacy controls exist and keep working. For an engineering team it is mostly operational: who can access production, whether data is encrypted, what is logged and for how long, how changes are reviewed, how data is deleted, and whether you can prove all of it for a whole audit period. This lesson covers what SOC 2, ISO/IEC 27001, HIPAA, GDPR, and PCI DSS ask of operations, the shared responsibility model, encryption at rest and in transit, audit logs, access reviews, evidence collection, data retention and deletion, business associate agreements for health data, and policy as code.

    It builds on the previous lessons: least privilege and access reviews in identity and access management, and patch deadlines in vulnerability and patch management.

    Not legal advice

    This page summarizes frameworks at a high level for engineers. Which laws and standards apply to you, and what they require in your case, depends on your contracts, data, and jurisdictions, and the texts change: proposals to amend the GDPR and the HIPAA Security Rule, and draft 2027 SOC 2 criteria, were all pending at the time of writing. Confirm with your legal, privacy, or compliance owner and the official texts linked below.

    Why it matters¶

    Customers increasingly ask for a SOC 2 report or an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate before they sign, hospitals need a business associate agreement before they share health data, and card brands require PCI DSS compliance before you touch card numbers. Regulators can fine heavily: GDPR Article 83 allows fines of up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover for the most serious infringements.

    For engineers, the expensive part is not the controls themselves (most are good practice anyway) but proving them after the fact. Teams that build evidence into their pipelines, such as reviewed pull requests, policy checks, locked audit logs, and recorded access reviews, pass audits with an export. Teams that do not spend weeks assembling screenshots and discover that a control silently stopped working months ago.

    Real-world usage¶

    • AICPA SOC 2. The AICPA describes SOC 2 as a report on controls at a service organization relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, or privacy, the five Trust Services Criteria categories. A Type 1 report covers the design of controls at a point in time; a Type 2 report also covers how they operated over a period. Reports use the 2017 Trust Services Criteria (with revised points of focus, 2022); in October 2026 the AICPA published exposure drafts of proposed 2027 criteria, which are not yet in effect.
    • ISO/IEC 27001:2022. The current edition of ISO/IEC 27001 specifies requirements for an information security management system (ISMS); Amendment 1:2024 added climate action changes to the context clauses. Its Annex A lists 93 controls grouped into organizational, people, physical, and technological themes.
    • HHS and HIPAA. The US Department of Health and Human Services publishes the HIPAA Security Rule (administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic protected health information) and guidance on cloud computing, which explains that a cloud provider storing electronic PHI is a business associate even if it only stores encrypted data it cannot read.
    • GDPR. Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the General Data Protection Regulation on EUR-Lex, requires security appropriate to the risk (Article 32, which names encryption and pseudonymisation), breach notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours where feasible (Article 33), and erasure on request when its conditions are met (Article 17).
    • PCI Security Standards Council. The Council's announcement Just Published: PCI DSS v4.0.1 describes the June 2024 limited revision of PCI DSS v4.0, which clarified requirements without adding or removing any. PCI DSS v4.0 was retired at the end of 2024, so v4.0.1 is the version assessments use.

    Popular tools and providers: AWS Artifact, AWS Config conformance packs, AWS Security Hub CSPM, AWS Audit Manager, CloudTrail, Azure Policy and Microsoft Defender for Cloud regulatory compliance, Google Cloud Audit Logs and Security Command Center, Open Policy Agent, Checkov, Kyverno, and compliance automation platforms such as Vanta, Drata, and Secureframe.

    How it works¶

    What each framework asks of operations¶

    Framework What it is Who it applies to What it asks of operations (high level)
    SOC 2 An attestation report by a CPA firm against the AICPA Trust Services Criteria Service organizations whose customers ask for it (common for B2B SaaS) Controls for access, change management, monitoring, incident response, vendor management, and backups, with evidence that they operated through the audit period (Type 2)
    ISO/IEC 27001:2022 A certifiable standard for an information security management system Any organization that wants certification A risk assessment, a Statement of Applicability choosing Annex A controls, internal audits, management review, and continual improvement, checked by surveillance audits
    HIPAA US law and regulations for protected health information Covered entities (health plans, clearinghouses, most providers) and their business associates Risk analysis, access control, audit controls, integrity, authentication, transmission security, contingency plans, BAAs, breach notification, documentation kept 6 years
    GDPR EU regulation on personal data Organizations processing personal data of people in the EU, under its territorial rules Security of processing, data protection by design, records of processing, processor contracts, data subject rights such as access and erasure, breach notification within 72 hours
    PCI DSS v4.0.1 An industry standard from the PCI Security Standards Council Anyone who stores, processes, or transmits payment card account data Network controls, secure configuration, protected stored card data, strong cryptography in transit, patching, need-to-know access, MFA, logging, regular testing

    A few concrete operational numbers that come up constantly:

    • PCI DSS 10.5.1: keep audit log history for at least 12 months, with at least the most recent three months immediately available for analysis.
    • PCI DSS 7.2.4: review all user accounts and their access privileges at least once every six months.
    • PCI DSS 6.3.3: install critical-severity security patches within one month of release.
    • HIPAA: retain required Security Rule documentation for six years. Notify affected individuals of a breach of unsecured PHI without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery.
    • GDPR Article 12(3): answer data subject requests (such as erasure) without undue delay and within one month, extendable by two further months where necessary.

    In January 2025 HHS published a proposed rule to update the HIPAA Security Rule (for example, making encryption and multi-factor authentication required rather than "addressable"). At the time of writing it was still a proposal, and the current rule remains in force; check HHS for its status.

    Shared responsibility¶

    A cloud provider's certifications cover its side of the line: the AWS shared responsibility model calls it security of the cloud, while the customer is responsible for security in the cloud. Practically:

    • Download the provider's reports (AWS Artifact, the Microsoft Service Trust Portal, Google's compliance reports manager) and give them to your auditor for the inherited controls, such as physical security.
    • Everything you configure is yours to prove: bucket policies, security groups, IAM, encryption settings, logging, backups, and your application's access control.
    • Each vendor that handles regulated data for you adds its own line and its own contract (a data processing agreement under GDPR, a BAA under HIPAA).

    Encryption at rest and in transit¶

    At rest In transit
    What Disks, databases, object storage, backups, snapshots, logs Browser to edge, edge to load balancer, service to service, service to database
    How Provider-managed encryption on by default, or customer-managed keys in a KMS with envelope encryption TLS 1.2 or later (1.3 preferred), HSTS for websites, TLS to databases and caches, mTLS in a service mesh
    What auditors check Encryption enabled everywhere, who can use the keys, key rotation, encrypted backups No plaintext protocols, current TLS versions and ciphers, certificates managed and renewed
    Framework hooks GDPR Art. 32, HIPAA addressable encryption, PCI DSS requirement 3, ISO A.8.24 GDPR Art. 32, HIPAA transmission security, PCI DSS requirement 4, ISO A.8.24

    Encryption at rest in the cloud mostly protects against lost media and some insider paths; it does not protect against a stolen credential that can read the data through the API. That is why key policies matter: customer-managed keys let you separate who can administer data from who can decrypt it, and every decryption is logged. Under HIPAA, data encrypted consistently with HHS guidance counts as "secured", which affects whether a lost device or leaked file is a reportable breach. For secrets and signing keys see secrets management; for TLS see TLS and certificate fundamentals and network security in the cloud.

    Audit logs¶

    Every framework asks for a record of who did what, when, and from where, protected from tampering and kept long enough to investigate. Collecting is not enough: the HIPAA Security Rule, for example, requires regularly reviewing records of information system activity such as audit logs and access reports, so pair logs with alerts and a review routine.

    • AWS CloudTrail records API calls. Event history keeps 90 days of management events per Region for free; for anything longer, create a multi-Region trail (an organization trail for all accounts) that delivers to an S3 bucket, ideally in a separate log-archive account. Turn on log file integrity validation, which writes signed hourly digest files that prove whether log files were modified or deleted, and protect the bucket with S3 Object Lock so not even an administrator can delete logs early. Data events (such as S3 object reads) are not recorded by default and cost extra; enable them for buckets holding regulated data.
    • Google Cloud Audit Logs: Admin Activity logs are always written; Data Access logs, except for BigQuery, are disabled by default and must be enabled for services holding sensitive data.
    • Azure: the Activity log records control-plane operations and keeps them for 90 days; export it with diagnostic settings for longer retention, alongside Microsoft Entra ID sign-in and audit logs.
    • Application audit logs are your responsibility: who viewed which patient record, who exported which report, who changed whose role. Log identifiers, not the sensitive data itself, and treat these logs as regulated data (see structured logging).

    Access reviews¶

    Access reviews confirm, on a fixed cadence, that every person and workload still needs the access it has. The mechanics (identity center exports, unused-access findings, one ticket per removal) are covered in identity and access management. For compliance, what matters is that the review happens on schedule (PCI DSS sets a maximum of six months; quarterly is common for production), that a named owner signs off, and that every removal is recorded. A review nobody can show did not happen.

    Evidence collection¶

    Auditors usually work with a population and samples: "list every production change in the period", then "for these 25, show the approval, the tests, and the deploy". Design so that the answer is a query:

    • One control, many frameworks. Map each internal control (such as "production changes are reviewed") to every framework it satisfies, and collect its evidence once.
    • Evidence from systems, not people: pull request approvals and branch protection settings, CI logs, Terraform plans and policy results, cloud configuration rule results, access review tickets, backup and restore test reports, incident postmortems, training records.
    • Timestamped and append-only: store exports in a bucket with versioning or Object Lock so evidence cannot be edited afterwards.
    • Continuous, not annual: a control that silently broke in month two fails a Type 2 audit in month twelve. Alert when a check fails.

    Data retention and deletion¶

    GDPR's storage limitation principle (Article 5) and most customer contracts require that personal data is kept no longer than needed, and deleted when a valid erasure request arrives. Make it operational:

    • A retention schedule: for each data type, how long, why (contract, law, legitimate need), and the mechanism that enforces it.
    • Mechanisms, not intentions: MongoDB TTL indexes, S3 lifecycle rules, log group retention settings, and scheduled jobs.
    • Erasure across copies: primary databases, search indexes, caches, analytics, data warehouses, file storage, and processors (email and support tools). Keep a record that the erasure happened without keeping the data.
    • Backups: editing backups is impractical; common practice is short backup retention, encrypted backups, and re-applying completed erasures after any restore. Write that down in your policy.
    • Exceptions: legal holds and legal obligations (such as accounting records) override deletion for specific data; restrict access to it.
    • Crypto-shredding: encrypting each tenant's data with its own key lets you make it unreadable everywhere, including backups, by destroying the key.

    BAAs for health data¶

    Under HIPAA, a covered entity (or a business associate) must have a business associate agreement with any vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on its behalf. HHS publishes sample BAA provisions. For cloud providers, rely only on what their official pages state:

    • AWS offers a Business Associate Addendum that customers review and accept in AWS Artifact, and its HIPAA page says to process, store, and transmit PHI only in HIPAA-eligible services.
    • Microsoft states on its Azure HIPAA page that the HIPAA BAA is available by default to covered entities and business associates through the Microsoft Product Terms and Data Protection Addendum, for in-scope services.
    • Google Cloud states on its HIPAA page that it enters into BAAs as necessary, covering its infrastructure and the listed products, and that unsupported services should not be used for PHI.

    For every other vendor in the data path (error tracking, email, SMS, analytics, support desks), check its official documentation; if it does not offer a BAA, PHI must not reach it. A BAA is necessary but not sufficient: Microsoft's page notes that using a BAA-covered service does not by itself make your solution compliant.

    Policy as code¶

    Policy as code turns rules such as "no public buckets" or "databases are encrypted" into automated checks:

    • Before deploy: check Terraform plans with Open Policy Agent, Checkov, or Trivy in pull requests (see IaC fundamentals and Terraform in practice).
    • In the cloud: AWS Config rules and conformance packs, Azure Policy, and Google Cloud organization policies evaluate or block the live configuration, including changes made outside Terraform.
    • In Kubernetes: admission controllers such as Kyverno or OPA Gatekeeper reject non-compliant workloads.
    • As evidence: every evaluation is timestamped, which is exactly what a Type 2 auditor wants to sample.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation Typical starting point
    B2B SaaS selling to mid-size and enterprise customers in the US SOC 2 Type 1, then Type 2
    Customers in Europe or regulated industries asking for certification ISO/IEC 27001
    Handling PHI for US covered entities HIPAA safeguards, BAAs with every vendor in the PHI path
    Personal data of people in the EU GDPR: records of processing, DPAs, rights handling, breach process
    Taking card payments PCI DSS; minimize scope with a hosted payment page or tokenization so card numbers never touch your servers
    Early-stage product with no regulated data and no customer demand Good security practice and a short security page; start formal programs when customers or data require them

    Avoid buying a compliance tool before you have controls to measure, and avoid expanding scope: keeping card numbers or PHI out of systems that do not need them is the cheapest control there is.

    Practical examples¶

    An audit trail that cannot be deleted¶

    # audit-trail.tf: every API call in every Region, written to a write-once bucket
data "aws_caller_identity" "current" {}
data "aws_region" "current" {}

locals {
  trail_name = "taskflow-audit"
  trail_arn  = "arn:aws:cloudtrail:${data.aws_region.current.region}:${data.aws_caller_identity.current.account_id}:trail/${local.trail_name}"
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket" "audit" {
  bucket              = "example-taskflow-audit-logs"
  object_lock_enabled = true # must be set when the bucket is created
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_versioning" "audit" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.audit.id
  versioning_configuration {
    status = "Enabled"
  }
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_object_lock_configuration" "audit" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.audit.id
  rule {
    default_retention {
      mode = "COMPLIANCE" # nobody, not even the root user, can delete or shorten it
      days = 400
    }
  }
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_public_access_block" "audit" {
  bucket                  = aws_s3_bucket.audit.id
  block_public_acls       = true
  block_public_policy     = true
  ignore_public_acls      = true
  restrict_public_buckets = true
}

data "aws_iam_policy_document" "cloudtrail_write" {
  statement {
    sid       = "CloudTrailAclCheck"
    actions   = ["s3:GetBucketAcl"]
    resources = [aws_s3_bucket.audit.arn]
    principals {
      type        = "Service"
      identifiers = ["cloudtrail.amazonaws.com"]
    }
    condition {
      test     = "StringEquals"
      variable = "aws:SourceArn"
      values   = [local.trail_arn]
    }
  }

  statement {
    sid       = "CloudTrailWrite"
    actions   = ["s3:PutObject"]
    resources = ["${aws_s3_bucket.audit.arn}/AWSLogs/${data.aws_caller_identity.current.account_id}/*"]
    principals {
      type        = "Service"
      identifiers = ["cloudtrail.amazonaws.com"]
    }
    condition {
      test     = "StringEquals"
      variable = "s3:x-amz-acl"
      values   = ["bucket-owner-full-control"]
    }
    condition {
      test     = "StringEquals"
      variable = "aws:SourceArn"
      values   = [local.trail_arn]
    }
  }
}

resource "aws_s3_bucket_policy" "audit" {
  bucket = aws_s3_bucket.audit.id
  policy = data.aws_iam_policy_document.cloudtrail_write.json
}

resource "aws_cloudtrail" "audit" {
  name                          = local.trail_name
  s3_bucket_name                = aws_s3_bucket.audit.id
  is_multi_region_trail         = true # one trail records every Region
  include_global_service_events = true # IAM, STS, and other global services
  enable_log_file_validation    = true # hourly signed digests prove nothing was altered

  depends_on = [aws_s3_bucket_policy.audit]
}

    Four hundred days of compliance-mode retention covers PCI DSS's 12-month minimum with margin. In an AWS Organization, put this bucket in a dedicated log-archive account and make the trail an organization trail, so that workload accounts cannot touch it.

    Continuous configuration checks as evidence¶

    # config-rules.tf: continuous checks that double as audit evidence
# (assumes an AWS Config recorder and delivery channel already exist in the account)
locals {
  managed_rules = {
    "cloudtrail-enabled"               = "CLOUD_TRAIL_ENABLED"
    "s3-bucket-versioning"             = "S3_BUCKET_VERSIONING_ENABLED"
    "s3-bucket-public-read-prohibited" = "S3_BUCKET_PUBLIC_READ_PROHIBITED"
    "ebs-volumes-encrypted"            = "ENCRYPTED_VOLUMES"
    "rds-storage-encrypted"            = "RDS_STORAGE_ENCRYPTED"
    "iam-user-mfa-enabled"             = "IAM_USER_MFA_ENABLED"
  }
}

resource "aws_config_config_rule" "managed" {
  for_each = local.managed_rules

  name = each.key
  source {
    owner             = "AWS"
    source_identifier = each.value
  }
}

    Each rule evaluates resources continuously and keeps a compliance history, so "were all volumes encrypted during the audit period?" becomes a report instead of a guess. Note that some rule names differ from their identifiers (cloudtrail-enabled is CLOUD_TRAIL_ENABLED). There is no rule for default S3 encryption here: since January 2023 Amazon S3 encrypts all new objects with SSE-S3 by default. Both files validate with Terraform 1.17.2 and AWS provider 6.73.0.

    Guardrails on every Terraform plan with Open Policy Agent¶

    # policy/terraform.rego: guardrails checked against `terraform show -json` output
package terraform.guardrails

# Changes that create or update something (ignore deletes and no-ops)
changes contains rc if {
    some rc in input.resource_changes
    some action in rc.change.actions
    action in {"create", "update"}
}

deny contains msg if {
    some rc in changes
    rc.type == "aws_s3_bucket_public_access_block"
    some setting in ["block_public_acls", "block_public_policy", "ignore_public_acls", "restrict_public_buckets"]
    rc.change.after[setting] != true
    msg := sprintf("%s: %s must be true", [rc.address, setting])
}

deny contains msg if {
    some rc in changes
    rc.type == "aws_vpc_security_group_ingress_rule"
    rc.change.after.cidr_ipv4 == "0.0.0.0/0"
    rc.change.after.from_port != 443
    msg := sprintf("%s: only port 443 may be open to the internet", [rc.address])
}

deny contains msg if {
    some rc in changes
    rc.type == "aws_db_instance"
    not rc.change.after.storage_encrypted
    msg := sprintf("%s: database storage must be encrypted", [rc.address])
}

    # policy/terraform_test.rego: unit tests for the guardrails (run with `opa test policy/`)
package terraform.guardrails_test

import data.terraform.guardrails

test_open_ssh_is_denied if {
    count(guardrails.deny) == 1 with input as {"resource_changes": [{
        "address": "aws_vpc_security_group_ingress_rule.ssh",
        "type": "aws_vpc_security_group_ingress_rule",
        "change": {"actions": ["create"], "after": {"cidr_ipv4": "0.0.0.0/0", "from_port": 22}},
    }]}
}

test_unencrypted_database_is_denied if {
    count(guardrails.deny) == 1 with input as {"resource_changes": [{
        "address": "aws_db_instance.main",
        "type": "aws_db_instance",
        "change": {"actions": ["create"], "after": {"storage_encrypted": false}},
    }]}
}

    terraform plan -out=tfplan
terraform show -json tfplan > plan.json
opa test policy/
opa eval --fail-defined --format pretty \
  --data policy/terraform.rego --input plan.json "data.terraform.guardrails.deny[msg]"

    Run against a plan that adds a public access block with block_public_acls = false and an SSH rule open to 0.0.0.0/0, the evaluation prints:

    [
  "aws_s3_bucket_public_access_block.exports: block_public_acls must be true",
  "aws_vpc_security_group_ingress_rule.ssh_anywhere: only port 443 may be open to the internet"
]

    --fail-defined makes the command exit with code 1 when any deny message exists, so it can gate a pull request. The policy uses the Rego v1 syntax of OPA 1.x (contains and if), passes opa fmt, and was tested with OPA 1.22.4 against a real terraform show -json plan. Conftest, a CLI built on OPA for testing configuration files, accepts the same syntax if you prefer it in CI.

    Keeping the policy run as evidence in CI¶

    # .github/workflows/terraform-policy.yml (excerpt)
jobs:
  policy:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    permissions:
      contents: read
      id-token: write
    defaults:
      run:
        working-directory: infra/envs/prod
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v7
      - uses: hashicorp/setup-terraform@dfe3c3f87815947d99a8997f908cb6525fc44e9e # v4.0.1
        with:
          terraform_wrapper: false
      - uses: aws-actions/configure-aws-credentials@v6
        with:
          role-to-assume: arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/taskflow-terraform-plan
          aws-region: eu-west-1
      - run: terraform init -input=false
      - run: terraform plan -input=false -lock=false -out=tfplan
      - run: terraform show -json tfplan > plan.json
      - name: Install OPA
        run: |
          curl -fsSL -o /usr/local/bin/opa \
            https://github.com/open-policy-agent/opa/releases/download/v1.22.4/opa_linux_amd64_static
          chmod +x /usr/local/bin/opa
      - name: Evaluate guardrails
        run: |
          opa eval --format pretty --data ../../../policy/terraform.rego --input plan.json \
            "data.terraform.guardrails.deny" | tee policy-result.txt
          opa eval --fail-defined --format pretty --data ../../../policy/terraform.rego \
            --input plan.json "data.terraform.guardrails.deny[msg]" > /dev/null
      - if: always()
        uses: actions/upload-artifact@v7
        with:
          name: policy-evidence-${{ github.sha }}
          path: infra/envs/prod/policy-result.txt

    The uploaded result ties a commit to the policies it passed. Artifact storage has its own retention limits, so for audit periods copy the results to the evidence bucket as well.

    Retention by TTL and an erasure job¶

    // scripts/retention-indexes.js: let MongoDB delete expiring data on its own
import { MongoClient } from "mongodb";

const client = new MongoClient(process.env.MONGODB_URI);
await client.connect();
const db = client.db();

// Sessions disappear 30 days after the user was last seen
await db.collection("sessions").createIndex(
  { lastSeenAt: 1 },
  { expireAfterSeconds: 60 * 60 * 24 * 30, name: "ttl_sessions_30d" },
);

// Password reset tokens live for one hour
await db.collection("passwordResets").createIndex(
  { createdAt: 1 },
  { expireAfterSeconds: 60 * 60, name: "ttl_password_resets_1h" },
);

await client.close();

    // src/privacy/erase-user.js: one job deletes a user's personal data everywhere we hold it
import { createHash } from "node:crypto";

export async function eraseUser(db, userId, { searchIndex, emailProvider }) {
  const user = await db.collection("users").findOne({ _id: userId });
  if (!user) return { status: "not_found" };

  await db.collection("tasks").deleteMany({ ownerId: userId });
  await db.collection("comments").deleteMany({ authorId: userId });
  await db.collection("sessions").deleteMany({ userId });
  await searchIndex.deleteByOwner(userId);           // copies outside MongoDB
  await emailProvider.deleteContact(user.email);      // processors too

  // Invoices stay (legal obligation); detach them from the person's profile
  await db.collection("invoices").updateMany(
    { userId },
    { $set: { customerName: "erased", customerEmail: null } },
  );

  await db.collection("users").deleteOne({ _id: userId });

  // Record that the erasure happened, without keeping who it was
  await db.collection("erasures").insertOne({
    subject: createHash("sha256").update(String(userId)).digest("hex"),
    completedAt: new Date(),
  });
  return { status: "erased" };
}

    A TTL index removes a document once its date field is older than expireAfterSeconds; MongoDB's background task runs about every minute, so expiry is not instant. The erasures collection lists the users to re-delete after any backup restore, and it is evidence that requests were handled on time. Whether invoices may keep a name depends on local accounting law; this is where your privacy owner decides, not the code.

    A retention schedule¶

    Data Retention Basis Mechanism
    Active account data While the account exists Contract Erasure job on account deletion
    Sessions 30 days after last use Security, legitimate need TTL index
    Application logs 90 days Operations, security Log retention setting; identifiers only
    Cloud audit logs 400 days PCI DSS 10.5.1, security investigations Object Lock on the audit bucket
    Backups 35 days Disaster recovery Backup retention policy; erasures re-applied on restore
    Invoices As required by accounting law Legal obligation Restricted access, scheduled deletion after the period
    Audit evidence Audit period plus one year Certification and attestation Versioned evidence bucket

    Best practices¶

    • Map each internal control to every framework it satisfies, and collect its evidence once, automatically.
    • Know your side of the shared responsibility line, and keep the provider's reports for the rest.
    • Encrypt everything at rest and in transit by default; control and log who can use the keys.
    • Send audit logs from every account and Region to a separate, locked account with integrity validation, kept at least as long as your strictest framework requires.
    • Run access reviews on schedule with named owners, and record every removal.
    • Check policies on every Terraform plan and continuously in the cloud; alert when a check fails.
    • Write a retention schedule and enforce it with TTLs, lifecycle rules, and jobs; plan erasure across every copy, including processors and backups.
    • Sign BAAs or DPAs before regulated data reaches a vendor, and keep regulated data out of systems that do not need it.
    • Rehearse the breach process, including the 72-hour GDPR clock and HIPAA's 60-day limit.

    In production¶

    • The auditor asks for a year of change approvals and nobody can produce them. Detect: evidence requests take weeks; some changes have no linked review. Cause: direct pushes, console changes, and approvals in chat. Fix: branch protection with required reviews, deploys only from CI, and exports of pull request and pipeline records to the evidence store.
    • CloudTrail had gaps for weeks. Detect: the cloudtrail-enabled rule reports non-compliance; digest validation shows missing files. Cause: a trail stopped or a bucket policy changed. Fix: an organization trail managed in code, Object Lock, Config rules and alerts on logging changes.
    • A departed engineer still had production access during the audit period. Detect: the access review sample shows an active account weeks after their last day. Cause: offboarding not tied to the identity provider, or IAM users outside single sign-on. Fix: single sign-on with automatic deprovisioning, no IAM users for people, and recorded reviews.
    • A restore brought back users who had asked to be erased. Detect: a complaint from a person who requested erasure. Cause: backups restored without re-applying erasures. Fix: keep the erasure list and run it as part of every restore procedure.
    • PHI showed up in a third-party error tracker without a BAA. Detect: a search of error events finds patient names in payloads. Cause: full request bodies attached to error reports. Fix: scrub or allowlist fields before sending, sign a BAA if the vendor offers one or stop sending such data, and assess whether a breach notification is required.
    • Encryption was enabled, but the same role could administer and read everything. Detect: key policies grant broad decrypt access. Cause: default keys with permissive access. Fix: customer-managed keys with separated administration and usage permissions, and alerts on unusual decrypt activity.

    Important terminology¶

    • SOC 2 Type 1 / Type 2: attestation reports on the design of controls at a point in time / on design and operating effectiveness over a period.
    • Trust Services Criteria: the AICPA criteria for security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy.
    • ISMS: an information security management system, the subject of ISO/IEC 27001.
    • Statement of Applicability: the ISO/IEC 27001 document stating which Annex A controls apply and why.
    • PHI / ePHI: protected health information / in electronic form.
    • Covered entity / business associate: organizations HIPAA applies to directly / vendors handling PHI on their behalf.
    • BAA: a business associate agreement required between those parties.
    • Controller / processor: under GDPR, who decides why and how data is processed / who processes it on the controller's behalf.
    • DPA: a data processing agreement between controller and processor.
    • Cardholder data environment (CDE): the systems that store, process, or transmit card data, which PCI DSS scopes.
    • Shared responsibility model: the split of security duties between cloud provider and customer.
    • Evidence: records that show a control operated, collected for auditors.
    • Retention schedule: how long each type of data is kept, why, and how deletion is enforced.
    • Crypto-shredding: making data unreadable by destroying its encryption key.
    • Policy as code: compliance and security rules written as automated, version-controlled checks.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Believing a cloud provider's certification makes your system compliant.
    • Treating compliance as a yearly project instead of continuous evidence.
    • Assuming a BAA or DPA with the cloud provider covers every other vendor.
    • Logging sensitive data (PHI, card numbers, passwords) in application logs to make debugging easier.
    • Keeping data "just in case", which increases risk and conflicts with storage limitation.
    • Promising deletion without accounting for backups, analytics, and processors.
    • Thinking encryption at rest stops attackers who hold valid credentials.
    • Equating passing an audit with being secure.
