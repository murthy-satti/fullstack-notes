    Health Probes and Rolling Updates¶

    Kubernetes can only keep an application available if it knows two things about each Pod: whether it can take traffic right now, and whether it is broken beyond repair. Health probes answer those questions. Rolling updates then use the answers to replace Pods a few at a time, and graceful termination makes sure the Pods being replaced finish their work before they disappear.

    This lesson covers liveness, readiness, and startup probes and exactly what each failure does, how to design probes that do not cause outages, the rolling update parameters maxSurge and maxUnavailable with a worked example, the full Pod termination sequence (endpoint removal, preStop, SIGTERM, the grace period, SIGKILL), PodDisruptionBudgets, and the kubectl rollout commands for watching and undoing a release.

    Why it matters¶

    Most deploy-time errors in Kubernetes come from these settings, not from the code being deployed. Without a readiness probe, new Pods receive traffic before they can serve it. Without graceful termination, every deploy cuts off in-flight requests and clients see 502 errors. A liveness probe that checks the database turns a short database outage into every API Pod restarting at once. A PodDisruptionBudget that allows no disruption blocks node upgrades indefinitely.

    Done well, the same settings give you zero-downtime deploys, automatic removal of unhealthy instances from load balancing, and rollouts that stop themselves when the new version is broken.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Google Cloud. The article Kubernetes best practices: Setting up health checks with readiness and liveness probes explains the difference between the two probes, why liveness probes need a generous initial delay or a startup phase, and how misconfigured probes cause restart loops.
    • Google Cloud. The companion article Kubernetes best practices: terminating with grace walks through the termination sequence: the Terminating state, the preStop hook, SIGTERM, the grace period, and SIGKILL.
    • Learnk8s. The guide Graceful shutdown and zero downtime deployments in Kubernetes shows that endpoint removal and SIGTERM happen in parallel, so an app can receive new requests after it was told to stop, and recommends waiting briefly before shutting down.
    • Google SRE. The workbook chapter Canarying releases describes releasing to a small part of the fleet first and judging it by health signals, the same idea that maxSurge, readiness gates, and progress deadlines automate in a rolling update.

    Popular tools and providers: kubectl rollout, Argo Rollouts and Flagger for canary and blue-green releases, Helm's --wait, the PodDisruptionBudget API, and cluster upgrade tooling that drains nodes respecting those budgets.

    How it works¶

    Three probes, three different questions¶

    Probe Question On repeated failure Runs
    startupProbe Has the app finished starting? Container killed and restarted From container start until its first success
    readinessProbe Can this Pod take traffic now? Endpoint marked not ready: no new traffic; no restart For the container's whole life
    livenessProbe Is the process stuck beyond self-recovery? Container killed and restarted For the container's whole life, after startup succeeds

    A Pod with no probes is considered ready as soon as its containers start, and it is restarted only if its process exits.

    Each probe uses one mechanism: httpGet (status 200 to 399 is success), tcpSocket (the port accepts a connection), exec (a command exits with 0), or grpc (the standard gRPC health checking protocol). And each has the same timing fields:

    Field Default Meaning
    initialDelaySeconds 0 Wait before the first probe
    periodSeconds 10 Time between probes
    timeoutSeconds 1 A probe slower than this counts as a failure
    failureThreshold 3 Consecutive failures before acting
    successThreshold 1 Consecutive successes to count as healthy again (must be 1 for liveness and startup)

    The arithmetic matters. A liveness probe with the defaults restarts a container after about 30 seconds of failures (3 × 10 s). A startup probe with periodSeconds: 10 and failureThreshold: 30 allows up to 300 seconds to start, while the liveness probe stays disabled; the moment startup succeeds, liveness takes over with its own short threshold. That is far better than a large initialDelaySeconds on the liveness probe, which delays failure detection forever after.

    Designing probes that do not cause outages¶

    • Liveness must not depend on other services. If /healthz checks MongoDB and MongoDB has a two-minute outage, every API Pod fails liveness and restarts, losing caches and connections, and all of them reconnect at once when MongoDB returns. Liveness should answer only "is this process able to respond at all?" For Node.js, a cheap HTTP handler on the same event loop already detects a blocked or deadlocked loop.
    • Readiness reflects this instance's ability to serve. Good checks: startup work finished, the app's own connection pool is established, the process is not shutting down. Be careful with shared dependencies: if every replica fails readiness because of one external service, all endpoints disappear together. Often it is better to stay ready and return fast errors (with a circuit breaker) than to vanish from the load balancer.
    • Keep probes cheap and give them time. The default one-second timeout is easy to exceed under CPU throttling or a garbage-collection pause, which turns load into restarts and more load. Use a few seconds of timeout and a failure threshold that tolerates a blip.
    • Use a startup probe for slow starters (migrations, cache warming, JIT-heavy runtimes) instead of stretching liveness timing.
    • Fail readiness during shutdown. Flipping /readyz to 503 on SIGTERM is a cheap extra safety net beside the preStop delay.

    Rolling updates¶

    A Deployment's default strategy, RollingUpdate, replaces Pods gradually by scaling a new ReplicaSet up and the old one down, within two limits:

    • maxSurge: how many Pods may exist above replicas during the update. Percentages round up.
    • maxUnavailable: how many Pods may be unavailable below replicas. Percentages round down.

    Both default to 25%. The alternative strategy, Recreate, deletes all old Pods before creating new ones: downtime, but never two versions at once.

    Worked example with 4 replicas: maxSurge = 25% of 4 = 1, and maxUnavailable = 25% of 4 = 1. So at most 5 Pods exist and at least 3 are available at every moment.

    The key word is available: a new Pod counts only after its readiness probe passes and it has stayed ready for minReadySeconds (default 0). That is what makes rolling updates safe. If version 2 never becomes ready, the controller cannot remove more old Pods, and the rollout stalls with three old Pods still serving. After progressDeadlineSeconds (default 600) without progress, the Deployment's Progressing condition becomes False with reason ProgressDeadlineExceeded. Kubernetes does not roll back automatically; your pipeline or kubectl rollout undo must.

    Settings Behaviour Needs
    maxSurge: 25%, maxUnavailable: 25% (default) Balanced speed and capacity Some spare room
    maxSurge: 1, maxUnavailable: 0 Never below full capacity; one extra Pod at a time Room for one extra Pod
    maxSurge: 0, maxUnavailable: 1 No extra Pods; capacity dips by one No spare room
    maxSurge: 100%, maxUnavailable: 0 Full new set first, then old removed Double capacity briefly

    Graceful termination¶

    Every rolling update deletes old Pods, so how a Pod stops matters as much as how a new one starts. When a Pod is deleted:

    1. The API server sets the Pod's deletionTimestamp; it shows as Terminating.
    2. In parallel: the EndpointSlice controller marks the endpoint as terminating (not ready), and every node's kube-proxy and every ingress controller update their rules, which takes from milliseconds to a few seconds; and the kubelet starts shutting the Pod down.
    3. The kubelet runs the container's preStop hook, if any, and waits for it.
    4. The kubelet sends SIGTERM (or the image's STOPSIGNAL) to the container's main process.
    5. The app stops accepting connections, finishes in-flight requests, closes pools, and exits.
    6. If containers are still running when terminationGracePeriodSeconds (default 30) has elapsed, counted from step 1 and including the preStop time, the kubelet sends SIGKILL.

    Because steps 2 and 3 race, an app that exits immediately on SIGTERM can still receive requests from proxies that have not yet updated, and those clients see connection errors. A short preStop sleep (5 to 10 seconds) keeps the app serving until the endpoint removal has spread. Kubernetes has a built-in sleep action for this (enabled by default since 1.30), so the image needs no sleep binary. The app's own shutdown logic is the handler from writing production Dockerfiles, and its total time must fit inside the grace period.

    PodDisruptionBudgets¶

    Kubernetes distinguishes voluntary disruptions (draining a node for an upgrade, the cluster autoscaler removing a node, an operator evicting Pods) from involuntary ones (a node crashing, a kernel panic). A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) limits voluntary disruptions: the eviction API refuses to evict a Pod if that would leave fewer than minAvailable (or more than maxUnavailable) Pods of the selected set.

    PDBs do not apply to a Deployment's own rolling update (that is governed by maxUnavailable) and cannot prevent involuntary failures. A PDB that allows zero disruptions (minAvailable equal to the replica count) blocks node drains and cluster upgrades until someone intervenes. Setting unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: AlwaysAllow lets broken Pods be evicted even when the budget is exhausted, so they cannot block a drain.

    Rollout commands¶

    Command Use
    kubectl rollout status deploy/taskflow-api --timeout=5m Wait for a rollout; non-zero exit on failure or timeout (use in CI)
    kubectl rollout history deploy/taskflow-api List revisions and their change causes
    kubectl rollout undo deploy/taskflow-api Return to the previous revision
    kubectl rollout undo deploy/taskflow-api --to-revision=3 Return to a specific revision
    kubectl rollout pause / resume Batch several changes into one rollout
    kubectl rollout restart deploy/taskflow-api Replace all Pods with the same template (picks up config changes)

    The CHANGE-CAUSE column in the history comes from the kubernetes.io/change-cause annotation; set it in your pipeline when you deploy (the old --record flag is deprecated).

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Workload Probes Strategy
    HTTP API (TaskFlow API) Readiness on /readyz, liveness on cheap /healthz, startup if boot is slow RollingUpdate with maxUnavailable: 0
    Background worker (no port) Liveness via exec or a small health port; readiness rarely needed RollingUpdate
    App that must never run two versions at once (incompatible migrations) As above Recreate, or better, make versions compatible
    Very slow start (JVM, large caches) Startup probe with a generous threshold RollingUpdate with minReadySeconds
    Batch Job Usually none Not a Deployment

    Do not add a liveness probe just because you can: if the process exits when it breaks, the kubelet already restarts it. Add one when the app can hang without exiting.

    Practical examples¶

    Probe endpoints in the API¶

    // src/health.js: liveness and readiness handlers for Express
import mongoose from "mongoose";

let shuttingDown = false;
export const markShuttingDown = () => { shuttingDown = true; };

// Liveness: the event loop responds. No dependency checks here.
export function healthz(_req, res) {
  res.status(200).send("ok");
}

// Readiness: this instance can serve right now.
export function readyz(_req, res) {
  const dbConnected = mongoose.connection.readyState === 1;
  if (shuttingDown || !dbConnected) {
    return res.status(503).send("not ready");
  }
  res.status(200).send("ready");
}

    // src/server.js (excerpt)
import { healthz, readyz, markShuttingDown } from "./health.js";

app.get("/healthz", healthz);
app.get("/readyz", readyz);

process.on("SIGTERM", () => {
  markShuttingDown();               // readiness fails from now on
  server.close(async () => {        // in-flight requests finish
    await mongoose.disconnect();
    process.exit(0);
  });
  setTimeout(() => process.exit(1), 20_000).unref();   // below the 30 s grace period
});

    Probes, rollout strategy, and termination in the Deployment¶

    # k8s/api-deployment.yaml (excerpt)
spec:
  replicas: 4
  minReadySeconds: 5
  progressDeadlineSeconds: 300
  strategy:
    type: RollingUpdate
    rollingUpdate:
      maxSurge: 1
      maxUnavailable: 0
  template:
    spec:
      terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 30
      containers:
        - name: api
          image: ghcr.io/example/taskflow-api:1.4.0
          ports:
            - name: http
              containerPort: 3000
          startupProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /healthz
              port: http
            periodSeconds: 5
            failureThreshold: 24          # up to 120 s to start
          readinessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /readyz
              port: http
            periodSeconds: 5
            timeoutSeconds: 2
            failureThreshold: 2
          livenessProbe:
            httpGet:
              path: /healthz
              port: http
            periodSeconds: 10
            timeoutSeconds: 3
            failureThreshold: 3
          lifecycle:
            preStop:
              sleep:
                seconds: 5                # let endpoint removal propagate

    The time budget adds up: 5 s of preStop, then up to 20 s of draining before the app's own timer exits, which is safely below the 30-second grace period. With maxSurge: 1 and maxUnavailable: 0, capacity never drops below four ready Pods during a release. On clusters older than 1.30, replace the sleep action with exec: {command: ["sleep", "5"]}, which requires a sleep binary in the image.

    Releasing and rolling back¶

    # Deploy a new version (normally done by CI or GitOps)
kubectl -n taskflow set image deploy/taskflow-api api=ghcr.io/example/taskflow-api:1.5.0
kubectl -n taskflow annotate deploy/taskflow-api \
  kubernetes.io/change-cause="release 1.5.0" --overwrite

# Wait for it; roll back automatically if it does not complete
kubectl -n taskflow rollout status deploy/taskflow-api --timeout=5m \
  || kubectl -n taskflow rollout undo deploy/taskflow-api

kubectl -n taskflow rollout history deploy/taskflow-api
kubectl -n taskflow get deploy taskflow-api \
  -o jsonpath='{.status.conditions[?(@.type=="Progressing")].reason}'

    A PodDisruptionBudget¶

    # k8s/api-pdb.yaml
apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: taskflow-api
  namespace: taskflow
spec:
  maxUnavailable: 1
  unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: AlwaysAllow
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app.kubernetes.io/name: taskflow-api

    kubectl -n taskflow get pdb taskflow-api
# NAME           MIN AVAILABLE   MAX UNAVAILABLE   ALLOWED DISRUPTIONS   AGE
# taskflow-api   N/A             1                 1                     2d

    maxUnavailable: 1 lets node drains proceed one API Pod at a time, however many replicas the autoscaler has added.

    Best practices¶

    • Give every serving container a readiness probe; add a liveness probe only for failure modes where the process hangs without exiting.
    • Keep liveness checks local and cheap; never check databases or other services from liveness.
    • Use startup probes for slow starts instead of long liveness delays.
    • Use timeouts of a few seconds and failure thresholds that tolerate a blip.
    • Handle SIGTERM: fail readiness, stop accepting, drain, close pools, exit well within the grace period.
    • Add a short preStop sleep so endpoint removal finishes before shutdown begins.
    • Use maxUnavailable: 0 for user-facing services when capacity allows, and set minReadySeconds and a realistic progressDeadlineSeconds.
    • Fail the pipeline on kubectl rollout status timeouts and roll back.
    • Give multi-replica services a PDB that allows at least one disruption.

    In production¶

    • Every API Pod restarts during a database outage. Detect: RESTARTS climbs on all Pods at the same time, and kubectl describe pod shows Liveness probe failed: HTTP probe failed with statuscode: 503 while MongoDB is down. Cause: the liveness endpoint checks the database. Fix: make liveness local only; reflect dependency trouble in readiness or in request errors.
    • Clients see 502 errors during every deploy. Detect: ingress or load balancer logs show upstream connection refused or reset errors only while a rollout runs. Cause: Pods exit as soon as they get SIGTERM, before every proxy has removed them, or in-flight requests are cut. Fix: a preStop sleep, a SIGTERM handler that drains, and a grace period longer than both.
    • A rollout hangs at "1 of 4 updated replicas are available". Detect: kubectl rollout status waits, the new Pods are Running but 0/1 ready, and the Deployment eventually reports ProgressDeadlineExceeded. Cause: the new version fails its readiness probe (bad config, missing Secret key, a probe path that changed). Fix: read kubectl describe pod and the logs, then kubectl rollout undo; automate the undo in CI.
    • Slow-starting Pods are killed in a loop. Detect: CrashLoopBackOff with Liveness probe failed events in the first minute, especially after adding startup work. Cause: liveness starts before the app can answer. Fix: add a startup probe whose failureThreshold × periodSeconds covers the worst-case start time.
    • Liveness failures under peak load make things worse. Detect: restarts correlate with traffic peaks and CPU throttling; probe events show context deadline exceeded. Cause: a one-second probe timeout on a busy process. Fix: raise timeoutSeconds and failureThreshold, keep the handler trivial, and give the container enough CPU.
    • A node drain never finishes. Detect: kubectl drain repeats Cannot evict pod as it would violate the pod's disruption budget. Cause: minAvailable equals the replica count, or some Pods are unhealthy and the budget is already used up. Fix: use maxUnavailable: 1, add replicas, and set unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: AlwaysAllow.
    • New Pods stay Pending during a rollout. Detect: the surge Pod reports 0/3 nodes are available: Insufficient cpu. Cause: maxSurge needs spare capacity that does not exist. Fix: a cluster autoscaler, smaller requests, or maxSurge: 0 with maxUnavailable: 1.

    Important terminology¶

    • Liveness probe: Decides whether to restart a container.
    • Readiness probe: Decides whether a Pod receives Service traffic.
    • Startup probe: Protects slow-starting containers; disables the other probes until it succeeds.
    • Rolling update: Replacing Pods gradually by scaling a new ReplicaSet up and the old one down.
    • maxSurge / maxUnavailable: The limits on extra Pods and missing available Pods during a rolling update.
    • minReadySeconds: How long a Pod must stay ready before it counts as available.
    • Progress deadline: The time after which a stalled rollout is marked as failed.
    • preStop hook: An action the kubelet runs before sending SIGTERM.
    • Termination grace period: The total time between deletion and SIGKILL.
    • PodDisruptionBudget: A limit on voluntary evictions for a set of Pods.
    • CrashLoopBackOff: The state of a container that keeps failing and is restarted with increasing delays.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Checking dependencies in liveness probes.
    • Using the same aggressive settings for liveness as for readiness.
    • Expecting a failing readiness probe to restart the container.
    • Using a long initialDelaySeconds instead of a startup probe.
    • Exiting immediately on SIGTERM without a preStop delay.
    • Setting a preStop sleep longer than the grace period allows.
    • Assuming Kubernetes rolls back a failed rollout automatically.
    • Believing PDBs protect against node crashes or apply to rolling updates.

    Revision questions¶

    1. What happens to a Pod when each of the three probes fails repeatedly?
    2. A startup probe has periodSeconds: 5 and failureThreshold: 24. How long may the app take to start, and what are liveness and readiness doing meanwhile?
    3. A Deployment has 10 replicas with the default maxSurge and maxUnavailable of 25%. What is the maximum number of Pods during a rollout, and the minimum number available?
    4. Why should a liveness probe not check MongoDB? What should readiness check instead?
    5. Describe the termination sequence, and explain why a preStop sleep reduces errors during deploys.
    6. A Pod has a 10-second preStop sleep, its app needs 25 seconds to drain, and the grace period is 30 seconds. What happens?
    7. Why does a stalled rollout not take the service down, and what does Kubernetes do after progressDeadlineSeconds?
    8. What does a PodDisruptionBudget protect against, and what does it not?
