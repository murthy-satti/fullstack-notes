    Backups¶

    A backup is a separate, protected copy of data taken at a point in time, kept so you can restore after the original is lost, corrupted, deleted by mistake, or encrypted by ransomware. Backups are the last line of defence: replication, redundancy, and high availability protect against machines failing, but only a backup protects against the data itself going wrong. And a backup is only as good as the last time someone successfully restored from it.

    What backups provide¶

    • Recovery from mistakes: a bad migration, a deleted collection, or a bug that overwrites records can be undone to a point before it happened.
    • Recovery from attacks: ransomware and malicious deletion are survivable if a copy exists that the attacker could not reach or change.
    • Disaster recovery: losing a region, an account, or a provider is survivable if data and the means to rebuild exist elsewhere.
    • Compliance and history: regulations, contracts, and audits often require data to be retained for set periods and restorable on demand.
    • Safer change: a fresh backup before a risky operation turns a potential catastrophe into an inconvenience.

    Backups also create obligations: they contain the same sensitive data as production, so they need encryption, access control, retention limits, and the ability to delete data when the law requires it.

    How a typical backup system works¶

    For TaskFlow API's MongoDB data and the files users upload:

    1. Decide what to protect and how much loss is acceptable. The recovery point objective (RPO) says how much recent data you may lose; the recovery time objective (RTO) says how long a restore may take.
    2. Back up on a schedule: a nightly snapshot or dump of the database, plus continuous capture of the oplog (or the WAL in PostgreSQL) for point-in-time recovery, and copies of object storage, configuration, secrets, and infrastructure state.
    3. Encrypt every backup with keys kept separately from the backups.
    4. Store copies offsite and immutably: another region and another account, with write-once retention (for example S3 Object Lock) so neither mistakes nor attackers can delete them early.
    5. Expire old backups on a retention schedule (daily, weekly, monthly).
    6. Restore regularly into a scratch environment and verify the result automatically: counts, checksums, and an application smoke test.

    Practical example¶

    A nightly logical backup of TaskFlow's MongoDB replica set, streamed directly into encrypted object storage, and the matching restore:

    # Back up: consistent dump (with oplog) from a secondary, compressed, to S3 with KMS encryption
set -o pipefail   # a failed mongodump must fail the job, not upload a truncated archive
mongodump --uri="$MONGODB_URI" --readPreference=secondary --oplog --archive --gzip \
  | aws s3 cp - "s3://taskflow-backups-example/mongodb/taskflow-$(date -u +%Y-%m-%dT%H%M%SZ).archive.gz" \
      --sse aws:kms --sse-kms-key-id "$BACKUP_KMS_KEY_ARN"
# Streams larger than 50 GB also need --expected-size <bytes> on aws s3 cp

# Restore into a scratch cluster, replaying the oplog captured during the dump
aws s3 cp "s3://taskflow-backups-example/mongodb/taskflow-2026-11-12T020000Z.archive.gz" - \
  | mongorestore --uri="$RESTORE_URI" --archive --gzip --oplogReplay --drop

    --oplog makes the dump consistent to a single moment even though writes continue during it. The restore goes into a separate environment first; restoring over production is the last step of a recovery, not the first. Larger databases usually move to snapshot-based backups, covered in the database lesson.

    Developer perspective and common misconceptions¶

    Start from the restore, not the backup: decide how fast you must be back and how much data you can lose, then design backups that meet those numbers, and prove it with scheduled restore tests. MongoDB-specific tools and options are covered in MongoDB backup and restore in the Databases section; this subject covers strategy, disaster recovery, and operations around them.

    • Replication is not a backup. A replica faithfully copies a dropped collection or a ransomware encryption within milliseconds.
    • RAID and multi-zone storage are not backups. They protect against hardware failure, not against deletion or corruption.
    • Snapshots in the same account are not offsite. Whoever can delete the database can usually delete its snapshots too.
    • "The cloud provider backs it up" is only partly true. Managed services offer backup features, but retention, cross-region copies, restore testing, and protection from your own mistakes are your job.
    • An untested backup is a hope, not a backup. Silent failures (wrong tool version, missing permissions, empty files) are common.
    • Encrypted backups are useless without the key. Back up and protect the keys, separately from the data.
