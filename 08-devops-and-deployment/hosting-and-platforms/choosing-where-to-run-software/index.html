    Choosing Where to Run Software¶

    Every program runs on somebody's computer. The real questions are how much of that computer you rent, how much of it you operate yourself, how you are billed for it, and how hard it would be to leave. This lesson explains the hosting models from virtual machines to static hosting, what you and the provider each manage in every model, how each one bills, the costs that never appear in the headline price, lock-in and portability, and the three families of providers: hyperscalers, developer platforms, and budget providers. It ends with a verdict for common situations and a decision tree.

    The next lessons apply this to frontends, backend APIs, and databases. How to ship a release onto any of these hosts (pipelines, rollouts, health checks) is covered in deployment strategies; the networking pieces (VPCs, subnets, security groups) are in cloud building blocks.

    Why it matters¶

    The hosting model decides four things that are expensive to change later:

    • How much operations work the team owns. A two-person team that picks Kubernetes spends days on cluster upgrades and node patching that a platform would have done for it.
    • The shape of the bill. Per-request pricing is close to free at low traffic and becomes the most expensive option at steady high traffic; instance pricing is the reverse.
    • The failure modes. Functions have cold starts and duration limits; a single VM is a single point of failure; a platform can raise prices, change plans, or stop selling a product.
    • The exit cost. Data, identity, and networking accumulate in one provider, and moving terabytes out is billed per GB.

    None of these choices is permanent, but each one gets harder to reverse every month. Writing down why you chose a host, and at what traffic or cost you would reconsider, turns an accident into a decision.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Netflix (moved into the cloud). In Completing the Netflix Cloud Migration (February 2016), Netflix explains that a major database corruption in 2008 stopped DVD shipping for three days and showed it had to move away from "vertically scaled single points of failure". It chose AWS and finished the migration in January 2016. The stated reasons were elasticity ("thousands of virtual servers and petabytes of storage within minutes") and availability; it says cost reduction "was not the main reason", although its per-stream cloud cost ended up lower than in its data centers.
    • 37signals (moved out of the cloud). In Our cloud-exit savings will now top ten million over five years (October 2024), the makers of Basecamp and HEY report cutting their cloud bill from a $3.2 million per year run rate to $1.3 million after moving their applications onto about $700,000 of their own hardware, with the remaining spend being Amazon S3, which they planned to replace with their own storage. Their reasoning, a large and steady workload that no longer benefits from elasticity, is collected at basecamp.com/cloud-exit.
    • Dropbox (moved storage in-house). The post Scaling to exabytes and beyond (March 2016) describes how Dropbox moved over 90% of user data off Amazon S3 onto its own storage system by October 2015, because owning the whole stack let it "customize the entire stack end-to-end", improve performance, and get "better unit economics" at its scale.
    • Platform risk is real. AWS closed App Runner to new customers (availability change) and recommends Amazon ECS Express Mode instead, and in February 2026 Heroku announced a sustaining engineering model focused on stability rather than new features, with Enterprise contracts no longer offered to new customers. Existing users of both keep running, but the roadmap you bought into changed.

    Popular tools and providers: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Cloudflare, Vercel, Netlify, Render, Railway, Fly.io, Heroku, DigitalOcean, Hetzner, Akamai Cloud (Linode), and colocation or owned hardware.

    How it works¶

    Virtual machines (IaaS)¶

    What it is: a rented virtual server with a fixed amount of vCPU, memory, disk, and network. Examples: Amazon EC2 and Lightsail, Azure Virtual Machines, Google Compute Engine, DigitalOcean Droplets, Hetzner Cloud, and Akamai Cloud (Linode).

    How it works underneath: a hypervisor on a physical host divides the machine into isolated virtual machines. Your VM boots an operating system image, gets a block-storage disk and a network interface, and keeps running (and billing) until you stop it.

    You manage: the OS and its patches, the runtime (Node.js version), the process manager, the reverse proxy, TLS, scaling (adding VMs and a load balancer), backups of anything on the disk, and monitoring. The provider manages: the data center, hardware, hypervisor, and the physical network.

    Typical use: steady workloads, software that needs a full OS, and teams that want maximum control or the lowest price per CPU.

    Containers on an orchestrator (CaaS)¶

    What it is: you package the app as a container image and a scheduler decides which machine runs each copy. Examples: Amazon EKS, Google Kubernetes Engine, Azure Kubernetes Service, and Amazon ECS on EC2.

    How it works underneath: a control plane (managed by the provider on EKS, GKE, and AKS) stores the desired state; worker nodes (VMs you pay for) run the containers; the scheduler restarts failed containers and spreads replicas across nodes and zones.

    You manage: images, manifests, node pools and their upgrades (unless you use a node-less mode such as GKE Autopilot), cluster add-ons, autoscaling rules, and networking policy. The provider manages: the control plane and the hardware.

    Typical use: many services, teams that already know Kubernetes, and workloads that need fine control over networking or scheduling. See the Kubernetes subject for how it works.

    Serverless containers¶

    What it is: you hand the provider a container image plus CPU and memory settings, and it runs as many copies as traffic needs, with no nodes to manage. Examples: AWS Fargate (under ECS or EKS), Google Cloud Run, and Azure Container Apps.

    How it works underneath: each task or instance runs in an isolated sandbox on the provider's fleet. Cloud Run and Container Apps can scale to zero instances and start one when a request arrives; Fargate tasks run until you stop them or a scaling policy removes them.

    You manage: the image (including the runtime and its patches), CPU and memory sizes, minimum and maximum instance counts, and networking choices. The provider manages: servers, OS, scheduling, and (on Cloud Run and Container Apps) the HTTPS endpoint.

    Typical use: stateless HTTP APIs and workers, from small teams that want containers without a cluster to large fleets.

    Platform as a service (PaaS)¶

    What it is: you push code (or an image), and the platform builds it, runs it, routes traffic to it, and gives you logs, TLS, and scaling. Examples: Heroku, Render, Railway, Fly.io, Azure App Service, Google App Engine, AWS Elastic Beanstalk, and DigitalOcean App Platform.

    How it works underneath: a build step turns your repository into an image (with buildpacks or your Dockerfile); the platform runs it on its own fleet behind its router. Fly.io runs each app instance as a lightweight VM it calls a Machine; Elastic Beanstalk is different in that it creates EC2 instances and a load balancer inside your own AWS account.

    You manage: code, configuration, instance size and count. The provider manages: almost everything else.

    Typical use: early startups, internal tools, and teams with no dedicated operations people.

    Functions (FaaS)¶

    What it is: you write a handler function; the provider runs it per event (an HTTP request, a queue message, a file upload) and bills per invocation and duration. Examples: AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, Cloud Run functions (formerly Cloud Functions), Cloudflare Workers, and Vercel Functions.

    How it works underneath: on AWS Lambda each execution environment is a lightweight microVM built on Firecracker. A classic Lambda environment handles one request at a time, so concurrency comes from starting more environments. When no warm environment is free, Lambda creates one, downloads your code, and runs your initialization code first: that delay is a cold start. Warm environments are reused for later requests until Lambda reclaims them.

    You manage: handler code, memory size, timeouts, and concurrency limits. The provider manages: servers, runtime patching, and scaling.

    Typical use: event processing, spiky APIs, scheduled jobs, and glue between services.

    Edge compute¶

    What it is: small functions that run in a CDN's points of presence, close to users. Examples: Cloudflare Workers, CloudFront Functions, and Lambda@Edge.

    How it works underneath: Cloudflare Workers run in V8 isolates (the sandbox technology of the Chrome JavaScript engine) inside every Cloudflare location, which start in milliseconds instead of booting a VM or container; CloudFront Functions run lightweight JavaScript on every request at CloudFront edge locations.

    You manage: small handlers with tight CPU and memory limits. The provider manages: global placement and scaling.

    Typical use: redirects, authentication checks, A/B routing, header rewrites, and APIs that read from globally replicated data.

    Static hosting with a CDN¶

    What it is: the host serves pre-built files (HTML, JavaScript, CSS, images) from a CDN. Examples: Amazon S3 with CloudFront, Cloudflare, Vercel, Netlify, GitHub Pages, Firebase Hosting, and Azure Static Web Apps.

    How it works underneath: your build output is uploaded once, copied or cached at edge locations worldwide, and served without running any of your code per request.

    You manage: the build output, cache headers, and redirects. The provider manages: everything else.

    Typical use: marketing sites, documentation, and single-page apps such as TaskFlow Web. The frontends lesson compares these hosts in detail.

    Who manages what¶

    The AWS shared responsibility model summarizes the split as the provider being responsible for security "of" the cloud and the customer for security "in" the cloud. Where the line falls depends on the model:

    Layer Own servers VMs Kubernetes Serverless containers PaaS Functions Static
    Hardware, data center You Provider Provider Provider Provider Provider Provider
    Host OS, hypervisor You Provider Provider Provider Provider Provider Provider
    Guest OS and patches You You Nodes: you (or Autopilot) Provider Provider Provider Provider
    Language runtime You You You (in the image) You (in the image) Provider (buildpack) Provider n/a
    Scaling and capacity You You You configure You set limits You pick size and count Provider Provider
    Application code You You You You You You Your files
    Data, identities, access You You You You You You You

    The last row never moves. A public S3 bucket, a leaked API key, or a database open to the internet is the customer's problem on every model.

    Prices also differ by region. Fly.io, for example, charges $0.02 per GB of outbound data in North America and Europe but $0.12 per GB in Africa and India (Fly.io pricing), while Google lists Mumbai (asia-south1) in the same Tier 1 price group as us-central1 for Cloud Run. To check a region such as India, pick it in the region selector of the pricing page or the calculator.

    Free tiers change as well. New AWS accounts now receive up to $200 in credits and choose between a free plan, which closes after six months or when the credits run out, and a paid plan; more than 30 services keep always-free monthly allowances on both (AWS Free Tier). Developer platforms revise their free plans often, so read the current terms before relying on one.

    The costs that are not in the headline price¶

    A pricing page shows the main meter. A real bill has several more. On AWS (us-east-1) the usual surprises are:

    Item List price Why it surprises people
    Data transfer out to the internet $0.09 per GB for the first 10 TB, after 100 GB per month free across AWS Every API response, download, and image leaves through this meter
    NAT gateway $0.045 per hour (about $32.85 per month) plus $0.045 per GB processed Private subnets need one per Availability Zone to reach the internet, ECR, or third-party APIs
    Data between Availability Zones $0.01 per GB in each direction Multi-AZ designs send app-to-database and replication traffic across zones
    Application Load Balancer $0.0225 per hour (about $16.43 per month) plus $0.008 per LCU-hour Billed even when traffic is zero; one per environment adds up
    Public IPv4 address $0.005 per hour (about $3.65 per month) per address, in use or idle Every public load balancer node, NAT gateway, and instance address counts
    CloudWatch Logs $0.50 per GB ingested, $0.03 per GB-month stored Verbose request logging can cost more than the servers
    EKS cluster $0.10 per hour; $0.50 per hour on extended support An old Kubernetes version quietly multiplies the control-plane fee by five
    Idle and forgotten resources gp3 volumes $0.08 per GB-month; databases billed per hour Stopped instances still pay for disks; dev databases run all weekend

    Sources: EC2 data transfer, VPC pricing, EBS pricing, Elastic Load Balancing pricing, CloudWatch pricing, and the AWS Price List Bulk API.

    Compute is about a fifth of the bill. The fixes are design choices, not discounts: S3 and DynamoDB gateway endpoints carry that traffic without a NAT gateway and without a charge, other AWS services can be reached through interface endpoints (which have their own hourly fee), a single NAT gateway halves that line at the cost of a zone dependency, and logging only errors and sampled requests cuts the log line. The cost optimization lesson covers these in depth.

    Lock-in and portability¶

    Lock-in is the cost of leaving: the code you would rewrite, the data you would move, and the people you would retrain. Some of it is worth paying for, but it should be chosen, not accidental.

    Layer Portable choice Provider-specific choice Cost of leaving the specific choice
    Packaging OCI container image Lambda handler signature, Workers APIs Rewrite entry points and local tooling
    Relational data PostgreSQL or MySQL (RDS, Cloud SQL, Neon, self-hosted) Aurora-only features Usually small: a dump and restore
    Document data MongoDB protocol (Atlas, self-hosted) DynamoDB, Firestore, Cosmos DB native APIs Rewrite the data-access layer and its access patterns
    Infrastructure definition Terraform or OpenTofu CloudFormation, Bicep Re-express resources; the concepts carry over
    Identity and permissions OIDC between systems IAM roles and policies Always provider-specific; rebuild per provider
    Data volume Small datasets Hundreds of TB in one provider Egress fees and transfer time

    Moving 50 TB out of AWS over the internet at list price costs roughly $4,400 (10 TB at $0.09 and 40 TB at $0.085 per GB). AWS says customers who move all their data off AWS can ask AWS Support for free data transfer out for that move (AWS News Blog), but you still pay in time and engineering effort.

    Three families of providers¶

    Family Examples Strengths Weaknesses Pick it when
    Hyperscalers AWS, Azure, Google Cloud Every service you could need, many regions, compliance programs and BAAs, commitment discounts, enterprise support Complexity, many metered extras, egress fees, IAM and networking learning curve You need breadth, compliance, specific regions, or long-term scale
    Developer platforms Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare, Render, Railway, Fly.io, Heroku Git-push deploys, preview environments, good defaults, almost no operations Price cliffs at plan limits, fewer regions and controls, platform and roadmap risk You want to ship fast with a small team
    Budget and infrastructure providers Hetzner, DigitalOcean, Akamai Cloud (Linode) Low prices per vCPU, generous included bandwidth, simple pricing Fewer managed services, fewer regions, compliance and support vary Steady workloads, cost-sensitive teams that can run servers

    These families mix well: a common and sensible setup is a static frontend on a developer platform, an API on a hyperscaler or budget VM, and a managed database from a specialist such as MongoDB Atlas.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Use a more managed option (platform, serverless containers, functions) when the team is small, traffic is uncertain or spiky, or speed of delivery matters most. Move toward more control (containers on your own cluster, VMs, owned hardware) when traffic is large and steady, when you need something the platform cannot do (long-running connections, special hardware, unusual networking), or when the platform's markup has become a large share of the budget.

    Do not choose Kubernetes because large companies use it, functions because they are fashionable, or owned hardware because of one blog post. Choose the cheapest option, counting people's time, that meets the hard constraints.

    Which to choose¶

    Situation Pick Runner-up Why
    Side project or portfolio Static frontend on Cloudflare or GitHub Pages; API on Cloud Run (free tier, scales to zero) A small VPS (DigitalOcean from $4 a month, Hetzner from €5.49) Costs nothing while idle; a VPS teaches Linux but bills every hour
    Early startup A developer platform (Render, Railway, Fly.io) or Cloud Run, plus a managed database; frontend on Vercel or Netlify ECS Express Mode on AWS (Fargate and a load balancer created for you) Fastest path to production with no operations hire; move when the bill or a constraint says so
    Steady high traffic Containers or VMs on committed capacity (ECS or EKS on EC2 with Savings Plans, GKE with committed use discounts) Owned or colocated hardware at very large, very steady scale Per-request pricing costs most at constant load; AWS Compute Savings Plans cut compute prices by up to 66% (AWS)
    Spiky or unpredictable traffic Functions (Lambda) or scale-to-zero containers (Cloud Run, Container Apps) Autoscaled containers with a small warm minimum You pay for the spike, not for idle hours; plan for cold starts and database connection limits
    Global audience CDN and edge for static and cacheable content; API in one or two regions near most users Multi-region instances on Fly.io or Cloud Run Most latency is distance; data still has a home region, so move reads to the edge first
    Compliance-heavy (for example healthcare) A hyperscaler under its BAA, using only HIPAA-eligible services: AWS, Google Cloud, Azure Platforms that sign BAAs: Fly.io, Vercel (a paid add-on on Pro, on request for Enterprise), Render (HIPAA workspaces on its Scale plan) You need the contract, audit logs, encryption, and access controls; a BAA alone does not make your app compliant
    Large files or heavy egress Object storage with low or no egress fees plus a CDN; compute on providers with large included transfer (Hetzner, Lightsail, DigitalOcean) CloudFront flat-rate plans, which include 50 TB of transfer from $15 per month per distribution At $0.09 per GB, egress dominates; see object and file storage
    Team with no ops people A fully managed platform (Render, Railway, Heroku-style) or Cloud Run with a managed database ECS Express Mode Nobody has to patch servers or run clusters; avoid Kubernetes until someone owns it

    Practical examples¶

    A cost model you can rerun¶

    Prices change and traffic grows, so keep the comparison as a small script next to the code and rerun it when either changes:

    // scripts/hosting-cost.mjs: compare monthly cost of four hosting models
// Prices: US list prices checked November 2026 (see the linked pages)
const PRICE = {
  lambdaPerRequest: 0.20 / 1e6,        // aws.amazon.com/lambda/pricing
  lambdaPerGbSecond: 0.0000166667,
  fargateVcpuHour: 0.04048,            // aws.amazon.com/fargate/pricing
  fargateGbHour: 0.004445,
  albHour: 0.0225,                     // aws.amazon.com/elasticloadbalancing/pricing
  albLcuHour: 0.008,
  ec2T4gSmallHour: 0.0168,             // aws.amazon.com/ec2/pricing/on-demand
  gp3GbMonth: 0.08,
  ipv4Hour: 0.005,                     // aws.amazon.com/vpc/pricing
  cloudRunVcpuSecond: 0.000024,        // cloud.google.com/run/pricing
  cloudRunGibSecond: 0.0000025,
  cloudRunPerRequest: 0.40 / 1e6,
};
const HOURS = 730;

export function monthlyCost({ requests, durationMs, memoryGb }) {
  const seconds = durationMs / 1000;

  // Functions: pay per request and per GB-second (ignoring the free tier)
  const lambda =
    requests * PRICE.lambdaPerRequest +
    requests * seconds * memoryGb * PRICE.lambdaPerGbSecond;

  // Scale-to-zero container, 1 vCPU, concurrency 1 (worst case): billable
  // time is rounded up to 100 ms, so an isolated 120 ms request bills 200 ms
  const billedSeconds = Math.ceil(durationMs / 100) / 10;
  const cloudRun =
    requests * billedSeconds * PRICE.cloudRunVcpuSecond +
    requests * billedSeconds * memoryGb * PRICE.cloudRunGibSecond +
    requests * PRICE.cloudRunPerRequest;

  // Two always-on Fargate tasks (0.5 vCPU, 1 GB) behind an ALB at ~1 LCU
  const fargate =
    2 * (0.5 * PRICE.fargateVcpuHour + 1 * PRICE.fargateGbHour) * HOURS +
    PRICE.albHour * HOURS + PRICE.albLcuHour * HOURS;

  // One small VM with a 20 GB disk and a public IPv4 address (no redundancy)
  const vm =
    PRICE.ec2T4gSmallHour * HOURS + 20 * PRICE.gp3GbMonth + PRICE.ipv4Hour * HOURS;

  return { lambda, cloudRun, fargate, vm };
}

const round = (n) => Math.round(n * 100) / 100;
for (const millions of [1, 2, 10, 50, 100]) {
  const cost = monthlyCost({ requests: millions * 1e6, durationMs: 120, memoryGb: 0.5 });
  console.log(`${millions}M requests/month`, Object.fromEntries(
    Object.entries(cost).map(([k, v]) => [k, `$${round(v)}`]),
  ));
}

    $ node scripts/hosting-cost.mjs
1M requests/month { lambda: '$1.2', cloudRun: '$5.45', fargate: '$58.31', vm: '$17.51' }
2M requests/month { lambda: '$2.4', cloudRun: '$10.9', fargate: '$58.31', vm: '$17.51' }
10M requests/month { lambda: '$12', cloudRun: '$54.5', fargate: '$58.31', vm: '$17.51' }
50M requests/month { lambda: '$60', cloudRun: '$272.5', fargate: '$58.31', vm: '$17.51' }
100M requests/month { lambda: '$120', cloudRun: '$545', fargate: '$58.31', vm: '$17.51' }

    The model is deliberately simple: it ignores free tiers, assumes the fixed-price options have enough capacity, and treats Cloud Run as if no two requests ever overlapped on an instance, which overstates its cost for I/O-bound Node.js code that serves many requests per instance. The backend lesson refines it and plots the crossover points.

    One image, three hosts¶

    A container image is the most portable unit you can build. The same TaskFlow API image can run on three very different hosting models:

    # 1. AWS: ECS Express Mode creates a Fargate service, an ALB, scaling, and networking
aws ecs create-express-gateway-service \
  --service-name taskflow-api \
  --execution-role-arn arn:aws:iam::<account-id>:role/ecsTaskExecutionRole \
  --infrastructure-role-arn arn:aws:iam::<account-id>:role/ecsInfrastructureRoleForExpressServices \
  --primary-container '{
      "image": "<account-id>.dkr.ecr.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/taskflow-api:1.4.0",
      "containerPort": 3000
    }' \
  --health-check-path "/healthz" \
  --scaling-target '{"minTaskCount":2,"maxTaskCount":6}'

# 2. Google Cloud: a scale-to-zero serverless container
gcloud run deploy taskflow-api \
  --image=us-docker.pkg.dev/example-project/apps/taskflow-api:1.4.0 \
  --region=us-central1 --port=3000 --min-instances=0 --max-instances=10

# 3. A plain VM: Docker restarts the container if it or the VM restarts
docker run -d --name taskflow-api --restart=unless-stopped \
  -p 127.0.0.1:3000:3000 --env-file /etc/taskflow/api.env \
  ghcr.io/example/taskflow-api:1.4.0

    The image did not change; only the surrounding responsibilities did. On the VM you also own nginx, TLS, patching, and a second VM for redundancy, which the backend deployment lesson walks through.

    Find the hidden costs on an AWS account¶

    These read-only AWS CLI queries list the usual hidden-cost items so you can review them each month:

    #!/usr/bin/env bash
# scripts/hidden-costs.sh: read-only checks for common hidden AWS costs
set -euo pipefail
export AWS_PAGER=""

echo "== Unattached EBS volumes (billed per GB-month while unused)"
aws ec2 describe-volumes --filters Name=status,Values=available \
  --query 'Volumes[].{id:VolumeId,gb:Size,created:CreateTime}' --output table

echo "== Elastic IPs not associated with anything (billed per hour)"
aws ec2 describe-addresses \
  --query 'Addresses[?AssociationId==null].PublicIp' --output text

echo "== Network interfaces holding a public IPv4 address"
aws ec2 describe-network-interfaces \
  --query 'NetworkInterfaces[?Association.PublicIp].{ip:Association.PublicIp,type:InterfaceType,desc:Description}' \
  --output table

echo "== NAT gateways (hourly charge each, plus per-GB processing)"
aws ec2 describe-nat-gateways --filter Name=state,Values=available \
  --query 'NatGateways[].{id:NatGatewayId,vpc:VpcId,subnet:SubnetId}' --output table

echo "== Log groups with no retention (stored forever)"
aws logs describe-log-groups \
  --query 'logGroups[?!retentionInDays].[logGroupName,storedBytes]' --output table

echo "== Last month's cost by usage type"
aws ce get-cost-and-usage \
  --time-period Start=2026-10-01,End=2026-11-01 \
  --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost \
  --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=USAGE_TYPE \
  --query 'ResultsByTime[0].Groups[].[Keys[0],Metrics.UnblendedCost.Amount]' --output text \
  | sort -k2 -n -r | head -20

    Usage types containing NatGateway-Hours, DataTransfer-Out-Bytes, PublicIPv4:InUseAddress, and DataProcessing-Bytes (log ingestion), often with a region prefix such as USE1-, point straight at the lines in the table above. Run the script per region (AWS_REGION=eu-west-1 ./scripts/hidden-costs.sh), because most of these resources are regional.

    Best practices¶

    • Choose per workload: static files, APIs, background jobs, and databases often belong on different hosting models.
    • Start with the most managed option that meets the hard constraints, and write down the traffic or cost at which you would revisit it.
    • Price every option with the full set of meters (egress, NAT, load balancer, IPv4, logs, cross-zone traffic), not just compute.
    • Keep the application portable where it is cheap: a container image, configuration in environment variables, standard database protocols, and infrastructure as code.
    • Put budgets and anomaly alerts on every account and project from the first day.
    • Tag or label every resource with its service, environment, and owner so the bill can be split and orphaned resources found.
    • Read the free tier and plan limits: whether it sleeps, expires, pauses, or forbids commercial use.
    • Track each provider's status pages and announcements for products you depend on; closures and pricing changes come with notice.

    In production¶

    • The bill doubled after moving tasks into private subnets. Detect: Cost Explorer shows NatGateway-Hours and NatGateway-Bytes as new top usage types. Cause: each private subnet's zone got a NAT gateway, and image pulls and API calls now go through it. Fix: add S3 and DynamoDB gateway endpoints, use interface endpoints for ECR and CloudWatch where they cost less than the NAT traffic, and decide deliberately between one NAT gateway per zone and one shared gateway.
    • Small new charges for "public IPv4" on every service. Detect: PublicIPv4:InUseAddress usage in the bill, roughly $3.65 per address per month. Cause: AWS bills every public IPv4 address, including load balancer nodes and instances that get one automatically. Fix: disable auto-assigned public IPs, put instances behind a load balancer, and use IPv6 where clients support it.
    • The Kubernetes control-plane fee jumped five times. Detect: EKS usage type extendedSupport at $0.50 per hour instead of $0.10. Cause: the cluster runs a Kubernetes version past standard support. Fix: schedule regular version upgrades; treat the extended support fee as an alert, not a plan.
    • The first request after a quiet period takes many seconds. Detect: latency spikes only on the first request after idle time; platform logs show the service starting. Cause: a free or scale-to-zero tier: Render spins down a Free web service after 15 minutes without traffic, Heroku Eco dynos sleep after 30 minutes, and Cloud Run with zero minimum instances starts one on demand. Fix: set a minimum of one instance, move to a paid always-on plan, or accept the delay for internal tools.
    • A site went offline at the end of the month. Detect: the platform dashboard shows the project paused for usage. Cause: a hard plan limit, such as Netlify's Free plan credit limit or Vercel Hobby limits, which pause the feature until the next period. Fix: upgrade before launch, set usage alerts, and use spend limits on paid plans instead of hard stops for production.
    • The provider stopped selling the service you depend on. Detect: an availability notice in the documentation, such as App Runner being closed to new customers. Cause: a product lifecycle change. Fix: follow the named replacement (ECS Express Mode for App Runner), keep deployments in infrastructure as code so a migration is a project, not a rewrite, and keep the app containerized.
    • Egress costs spike overnight. Detect: DataTransfer-Out-Bytes jumps; CDN or load balancer logs show one referrer or crawler downloading large files repeatedly. Cause: hotlinking, a scraper, or an uncached large download. Fix: serve files through a CDN with caching, add rate limits and bot rules, and use signed URLs for large private files.

    Important terminology¶

    • IaaS (infrastructure as a service): renting virtual machines, disks, and networks that you configure and operate.
    • CaaS (containers as a service): a managed service that runs container images, with or without visible nodes.
    • PaaS (platform as a service): a platform that builds and runs your code and handles routing, TLS, and scaling.
    • FaaS (functions as a service): running handler functions per event, billed per invocation and duration.
    • Edge compute: code that runs in a CDN's locations near users.
    • Shared responsibility model: the split of security and operations duties between provider and customer, which changes by hosting model.
    • Cold start: the delay when a platform must create a new execution environment or instance before handling a request.
    • Egress: data sent out of a provider's network, usually billed per GB.
    • NAT gateway: a managed service that lets resources in private subnets reach the internet; billed per hour and per GB.
    • Lock-in: the cost, in rewriting, retraining, and data transfer, of moving to another provider.
    • Hyperscaler: one of the largest general-purpose clouds (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud).
    • Committed use discount: a lower price in exchange for committing to a level of spend or usage for one or three years.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Comparing only compute prices and ignoring egress, NAT gateways, load balancers, IPv4 addresses, and logs.
    • Choosing Kubernetes before there is anyone to own the cluster.
    • Assuming serverless is always cheaper; at steady high traffic it usually is not.
    • Assuming the cloud is always more expensive than owned hardware without counting people, data center contracts, and spare capacity.
    • Launching a commercial product on a free tier that sleeps, expires, pauses, or forbids commercial use (GitHub Pages and Vercel Hobby both restrict commercial use).
    • Treating "managed" as "no responsibility": data, access control, and configuration mistakes are always yours.
    • Leaving development environments, snapshots, and test databases running indefinitely.
    • Building deep lock-in by accident instead of choosing where it pays off.
