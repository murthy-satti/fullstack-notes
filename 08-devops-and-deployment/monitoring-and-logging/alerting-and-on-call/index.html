    Alerting and On-Call¶

    An alert is a decision, made in advance, that a condition deserves a human's attention. Paging someone at 3 a.m. is the most expensive thing a monitoring system can do, so every alert has to earn it. This lesson covers alerting on symptoms rather than causes, the difference between a page and a ticket, severity levels, how to keep alerts actionable and avoid alert fatigue, how Alertmanager groups, deduplicates, inhibits, and routes alerts, why every alert needs a runbook, and how to run a humane on-call rotation: handoffs, escalation policies, follow-the-sun, and the metrics that show whether on-call is healthy.

    Why it matters¶

    Bad alerting fails in two directions. Too few alerts and users discover outages before you do. Too many and engineers learn to ignore them: when nine pages out of ten need no action, the tenth (the real one) is acknowledged and snoozed like the rest. Alert fatigue is a major reliability risk, and it also burns people out and makes on-call something everyone avoids.

    Good alerting is small and precise. A team running TaskFlow API should be paged only when users are being hurt, or are about to be, and something can be done about it now. Everything else becomes a ticket, a dashboard, or automation. Combined with a fair rotation and good runbooks, that makes on-call sustainable.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Google SRE (philosophy of paging). The chapter Monitoring Distributed Systems says pages should be urgent, actionable, and require human intelligence, and that you should page on symptoms rather than causes; anything that can be handled by a robot should not page a human.
    • Google SRE (being on-call). The chapter Being On-Call describes primary and secondary rotations, caps the share of time an engineer spends on-call, and sets a target of at most two incidents per 12-hour shift so each incident gets proper follow-up.
    • Prometheus. The project's alerting best practices recommend keeping alerting simple, alerting on symptoms associated with end-user pain, alerting on latency and errors as high in the stack as possible, and having consoles that let you find the cause.
    • PagerDuty Incident Response. PagerDuty's public incident response documentation, including Being On-Call, describes on-call responsibilities, acknowledgement expectations, handoffs, and escalation in a form many companies adopt.

    Popular tools and providers: Prometheus Alertmanager, Grafana Alerting, PagerDuty, Jira Service Management, incident.io, Splunk On-Call, and cloud-native alarms such as Amazon CloudWatch alarms with SNS.

    How it works¶

    Alert on symptoms, not causes¶

    A symptom is something a user experiences: errors, slowness, missing data. A cause is a reason it might happen: high CPU, a full disk, a restarted pod. Causes are many, change with the architecture, and often do not hurt anyone. Symptoms are few and always matter.

    Cause-based alert (avoid paging) Symptom-based alert (page)
    CPU above 90% on an API pod Error budget burning 14x too fast
    One of six pods restarted p99 latency above SLO for the create-task journey
    MongoDB replica lag above 10 s Synthetic "create and read a task" check failing from two regions
    Queue depth above 1,000 Reminder emails more than 15 minutes late (freshness SLI)
    Memory above 80% Public TLS certificate expires in under 7 days with renewal failing

    Cause-based signals are still valuable: they belong on dashboards, in tickets (a disk that will fill in five days), and in the runbook as diagnosis steps. The exception is a cause that predicts imminent, unavoidable user pain with no slack, such as a disk that will be full in two hours; that can page.

    Pages, tickets, and dashboards¶

    The SRE book describes three kinds of monitoring output:

    Output Meaning Response time TaskFlow examples
    Page A human must act now Minutes, any hour Fast error budget burn, site down from outside, data loss risk
    Ticket A human must act, but not now Hours to days, working hours Slow budget burn, certificate expiring in 20 days, disk full in 5 days, backup job failed once
    Dashboard or log Nobody needs to act; useful for diagnosis Never, unless investigating CPU, memory, individual pod restarts, cache hit ratio

    Most alerts people create should be tickets or dashboard panels. A practical rule: if the right response is "look at it tomorrow", it must not page tonight.

    Severity levels¶

    Severity labels on alerts drive routing. Keep the scheme small and written down:

    Severity Definition Routing
    page (critical) User-facing SLO at risk now; needs action within minutes Pager service, primary on-call, escalation
    ticket (warning) Will become user-facing if ignored for days Ticket queue for the owning team
    info Context only Chat channel or dashboard, no notification

    Do not invent more levels than you route differently; five severities that all go to the same channel are one severity.

    Actionable alerts and alert fatigue¶

    Every paging alert should pass four tests:

    1. Urgent: waiting until morning would hurt users or the business.
    2. Actionable: the responder can do something (mitigate, roll back, fail over, scale), and the runbook says what.
    3. Requires judgement: if the fix is a fixed script, automate it instead of paging.
    4. Novel: it is not a duplicate of another page for the same problem.

    Alert fatigue builds up from small exceptions: a flapping alert nobody fixed, a threshold set on a quiet week, a duplicate per pod. Counter it with process:

    • Review every page in a weekly on-call review: was it actionable? If not, fix, downgrade, or delete the alert that week.
    • Track the actionable rate: the share of pages that led to a real action. A healthy target is well above half; anything near zero means the alert is noise.
    • Use for: to require a condition to persist (for example 5 minutes) for threshold alerts, so momentary spikes do not page. SLO burn-rate alerts already have this built into their windows.
    • Delete alerts nobody would miss. An alert that has not fired in a year and has no clear owner is not protection, it is clutter.

    The alerting pipeline¶

    Alerting rules live in Prometheus (or Grafana, or a cloud provider). Each rule has an expression, an optional for duration (the condition must hold that long; until then the alert is pending), optional keep_firing_for (keeps it firing briefly after the condition clears, to avoid flapping), labels such as severity, and annotations such as summary and runbook_url.

    Alertmanager receives firing alerts from one or more Prometheus servers and decides who hears about them:

    • Deduplication: run two Prometheus servers for redundancy and both send the same alert; Alertmanager notifies once. Run Alertmanager itself as a small cluster (two or three instances) that shares state.
    • Grouping: group_by: [alertname, service] turns 40 pod-level alerts into one notification. group_wait (default 30 s) waits to collect the first batch, group_interval (default 5 m) controls updates to an existing group, and repeat_interval (default 4 h) re-sends while it is still firing.
    • Inhibition: suppress some alerts while others fire. If "TaskFlow API down" is paging, the latency and error-budget alerts for the same service add nothing.
    • Silences: temporary, time-limited mutes created by people (in the UI or with amtool) during maintenance or while an incident is already being worked. Every silence should have an author, a comment, and an expiry.
    • Routing: a tree of matchers on labels sends each group to a receiver (pager, ticket webhook, chat, email).

    Runbook links in every alert¶

    A page at 3 a.m. should never leave the responder wondering what it means. Every alert carries a runbook_url annotation pointing to a page that answers:

    1. What this alert means, in one sentence, and what users experience.
    2. Impact and urgency: which journeys and how badly.
    3. Dashboards and queries to open first (linked, with the right variables).
    4. Diagnosis steps: the likely causes, how to tell them apart.
    5. Mitigation: the safe actions, such as rolling back the last deploy, scaling out, failing over, disabling a feature flag, in order of preference.
    6. Escalation: who to call when the runbook does not work.

    Runbooks are reviewed after every incident that used them, and an alert without a runbook is not allowed to page.

    On-call rotations and handoffs¶

    A rotation is the schedule of who responds:

    • Primary and secondary: the primary responds to pages; the secondary backs up the primary (escalation) and takes over if the primary is overloaded or unreachable.
    • Shift length: commonly one week, handing over on a weekday morning so the handoff happens during work hours.
    • Team size: with fewer than about six to eight people in a rotation, each person is on-call too often; small teams share a rotation with a neighbouring team or buy time with better automation.
    • Handoffs: a short written note (or meeting) at every change of shift: open incidents, active silences and why, noisy alerts, risky changes scheduled, anything half-finished.
    • Compensation and recovery: time off after a night of pages, and pay or time in lieu for being on-call, are part of a sustainable rotation.

    Escalation policies¶

    An escalation policy says what happens when a page is not acknowledged: page the primary; if no acknowledgement in 5 minutes, page the secondary; after 15 minutes, page the engineering manager. It ensures that a dead phone battery or a missed vibration does not turn a 10-minute incident into a 2-hour one. Acknowledgement only means "I am on it"; the responder then works the problem or declares an incident (see incident response and postmortems).

    Follow-the-sun¶

    Teams spread across time zones can split the day so nobody is paged at night: for example, a team in Europe covers 08:00 to 16:00 UTC, one in the Americas 16:00 to 24:00, and one in Asia-Pacific 00:00 to 08:00. This removes night pages but needs at least two or three sites with real expertise in the service, consistent runbooks, and excellent handoffs at each boundary.

    On-call health metrics¶

    Measure on-call like any other system:

    Metric What it tells you Healthy signal
    Pages per shift Load on the responder Low single digits per week; at most about two incidents per 12-hour shift
    Pages outside working hours Sleep disruption Trending down
    Actionable rate Alert quality Most pages led to action
    Time to acknowledge (MTTA) Responsiveness and paging reliability A few minutes
    Time to mitigate Effectiveness of runbooks and tooling Trending down
    Top alerts by count Where to invest The top three are fixed or deleted each quarter
    Repeat incidents Whether follow-ups get done Rare

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Condition Action
    SLO burn rate fast (14.4x or 6x) Page
    Service unreachable from external probes in two or more regions Page
    SLO burn rate slow (1x over days) Ticket
    Certificate expires in under 21 days Ticket; page only if under 7 days and renewal is failing
    Disk will be full within 4 hours at current rate Page (imminent, no slack)
    Disk will be full within 5 days Ticket
    One pod restarting, others healthy and SLO fine Dashboard; ticket if it persists
    CPU or memory high, SLO fine Dashboard
    Backup job failed once Ticket; page if no successful backup for over 26 hours on a critical database
    Prometheus or Alertmanager itself is down Page through an external watchdog

    Practical examples¶

    Prometheus alerting rules¶

    # prometheus/rules/taskflow-alerts.yml
groups:
  - name: taskflow-availability
    rules:
      - alert: TaskflowApiDown
        expr: sum(up{job="taskflow-api"}) == 0 or absent(up{job="taskflow-api"})   # absent(): no targets discovered at all
        for: 2m
        labels:
          severity: page
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "No TaskFlow API instance can be scraped"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/api-down"

      - alert: TaskflowSyntheticCheckFailing
        # Blackbox exporter probes from several regions; page only when two or more fail.
        expr: count(probe_success{job="blackbox-taskflow"} == 0) >= 2
        for: 3m
        labels:
          severity: page
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "External probes failing from {{ $value }} regions"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/synthetic-failing"

  - name: taskflow-capacity
    rules:
      - alert: TaskflowDbDiskFullSoon
        expr: predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes{job="node", mountpoint="/var/lib/mongodb"}[6h], 5 * 86400) < 0
        for: 30m
        labels:
          severity: ticket
          service: taskflow-db
        annotations:
          summary: "MongoDB data disk on {{ $labels.instance }} will be full within 5 days"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-db/disk-full"

      - alert: TaskflowCertificateExpiringSoon
        expr: (probe_ssl_earliest_cert_expiry{job="blackbox-taskflow"} - time()) / 86400 < 21
        for: 1h
        labels:
          severity: ticket
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "TLS certificate for {{ $labels.instance }} expires in {{ $value | humanize }} days"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/certificate-expiry"

  - name: meta
    rules:
      - alert: Watchdog
        # Always firing. An external service pages if it STOPS receiving this.
        expr: vector(1)
        labels:
          severity: none
        annotations:
          summary: "Alerting pipeline heartbeat"

    The SLO burn-rate pages from SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets are the main symptom alerts; these rules cover total outages, outside-in checks, capacity tickets, and the pipeline itself. predict_linear extrapolates the last 6 hours of free space 5 days ahead. The Watchdog alert is a dead man's switch: it proves the whole path from Prometheus to the receiver works, and its absence is what pages.

    Alertmanager routing, grouping, and inhibition¶

    # alertmanager/alertmanager.yml
global:
  resolve_timeout: 5m

route:
  receiver: team-chat            # default: anything not matched below
  group_by: [alertname, service]
  group_wait: 30s
  group_interval: 5m
  repeat_interval: 4h
  routes:
    - matchers: ['alertname="Watchdog"']
      receiver: heartbeat
      group_interval: 1m           # repeats are only sent at group_interval ticks
      repeat_interval: 1m
    - matchers: ['severity="page"']
      receiver: taskflow-pager
      repeat_interval: 1h
    - matchers: ['severity="ticket"']
      receiver: taskflow-tickets
      repeat_interval: 24h

inhibit_rules:
  # While the API is completely down, its other alerts add nothing.
  - source_matchers: ['alertname="TaskflowApiDown"']
    target_matchers: ['alertname!="TaskflowApiDown"', 'service="taskflow-api"']
    equal: [service]
  # A page for a service hides that service's tickets.
  - source_matchers: ['severity="page"']
    target_matchers: ['severity="ticket"']
    equal: [service]

receivers:
  - name: taskflow-pager
    pagerduty_configs:
      - routing_key_file: /etc/alertmanager/secrets/pagerduty-routing-key
        severity: critical
  - name: taskflow-tickets
    webhook_configs:
      - url: https://tickets.example.com/hooks/alertmanager
  - name: team-chat
    slack_configs:
      - api_url_file: /etc/alertmanager/secrets/slack-webhook-url
        channel: "#taskflow-alerts"
        send_resolved: true
  - name: heartbeat
    webhook_configs:
      - url: https://heartbeat.example.com/ping/<check-id>

    Parts: the route tree matches top to bottom and stops at the first matching child; group_by keeps one notification per alert name and service; the inhibit rules mute duplicates while a bigger alert is firing; secrets (the PagerDuty routing key and chat webhook URL) are read from files mounted from a secret store, not written in the config; the heartbeat receiver pings an external dead man's switch service every minute. Alertmanager rounds repeat_interval up to a multiple of group_interval, so the heartbeat route lowers both; with the inherited 5-minute group_interval it would ping only every 5 minutes.

    Validating and testing alerts¶

    # Syntax and semantic checks, run in CI on every change
promtool check rules prometheus/rules/*.yml
amtool check-config alertmanager/alertmanager.yml

# Unit tests for alert rules
promtool test rules prometheus/tests/taskflow-alerts.test.yml

# Show which receiver an alert with these labels would reach
amtool config routes test --config.file=alertmanager/alertmanager.yml severity=page service=taskflow-api

# Silence an alert during planned maintenance, with an expiry and a reason
amtool silence add alertname=TaskflowDbDiskFullSoon service=taskflow-db \
  --duration=2h --comment="Disk resize in progress (change 4821)" \
  --alertmanager.url=http://alertmanager:9093

    # prometheus/tests/taskflow-alerts.test.yml
rule_files:
  - ../rules/taskflow-alerts.yml

evaluation_interval: 1m

tests:
  - interval: 1m
    input_series:
      - series: 'up{job="taskflow-api", instance="api-1:9464"}'
        values: "1 1 0 0 0 0 0"
      - series: 'up{job="taskflow-api", instance="api-2:9464"}'
        values: "1 1 0 0 0 0 0"
    alert_rule_test:
      - eval_time: 3m
        alertname: TaskflowApiDown
        exp_alerts: []          # down for 1 minute: still pending
      - eval_time: 5m
        alertname: TaskflowApiDown
        exp_alerts:
          - exp_labels:
              severity: page
              service: taskflow-api
            exp_annotations:
              summary: "No TaskFlow API instance can be scraped"
              runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/api-down"

    Testing alerts in CI catches typos in label names (which silently route a page to the wrong place) and checks that for durations behave as intended.

    A handoff note template¶

    On-call handoff: taskflow, week 46 (primary: <name>, secondary: <name>)

Open incidents:        none / INC-123 (status, next step, owner)
Active silences:       TaskflowDbDiskFullSoon until Thu 14:00 (disk resize, change 4821)
Pages this week:       6 (4 actionable). Noisy: TaskflowSyntheticCheckFailing from ap-south (ticket TF-881)
Risky changes ahead:   MongoDB 8.0 upgrade Wed 10:00 UTC, rollback plan in change 4830
Follow-ups for review: runbook for certificate expiry was out of date (fixed), ticket TF-879

    Best practices¶

    • Page on symptoms tied to SLOs; send causes to dashboards and tickets.
    • Make every page urgent, actionable, judgement-requiring, and novel.
    • Require a runbook_url and a dashboard link on every paging alert.
    • Use a small, written severity scheme that maps one-to-one to routing.
    • Group and inhibit aggressively so one problem produces one page.
    • Run Prometheus and Alertmanager redundantly, and watch them with an external dead man's switch.
    • Test rules and routes in CI with promtool and amtool.
    • Review every page weekly; fix or delete non-actionable alerts that week.
    • Keep rotations large enough, hand off in writing, compensate on-call, and give recovery time after bad nights.
    • Track pages per shift, actionable rate, and time to acknowledge.

    In production¶

    • The on-call engineer receives 40 pages in 2 minutes for one outage. Detect: the pager history shows one page per pod and per alert for a single database failure. Cause: group_by includes instance or pod, or no inhibition from the root-cause alert. Fix: group by alertname and service, add inhibit rules from "service down" to its dependent alerts, and alert on service-level symptoms rather than per-pod conditions.
    • A real outage was acknowledged and ignored. Detect: the postmortem timeline shows the page acknowledged within a minute but no action for 30 minutes. Cause: alert fatigue: the same alert had fired and self-resolved dozens of times that month. Fix: measure the actionable rate per alert, fix or delete flapping alerts, and tie paging to SLO burn rates.
    • Nobody was paged during a full outage. Detect: users reported the outage; the alert history shows the rule firing but no notification sent. Cause: Alertmanager was down, its pager secret had expired, or a route matcher had a typo in the severity label. Fix: an external dead man's switch on a Watchdog alert, amtool config routes test in CI, and a periodic end-to-end test page.
    • Alerts keep firing after the problem is fixed. Detect: the dashboard shows recovery, but the alert stays firing for an hour and re-pages. Cause: a long-window expression without a short window, or a repeat_interval that is too short for the receiver. Fix: use multiwindow burn-rate rules, and set repeat intervals per route.
    • A silence outlived the maintenance and hid a real failure. Detect: an incident review finds a matching silence created days earlier with no expiry note. Cause: broad silences (matching a whole service) with long durations. Fix: narrow matchers, short expiries, mandatory comments, and active silences listed in every handoff.
    • On-call engineers burn out and leave the rotation. Detect: rising pages outside working hours, people swapping shifts, and repeat incidents. Cause: noisy alerts, small rotations, and no time to fix root causes. Fix: cap on-call load, prioritize the top noisy alerts in planning, enlarge or merge rotations, and give recovery time.

    Important terminology¶

    • Alert rule: An expression evaluated periodically; when true (for the for duration), it fires.
    • Pending / firing: States of an alert before and after its for duration has elapsed.
    • Page: A notification that interrupts a person immediately.
    • Ticket: A tracked task for non-urgent follow-up.
    • Symptom / cause: What users experience versus why it happens.
    • Grouping: Combining related alerts into one notification.
    • Inhibition: Suppressing alerts while a related, more important alert fires.
    • Silence: A time-limited, manual mute of matching alerts.
    • Runbook: A document describing how to diagnose and mitigate a specific alert.
    • Escalation policy: Rules for who is notified when a page is not acknowledged.
    • Follow-the-sun: Splitting on-call across time zones to avoid night shifts.
    • Dead man's switch: An always-firing alert whose absence triggers notification, proving the pipeline works.
    • MTTA / MTTR: Mean time to acknowledge, and to restore or resolve.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Paging on CPU, memory, or individual pod restarts.
    • Thinking that more alerts mean better coverage.
    • Alerts without runbooks, owners, or links to dashboards.
    • Copying thresholds from a blog post instead of deriving them from SLOs.
    • Grouping by instance, producing one page per pod.
    • Silences without expiry or comments.
    • Monitoring the alerting system with itself.
    • Treating on-call as a punishment, or expecting one person to carry it alone.
    • Muting a noisy alert instead of fixing or deleting it.

    Revision questions¶

    1. Give two cause-based alerts and rewrite each as a symptom-based alert for TaskFlow API.
    2. What four properties should every paging alert have?
    3. Explain Alertmanager's grouping, inhibition, and silences with an example of each.
    4. What do group_wait, group_interval, and repeat_interval control?
    5. What belongs in a runbook linked from an alert?
    6. A rotation of five engineers takes weekly shifts. How many weeks a year is each person on-call as primary, and why might that be too much? (52 / 5 = about 10.4 weeks, one week in five, which leaves little time to recover and is often combined with secondary duty as well.)
    7. In one week the primary received 18 pages, 11 outside working hours, and 5 led to an action. What would you change first?
    8. Why does a Watchdog alert need an external receiver?
