    SLIs, SLOs, and Error Budgets¶

    "Is TaskFlow reliable enough?" is an argument until you put numbers on it. A service level indicator (SLI) measures one aspect of the service as users experience it, a service level objective (SLO) sets a target for that measurement over a time window, and the error budget is the amount of unreliability the target still allows. This lesson defines SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs, shows how to choose good SLIs, computes the "nines" correctly, works through error budget math, explains error budget policies, and builds the multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts recommended by Google's SRE Workbook, ending with an SLO document for TaskFlow API.

    Why it matters¶

    Without an agreed target, reliability work is driven by whoever shouts loudest: product wants features, operations wants stability, and every incident triggers a debate about whether things are "bad enough" to slow down. An SLO turns that into a shared, measurable contract inside the team. If the service is meeting its SLO, you ship; if it is burning the budget too fast, you stop and fix reliability. Nobody has to win an argument.

    SLOs also fix alerting. Instead of paging on CPU, memory, or every error spike, you page when users are being hurt fast enough to threaten the objective. That single change removes most alert noise, as the next lesson on alerting and on-call shows.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Google SRE book. The chapter Service Level Objectives defines SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs, explains why 100% is the wrong target, and recommends choosing a small number of indicators that reflect what users care about.
    • Google SRE Workbook: error budgets in practice. The chapter Implementing SLOs walks through choosing SLIs, measuring them, and using error budgets to make decisions, and the Example Error Budget Policy shows a policy that halts non-critical releases when a service exceeds its budget.
    • Google SRE Workbook: alerting. The chapter Alerting on SLOs compares six alerting approaches and recommends multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts, with a table of burn rates (14.4, 6, and 1) and windows for a 99.9% SLO.
    • Cloud provider SLAs. Public SLAs such as the Amazon Compute Service Level Agreement promise a monthly uptime percentage and define service credits when it is missed, which is the contractual layer that sits on top of internal SLOs.

    Popular tools and providers: Prometheus recording and alerting rules, Sloth and Pyrra (generate SLO rules for Prometheus), the OpenSLO specification, Grafana SLO, Datadog SLOs, Google Cloud Service Monitoring, Nobl9, and Amazon CloudWatch Application Signals.

    How it works¶

    SLI, SLO, and SLA¶

    Term What it is Who it is for TaskFlow example
    SLI (indicator) A measurement, usually a ratio of good events to valid events Engineers Proportion of /tasks requests that return a non-5xx response
    SLO (objective) A target for an SLI over a window The team and its stakeholders 99.9% of requests succeed over a rolling 30 days
    SLA (agreement) A contract with consequences, usually money or service credits Customers and lawyers 99.5% monthly availability or a 10% credit
    Error budget 1 - SLO: how much failure the SLO allows The team, to make decisions 0.1% of requests may fail: about 50,000 of 50 million a month

    An SLA should always be looser than the internal SLO. If TaskFlow promises customers 99.5%, the team aims for 99.9% so it notices and reacts long before the contract is at risk. Many internal services have SLOs and no SLA at all.

    Choosing good SLIs¶

    The SRE books recommend expressing SLIs as a ratio:

    SLI = good events / valid events   (x 100%)

    Common SLI types:

    Type Question TaskFlow SLI
    Availability Did the request succeed? Non-5xx responses / all API requests (excluding health checks)
    Latency Was it fast enough? Requests served in under 300 ms / all requests
    Freshness Is the data recent enough? Reminder emails sent within 5 minutes of the due time / reminders due
    Correctness Was the answer right? Synthetic task reads that return the expected content / synthetic reads
    Throughput or coverage Did the batch process everything? Nightly export jobs that complete by 06:00 / jobs scheduled

    Guidelines that make SLIs trustworthy:

    • Measure as close to the user as practical. Load balancer or ingress logs and metrics see requests that never reached the app (a crashed pod, a full connection queue). Application metrics miss those. Real-user or synthetic measurements from outside catch DNS and CDN failures too.
    • Define "valid" carefully. Exclude health checks and synthetic traffic you do not want to count. Decide deliberately how to treat 4xx: a 404 for a missing task is the client's problem (good, or not valid), but a 429 caused by your own overloaded rate limiter may well be a failure.
    • Use thresholds, not averages, for latency. "99% of requests faster than 300 ms" is understandable and directly measurable from a histogram bucket at le="0.3". Some teams use two thresholds (p90 under 100 ms and p99 under 500 ms).
    • Few SLIs per user journey. Two or three (availability, latency, maybe freshness) for each critical journey such as "create a task" and "list my tasks". Twenty SLIs is a dashboard, not an objective.

    SLO targets and the nines¶

    Allowed full-outage time per window:

    SLO Error budget Per 30 days (43,200 min) Per year (365 days)
    99% 1% 432 min = 7 h 12 min 3.65 days
    99.5% 0.5% 216 min = 3 h 36 min 1.83 days
    99.9% 0.1% 43.2 min = 43 min 12 s 8.76 hours
    99.95% 0.05% 21.6 min = 21 min 36 s 4.38 hours
    99.99% 0.01% 4.32 min = 4 min 19 s 52.6 minutes
    99.999% 0.001% 0.432 min = 26 s 5.26 minutes

    How to compute any row: window length times (1 - SLO). For 99.95% over 30 days: 43,200 min x 0.0005 = 21.6 min.

    Choosing a target:

    • Each extra nine costs far more than the last in engineering and infrastructure (the SRE book warns an increment may cost 100x more): redundancy across zones, faster detection, automated rollback, on-call staffing.
    • You cannot be more reliable than your dependencies without redundancy. If TaskFlow depends on a single managed database with a 99.95% SLA and a single region, a 99.99% SLO is fiction.
    • Users cannot tell the difference above a point. Their own Wi-Fi and mobile networks fail more often than 0.01% of the time.
    • Start from current performance. Measure the SLI for a few weeks, set the SLO slightly below what you already achieve, and tighten it as the service improves.
    • Prefer request-based SLOs over time-based ones for APIs: "0.1% of requests may fail" handles partial outages (one bad pod out of ten) better than "the service is up or down each minute".

    Error budget math¶

    A worked example for TaskFlow API:

    SLO: 99.9% of API requests succeed, rolling 30 days
Traffic: ~50,000,000 requests per 30 days (about 19 requests/second on average)

Error budget = 50,000,000 x (1 - 0.999) = 50,000 failed requests

Incident A: full outage for 15 minutes at 20 requests/second
  failed = 15 x 60 x 20 = 18,000            -> 36% of the budget

Incident B: bad deploy, 5% of requests fail for 2 hours at 25 requests/second
  failed = 2 x 3,600 x 25 x 0.05 = 9,000     -> 18% of the budget

Background: 0.02% of requests fail all month
  failed = 50,000,000 x 0.0002 = 10,000      -> 20% of the budget

Total used = 37,000 of 50,000 = 74%; 26% remaining

    The burn rate is how fast the budget is being consumed relative to the rate that would use exactly all of it over the window:

    burn rate = observed error ratio / (1 - SLO)

0.1% errors on a 99.9% SLO  -> burn rate 1    -> budget lasts exactly 30 days
0.5% errors                 -> burn rate 5    -> budget gone in 30 / 5 = 6 days
1.44% errors                -> burn rate 14.4 -> budget gone in about 2.1 days (50 h)
100% errors (full outage)   -> burn rate 1,000 -> budget gone in about 43 minutes

    Burn rate is the bridge between SLOs and alerting: a burn rate of 1 is exactly on budget, anything far above it is an emergency.

    Error budget policies¶

    An SLO without consequences is a dashboard. An error budget policy is a short, agreed document saying what the team does as the budget runs low. A typical policy for TaskFlow:

    Budget remaining (rolling 30 days) Action
    More than 50% Normal development; experiments and risky migrations allowed
    25% to 50% Extra review for risky changes; prioritize reliability bugs from recent incidents
    Below 25% Freeze non-critical feature releases; only fixes and reliability work ship
    Exhausted Freeze continues until the SLI is back within the SLO over the window; postmortem required for any single incident using more than 20%

    Good policies are agreed in advance by engineering and product leadership, apply automatically (no meeting needed to decide), define exceptions (security fixes always ship), and are reviewed when they do not seem to work. The budget is also permission: with 70% left near the end of the window, the team can afford a risky but valuable migration.

    Alerting on SLOs: multiwindow, multi-burn-rate¶

    The naive alert "error ratio over 0.1% for 5 minutes" fires on every small blip and resolves before anyone looks. The opposite, "budget below 10%", fires days too late. The SRE Workbook's recommendation is to alert on burn rate, over two windows at once:

    • A long window proves the burn is significant (not a 30-second spike).
    • A short window, one-twelfth of the long one, proves it is still happening, so the alert resolves soon after a fix instead of firing for another hour.

    Recommended starting parameters for a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, from the SRE Workbook:

    Severity Budget consumed Long window Short window Burn rate Error ratio threshold
    Page 2% 1 hour 5 minutes 14.4 14.4 x 0.1% = 1.44%
    Page 5% 6 hours 30 minutes 6 6 x 0.1% = 0.6%
    Ticket 10% 3 days 6 hours 1 1 x 0.1% = 0.1%

    Where 14.4 comes from: 2% of a 30-day budget consumed in 1 hour means burning at 0.02 x 720 hours / 1 hour = 14.4 times the sustainable rate. Likewise 0.05 x 720 / 6 = 6 and 0.10 x 720 / 72 = 1.

    Properties of this scheme:

    • Detection time scales with severity. A full outage (100% errors, burn rate 1,000) trips the 1-hour condition after about 0.0144 x 60 min = 52 s; a 2% error ratio takes about 43 minutes; a slow 0.2% leak never pages but opens a ticket within days.
    • Fast reset. The short window drops below the threshold minutes after a fix.
    • Precision. A page always means at least 2% (or 5%) of the monthly budget is being lost, which is worth waking someone for.

    Low-traffic services need care: with 20 requests an hour, one failure is a 5% error ratio. Options are synthetic traffic to keep a steady denominator, longer windows, or a minimum count of failed requests before alerting.

    SLO documents¶

    An SLO is written down and versioned, like code. The SRE Workbook's example SLO document includes the service overview, the SLIs and SLOs with their exact measurement, the rationale for each target, the error budget, clarifications and caveats, and links to the error budget policy and dashboards. Keep it in the service's repository and review it at least quarterly.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation Approach
    User-facing API or website Availability and latency SLOs per critical journey
    Asynchronous pipeline (reminders, exports) Freshness and completeness SLOs
    Internal platform used by other teams SLOs agreed with those teams; they are your users
    Prototype or internal tool with no users yet Skip formal SLOs; basic alerts are enough
    Hard regulatory or safety guarantees SLOs help operations, but they do not replace formal requirements and testing
    Contract with customers An SLA, backed by a stricter internal SLO

    SLOs are not a tool for ranking teams or punishing incidents, and they do not replace debugging telemetry; they decide when to act, not what is broken.

    Practical examples¶

    SLI recording rules for TaskFlow API¶

    # prometheus/rules/taskflow-slo-recording.yml
groups:
  - name: taskflow-slo-sli
    rules:
      # Availability SLI: share of requests that failed (5xx), per window.
      # Health checks and unmatched routes are not valid events.
      - record: taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[5m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[5m]))
      - record: taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate30m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[30m]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[30m]))
      - record: taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate1h
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[1h]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[1h]))
      - record: taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate6h
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[6h]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[6h]))
      - record: taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate3d
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[3d]))
          /
          sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[3d]))

      # Latency SLI: share of valid requests slower than 300 ms (bucket boundary at 0.3).
      - record: taskflow:slo_latency_bad:ratio_rate1h
        expr: |
          1 - (
            sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", le="0.3"}[1h]))
            /
            sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[1h]))
          )

    Tools such as Sloth or Pyrra generate exactly this kind of rule set from a short SLO definition, which avoids copy-paste mistakes across windows.

    Multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts¶

    # prometheus/rules/taskflow-slo-alerts.yml
groups:
  - name: taskflow-slo-alerts
    rules:
      - alert: TaskflowErrorBudgetBurnFast
        expr: |
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate1h > (14.4 * 0.001)
          and
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate5m > (14.4 * 0.001)
        labels:
          severity: page
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "TaskFlow API is burning its 30-day error budget at 14x or more"
          description: "1h error ratio {{ $value | humanizePercentage }}; at this rate 2% of the monthly budget is gone every hour."
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/error-budget-burn"

      - alert: TaskflowErrorBudgetBurnSustained
        expr: |
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate6h > (6 * 0.001)
          and
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate30m > (6 * 0.001)
        labels:
          severity: page
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "TaskFlow API has burned 5% of its error budget in 6 hours"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/error-budget-burn"

      - alert: TaskflowErrorBudgetBurnSlow
        expr: |
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate3d > (1 * 0.001)
          and
          taskflow:slo_errors:ratio_rate6h > (1 * 0.001)
        labels:
          severity: ticket
          service: taskflow-api
        annotations:
          summary: "TaskFlow API is on track to exhaust its error budget this month"
          runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/taskflow-api/error-budget-burn"

    No for: clause is needed: the windows already provide the smoothing, and adding for only delays detection. The severity label is what Alertmanager routes on (pager versus ticket queue), covered in alerting and on-call.

    Error budget remaining¶

    # Budget remaining over the last 30 days, as a fraction (1 = untouched, 0 = exhausted)
1 - (
  (
    sum(increase(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz", status_code=~"5.."}[30d]))
    /
    sum(increase(http_request_duration_seconds_count{job="taskflow-api", route!~"unmatched|/healthz|/readyz"}[30d]))
  ) / 0.001
)

    A query over 30 days of raw data is expensive; in production compute it from a recording rule, or from long-term storage such as Mimir or Thanos, and show it as the headline panel on the service dashboard.

    An SLO document for TaskFlow API¶

    # docs/slo/taskflow-api.yaml (team-owned, reviewed quarterly)
service: taskflow-api
owner: tasks-team
window: rolling 30 days
user_journeys:
  - name: create and list tasks
    endpoints: ["POST /tasks", "GET /tasks", "GET /tasks/:id", "PATCH /tasks/:id"]
slos:
  - name: availability
    sli: share of valid requests that do not return 5xx, measured at the ingress
    valid_events: all API requests except health checks and synthetic probes
    objective: 99.9%
    error_budget: 0.1% of requests (about 50,000 per 30 days at current traffic)
  - name: latency
    sli: share of valid requests served in under 300 ms, measured at the ingress
    objective: 99%
rationale: >
  Current performance over the last quarter is 99.95% availability and 99.4% under
  300 ms. Targets leave headroom for planned migrations. The managed database
  plan's SLA is stricter than 99.9%, so it does not cap these objectives.
alerting: multiwindow multi-burn-rate (14.4 / 6 page, 1 ticket), see taskflow-slo-alerts.yml
error_budget_policy: docs/slo/error-budget-policy.md
dashboard: https://grafana.example.com/d/taskflow-slo
review: quarterly, or after any incident consuming more than 20% of the budget

    The format is illustrative; the content follows the structure of the SRE Workbook's example document. The OpenSLO project defines a standard YAML schema for the same information if you want tooling to read it.

    Best practices¶

    • Write SLIs as good events divided by valid events, measured as close to users as practical.
    • Pick two or three SLIs per critical user journey; leave the rest to dashboards.
    • Set SLOs from measured current performance and real user needs, never 100%, and keep SLAs looser than SLOs.
    • Use request-based SLOs for APIs; time-based ones hide partial failures.
    • Align histogram buckets with latency SLO thresholds.
    • Alert on burn rate with long and short windows; page only for fast burns.
    • Agree an error budget policy in advance with product leadership, and follow it.
    • Version the SLO document with the service and review it regularly.
    • Report budget remaining on the main dashboard and in planning meetings.

    In production¶

    • The SLO dashboard is green while customers complain. Detect: support tickets and social media reports of errors, while the application-level SLI stays above target. Cause: the SLI is measured in the app, so requests that fail before reaching it (crashed pods, ingress timeouts, DNS or certificate problems) are invisible. Fix: measure at the load balancer or ingress, add synthetic checks from outside, and include those failures in the SLI.
    • The burn-rate page fires every night for a few minutes. Detect: repeated short pages at low-traffic hours, each with a handful of failed requests. Cause: tiny denominators make a few errors look like a high error ratio. Fix: add a minimum event count to the alert, generate steady synthetic traffic, or use longer windows for low-traffic services.
    • The budget is always exhausted, and nothing changes. Detect: budget remaining sits at zero for months while features keep shipping. Cause: an SLO set above what the system can deliver, or no error budget policy with agreed consequences. Fix: reset the target to measured performance, agree a policy with leadership, and invest in the top causes of budget burn.
    • The budget is never touched, and the team is still afraid to deploy. Detect: 95% of the budget remains every month, yet releases are slow and heavily gated. Cause: the SLO is looser than needed, or the team is not using the budget as permission. Fix: spend it deliberately on faster releases and experiments, or tighten the SLO if users need more.
    • Latency SLI values jump when the dashboard is changed from 5m to 1h. Detect: the "requests under 300 ms" ratio looks different across panels and the alert disagrees with the dashboard. Cause: the histogram has no bucket at 0.3 s, so the ratio is interpolated, or panels average ratios instead of dividing summed rates. Fix: add a le="0.3" bucket and compute ratios as sum(rate(good[1h])) / sum(rate(valid[1h])), with the same range for both.
    • A dependency outage burns the whole budget and the team argues about whether it counts. Detect: a postmortem debate over excluding the incident. Cause: the SLO document did not define how dependency failures are treated. Fix: users felt it, so it counts; record the decision in the SLO document and invest in redundancy or graceful degradation for that dependency.

    Important terminology¶

    • SLI: A measured indicator of service level, usually good events / valid events.
    • SLO: A target value for an SLI over a time window.
    • SLA: A contract with customers that includes consequences for missing a target.
    • Error budget: 1 - SLO; the allowed amount of failure in the window.
    • Burn rate: Observed error ratio divided by the error budget ratio; 1 means on budget.
    • Rolling window: A window that moves with time (the last 30 days), as opposed to a calendar month.
    • Error budget policy: Agreed actions taken as the budget is consumed.
    • Multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alert: An alert that requires both a long and a short window to exceed a burn-rate threshold, at several severities.
    • The nines: Shorthand for availability levels (three nines = 99.9%).

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Targeting 100%, which forbids change and is impossible anyway.
    • Using SLA numbers as internal SLOs, leaving no margin before contractual breaches.
    • Measuring SLIs only inside the app.
    • Averaging latency instead of using thresholds and histograms.
    • Having dozens of SLOs, so none of them drives decisions.
    • Alerting on raw error ratios with a short for: instead of burn rates.
    • Computing nines wrongly: 99.9% is 43 minutes a month, not 4.3, and 99.99% is about 4 minutes, not 43.
    • Defining SLOs and never agreeing what happens when they are missed.
    • Treating error budgets as a quota to be avoided rather than spent.

    Revision questions¶

    1. Explain the difference between an SLI, an SLO, and an SLA with a TaskFlow example of each. Why must the SLA be looser than the SLO?
    2. How much full downtime does a 99.95% SLO allow in 30 days, and in a year? (21 min 36 s; about 4.38 hours.)
    3. TaskFlow serves 80 million requests in 30 days with a 99.9% SLO. How many requests may fail? A 25-minute outage at 40 requests per second uses what share of that budget? (80,000; 25 x 60 x 40 = 60,000, which is 75%.)
    4. The current error ratio is 0.3% on a 99.9% SLO. What is the burn rate, and when will a fresh budget run out? (3; in 10 days.)
    5. Where does the burn-rate threshold 14.4 come from, and why does the alert need both a 1-hour and a 5-minute window?
    6. Why should the availability SLI be measured at the ingress rather than inside the Express app?
    7. Should a 429 Too Many Requests count as a good or bad event? Argue both sides.
    8. What should an error budget policy say, and who must agree to it?
