    DevOps, SRE, and Platform Engineering¶

    Job advertisements use "DevOps engineer", "site reliability engineer", and "platform engineer" almost interchangeably, yet the three names come from different places and describe different mechanisms. DevOps is a philosophy about how development and operations work together. Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) is Google's concrete implementation of the reliability side of it, with error budgets and limits on manual work. Platform engineering builds internal products that make good practice self-service for every team.

    This lesson defines each one from its primary sources, shows how they fit together, explains toil and the SRE time budget with a worked example, describes the common ways SRE teams are organized, and introduces Team Topologies' four team types and three interaction modes, applied to the teams that build TaskFlow.

    Why it matters¶

    Organizations regularly get these models wrong in ways that cost years. An operations team is renamed "SRE" but still spends all its time on tickets. A "DevOps team" is created to own every deployment and becomes the new bottleneck. A platform team builds a portal nobody asked for and then mandates it. Each failure comes from copying a name without the mechanism behind it: SRE works because it caps operational work and ties releases to an error budget; platforms work because they are run as products for developers; DevOps works because the people who build a service also run it.

    Knowing the mechanisms also helps you choose. A five-person startup needs DevOps habits but not an SRE organization. A company with forty product teams that each built their own pipeline probably needs a platform team. A service with strict reliability needs may justify dedicated SREs.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Google's definition of SRE. In the introduction to the SRE book, Benjamin Treynor Sloss writes: "SRE is what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team." He describes joining Google in 2003 to run a production team of seven engineers, and the rule that "Google places a 50% cap on the aggregate 'ops' work for all SREs."
    • SRE and DevOps. The SRE Workbook chapter How SRE Relates to DevOps sums up the relationship as "class SRE implements interface DevOps", and clarifies that "product development teams in Google own their service by default"; SRE "is neither available nor warranted for the bulk of services."
    • How SRE teams are organized. Google Cloud's 2019 post How SRE teams are organized, and how to get started describes six implementations, from a "kitchen sink" team that does everything to consulting teams that advise without changing code, with the pros and cons of each.
    • Platform engineering. DORA's platform engineering capability defines an internal developer platform as shared tools, services, and "golden paths", and reports that by 2025, 90% of organizations used an internal developer platform and 76% had dedicated platform teams.
    • Team Topologies. The Team Topologies key concepts describe four fundamental team types (stream-aligned, enabling, complicated subsystem, and platform) and three interaction modes (collaboration, X-as-a-Service, and facilitating), and call stream-aligned teams "You Built It, You Run It" teams.

    Popular tools and providers: SLO tooling such as Prometheus with Sloth or Pyrra, Grafana SLO, and Datadog SLOs; incident and on-call tools such as PagerDuty and incident.io; platform building blocks such as Kubernetes, Argo CD, Crossplane, and Terraform; and developer portals such as Backstage.

    How it works¶

    Three names for answers to one problem¶

    All three respond to the same problem from the previous lesson: work and knowledge split between people who change systems and people who run them. They answer it at different levels.

    DevOps SRE Platform engineering
    What it is A culture and set of practices A job role and a set of practices, defined at Google A discipline that builds internal products for developers
    Origin Practitioner movement, 2008 to 2009 Google, from 2003 Grew from DevOps and SRE as organizations scaled
    Core mechanism Shared ownership, CALMS, the Three Ways SLOs and error budgets, a cap on operational work, toil reduction Self-service golden paths, run as a product
    Primary customer Users of the product Users of the service, through its reliability Internal developers
    Typical measures DORA delivery metrics SLO compliance, error budget, share of time on toil, pages per shift Adoption, developer satisfaction, product teams' DORA metrics
    Typical failure A "DevOps team" that becomes a new silo An operations team renamed SRE, still doing tickets all day A ticket queue or a mandated portal nobody wants

    SRE as Google defines it¶

    The SRE book describes the traditional "sysadmin" model, in which an operations team grows linearly with the service, because every new server, customer, and feature adds manual work. SRE's answer is to staff operations with software engineers who will "quickly become bored by performing tasks by hand" and have the skills to automate them. The book lists the core tenets every Google SRE team follows:

    • Ensuring a durable focus on engineering: at most 50% of SRE time on operational work, at most two events per 8 to 12-hour on-call shift so each gets proper follow-up, and blame-free postmortems for all significant incidents.
    • Pursuing maximum change velocity without violating the SLO: the error budget, described below.
    • Monitoring: software interprets signals, and humans are notified only when they need to act: alerts for action now, tickets for action later; logs are kept for diagnosis and notify no one.
    • Emergency response: reliability depends on time to repair, and the book reports that playbooks written in advance produce "roughly a 3x improvement in MTTR" compared with "winging it".
    • Change management: "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system", so SRE automates progressive rollouts, fast detection, and safe rollback.
    • Demand forecasting, capacity planning, provisioning, efficiency and performance: SRE owns capacity because capacity is availability (see load testing fundamentals and autoscaling server fleets).

    The 50% cap is enforced, not aspirational. Google measures how SRE time is spent, and when operational work exceeds the cap the excess is redirected to the product development team: bugs and tickets go to development managers, and developers rejoin the pager rotation. The SRE Workbook suggests thinking of the limit less as a cap than as "a guarantee": an explicit promise that engineering time exists to remove the work, rather than toiling at it forever.

    Error budgets: the contract between development and SRE¶

    SRE replaces the argument between "ship faster" and "change nothing" with a number. The product owners choose an availability target, and "the error budget is one minus the availability target." A 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows about 43 minutes of full outage, or 0.1% of requests failing. While budget remains, teams spend it on launches and experiments; when it is gone, an agreed policy slows or stops risky releases until reliability recovers. In the SRE book's words, "SRE's goal is no longer 'zero outages'; rather, SREs and product developers aim to spend the error budget getting maximum feature velocity."

    SLIs, SLO targets, burn rates, and error budget policies are covered in depth in SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets.

    Toil¶

    The SRE book's chapter Eliminating Toil defines toil as "the kind of work tied to running a production service that tends to be manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and that scales linearly as a service grows."

    Attribute Test TaskFlow example
    Manual A human does it, even if only by running a script Running npm run migrate on the production host by hand
    Repetitive Done over and over, not a one-off Resetting staging test data every few days
    Automatable A machine could do it, or the need could be designed away Granting read access to production logs via tickets
    Tactical Interrupt-driven and reactive; the book counts handling pager alerts Restarting a stuck reminder worker at 3 a.m.
    No enduring value The service is in the same state afterwards Answering "is the API down?" in chat
    O(n) with growth Grows with traffic, users, or services Rotating API keys for every new partner by hand

    Not all unpleasant work is toil. Team meetings, goal setting, and HR paperwork are overhead, and cleaning up a messy alert configuration may be grungy but has lasting value. Google reports that its SREs spend on average about 33% of their time on toil, with wide variation between individuals, against a target of under 50%.

    A worked example. The four engineers on the TaskFlow tasks team (160 hours a week) keep a toil log for a month:

    toil: 15.5 h/week of 160 h team time (9.7%)

task                                   h/week   hours to automate   payback
Reset staging test data                   2.0                   6   3.0 weeks
Answer is-the-API-down questions          3.0                  10   3.3 weeks
Rotate partner API keys by hand           4.0                  24   6.0 weeks
Grant read access to production logs      2.0                  12   6.0 weeks
Run database migrations by hand           3.0                  20   6.7 weeks
Restart stuck reminder worker             1.5                  16  10.7 weeks

    Ranking by payback (hours to automate divided by hours saved per week) tells the team what to automate first: a staging reset script pays for itself in three weeks, a public status page in just over three. The script that produces this report is in the practical examples below.

    How SRE teams are organized¶

    Google Cloud's 2019 post describes six implementations, roughly in the order organizations adopt them:

    Implementation Scope Main strength Main risk
    Kitchen sink ("everything SRE") Every service and workflow, usually the first SRE team Easy to start without reorganizing Unbounded scope; day-to-day demands crowd out SRE practices
    Infrastructure Shared infrastructure such as clusters, CI/CD, monitoring, networking Defines production standards as code that simplify every team's work Can drift away from customer experience
    Tools Software that helps others measure and improve reliability Reusable reliability tooling Solving the wrong problems if far from front-line teams
    Product or application One critical application or business area Clear link between business priorities and SRE effort Duplicated infrastructure and divergent practices across many such teams
    Embedded SREs work inside development teams for a period, changing code and configuration Hands-on teaching of SRE practices Divergent practices; embedded SREs lose contact with peers
    Consulting Advises teams without changing their code Scales the impact of an experienced SRE group Advice without enough context; seen as hands-off

    The post recommends starting even earlier, with a few engineers spending part of their time as SRE advocates, before staffing any dedicated team. It also notes that an SRE team can act as the company's "reliability standards and practices" group, defining how production may be changed, how incidents run, and how error budgets work.

    Platform engineering¶

    DORA defines platform engineering as "a sociotechnical discipline" focused on "automation, self-service, and repeatability", which designs and builds toolchains and workflows, "often called an 'Internal Developer Platform'", that provide shared tools, services, and "golden paths". It stresses that the platform is "an internal product designed specifically for developers", not a collection of infrastructure tickets.

    The idea is not new. In his 2012 post against "DevOps teams", Jez Humble described the one central team he did support, listing its first job as "Builds a platform that allows developers to self-service environments for testing and production", and added: "This platform is a product." Google SRE reached a similar place from the other direction. The SRE book chapter The Evolving SRE Engagement Model describes how reviewing services one at a time did not scale, so SRE built shared frameworks and a common production platform; services built on it become "production ready" by design, SRE supports the platform, and development teams provide on-call support for bugs in their own application code.

    Platforms are covered in depth in platform engineering and developer portals.

    How the three relate¶

    Read the diagram as an interface and two implementations:

    • DevOps says what good looks like: shared ownership, small batches, fast feedback, continuous learning. The SRE Workbook notes it is "relatively silent on how to run operations at a detailed level."
    • SRE says how to run reliable services: an "opinionated intellectual framework (including concepts like error budgets)" with measurable limits on toil and paging.
    • Platform engineering says how to make the right way the easy way for many teams: a product that turns repeated decisions (how to build, deploy, observe, and secure a service) into self-service defaults.
    • Product teams remain the owners. Neither SRE nor a platform team should take ownership of other teams' services away from them. Their job is to make that ownership affordable.

    A small organization may have all three in one person's head: a team that owns its service (DevOps), sets an SLO with an error budget (SRE), and reuses one shared deploy workflow (a very thin platform).

    Team Topologies¶

    Melvin Conway observed in 1968 that "organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" (How Do Committees Invent?). Team Topologies, by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais (second edition 2025), turns that observation into a design tool: decide how teams should interact so that the architecture you want, and fast flow of change, become the natural result.

    Team type Purpose (from the key concepts page) TaskFlow example
    Stream-aligned "aligned to a flow of work from (usually) a segment of the business domain"; owns it end to end Tasks team (API), Web team, Reminders team
    Platform "provide a compelling internal product to accelerate delivery by Stream-aligned teams" Delivery platform team: CI/CD templates, Kubernetes, observability
    Enabling "helps a Stream-aligned team to overcome obstacles"; temporarily boosts skills, then moves on Reliability enabling team teaching SLOs and on-call practice
    Complicated subsystem "where significant mathematics/calculation/technical expertise is needed" Scheduling engine that suggests due dates
    Interaction mode Meaning When to use it
    Collaboration Working closely together for a defined period to discover new things A new capability whose interface is not yet known; high bandwidth, high cost, so time-box it
    X-as-a-Service One team provides and the other consumes, with minimal interaction Mature, well-understood capabilities such as the deploy pipeline
    Facilitating One team helps and mentors another Teaching a practice until the receiving team can do it alone

    The approach is built around team cognitive load: a team can only own so much before quality and speed fall. Platform, enabling, and complicated-subsystem teams exist to reduce the load on stream-aligned teams, and the key concepts page warns that "most internal platforms become bloated monstrosities" unless they follow the Thinnest Viable Platform idea: provide just enough capability to make stream-aligned teams faster.

    Job titles in practice¶

    Titles vary between companies, so read the job description, not the label. Typical scopes are:

    Title Usually means Watch out for
    DevOps engineer Builds pipelines, infrastructure as code, and deployment tooling, often inside or alongside product teams A role that owns everyone's deployments, recreating the wall
    Site reliability engineer Owns reliability of services with SLOs, incident response, capacity, and automation; spends a bounded share of time on operations An operations job renamed, with no engineering time or error budget
    Platform engineer Builds and runs the internal platform as a product: golden paths, self-service APIs, developer portal A ticket-driven infrastructure team with a new name

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Context Good fit Not yet
    One team, one product, early stage DevOps habits: the team owns build, deploy, and on-call; one SLO per critical journey A separate SRE team or a platform team
    3 to 10 product teams, each solving deployment differently A thin platform: shared pipeline templates, one deploy path, a service catalog A large portal project before the common needs are known
    A critical service whose outages are expensive Dedicated SRE support (product or embedded), with a production readiness review SRE for every service; most should stay with their developers
    Many teams, high cognitive load, repeated reliability problems Platform team plus an SRE "standards and practices" or enabling team Mandating tools without user research
    An operations team that wants to "become SRE" Measure toil, set SLOs, cap operational work, automate the top toil Renaming the team and keeping the ticket queue

    The SRE Workbook calls renaming without changing practices "rename-and-shame" and warns it is "a hollow one, unlikely to yield benefit." The same is true of renaming an infrastructure team "platform".

    Practical examples¶

    A toil log and payback report¶

    Keep a simple log of recurring operational work for a few weeks:

    # toil.csv
task,kind,per_week,minutes_each,automation_hours
Restart stuck reminder worker,manual fix,3,30,16
Rotate partner API keys by hand,ticket,2,120,24
Answer is-the-API-down questions,interrupt,12,15,10
Reset staging test data,ticket,4,30,6
Run database migrations by hand,release chore,3,60,20
Grant read access to production logs,ticket,6,20,12

    // scripts/toil-report.js: weekly toil, share of team time, and automation payback
// Usage: node scripts/toil-report.js toil.csv <engineers> <hours per engineer per week>
import { readFile } from "node:fs/promises";

const [file, engineers = "4", hoursEach = "40"] = process.argv.slice(2);
const teamHours = Number(engineers) * Number(hoursEach);

const [header, ...rows] = (await readFile(file, "utf8")).trim().split(/\r?\n/);
const cols = header.split(",");
const items = rows.map((line) => {
  const v = Object.fromEntries(line.split(",").map((x, i) => [cols[i], x]));
  const weekly = (Number(v.per_week) * Number(v.minutes_each)) / 60;
  return { task: v.task, weekly, automate: Number(v.automation_hours) };
});

const total = items.reduce((sum, i) => sum + i.weekly, 0);
console.log(`toil: ${total.toFixed(1)} h/week of ${teamHours} h team time (${((100 * total) / teamHours).toFixed(1)}%)`);
console.log("payback  h/week  build h  task");
for (const i of items.sort((a, b) => a.automate / a.weekly - b.automate / b.weekly)) {
  const payback = `${(i.automate / i.weekly).toFixed(1)} wk`;
  console.log(`${payback.padStart(7)}  ${i.weekly.toFixed(1).padStart(6)}  ${String(i.automate).padStart(7)}  ${i.task}`);
}

    $ node scripts/toil-report.js toil.csv 4 40
toil: 15.5 h/week of 160 h team time (9.7%)
payback  h/week  build h  task
 3.0 wk     2.0        6  Reset staging test data
 3.3 wk     3.0       10  Answer is-the-API-down questions
 6.0 wk     4.0       24  Rotate partner API keys by hand
 6.0 wk     2.0       12  Grant read access to production logs
 6.7 wk     3.0       20  Run database migrations by hand
10.7 wk     1.5       16  Restart stuck reminder worker

    The parser is deliberately simple (no quoted commas in fields). The numbers are estimates; their job is to rank the work, not to be precise. Notice that the cheapest fix is not always to automate the task: the stuck reminder worker should be fixed so it stops getting stuck.

    A production readiness checklist¶

    Google SRE runs a Production Readiness Review (PRR) before taking on a service, to "verify that a service meets accepted standards of production setup and operational readiness". A lightweight version is useful for any team launching a service it will run itself:

    # docs/production-readiness.yaml: TaskFlow reminders service
service: taskflow-reminders
owner: reminders-team
review_date: 2026-11-10
checks:
  slos:
    - "Freshness SLO: 99% of reminders sent within 5 minutes of the due time"
    - "Dashboard shows every SLI in the SLO document"
  alerting:
    - "Burn-rate alerts page the owning team's rotation"
    - "Every paging alert links to a runbook"
  operations:
    - "On-call rotation has at least two people per shift (primary and secondary)"
    - "Rollback tested in staging within the last 30 days"
    - "Graceful shutdown drains in-flight jobs on SIGTERM"
  capacity:
    - "Load test at 3x the expected peak reminders per minute"
    - "Queue depth alert before the backlog breaks the freshness SLO"
  data:
    - "Backups restored successfully in the last quarter"
  dependencies:
    - "Behavior documented and tested when the email provider is down"
open_items:
  - "Add a runbook for 'reminder backlog growing' (owner: reminders-team, due 2026-11-24)"

    The format is illustrative; the value is in reviewing the answers with someone who has operated similar services, before users depend on them.

    A team charter with Team Topologies vocabulary¶

    Writing down each team's type, ownership, and interactions makes dependencies explicit and shows when an interaction mode should change:

    # teams/reminders-team.yaml
team: reminders-team
type: stream-aligned
owns:
  services: [taskflow-reminders]
  on_call: true                       # you build it, you run it
interactions:
  - team: delivery-platform
    mode: x-as-a-service              # uses pipelines, clusters, dashboards
  - team: reliability-enabling
    mode: facilitating
    purpose: "Define SLOs and set up burn-rate alerting"
    until: 2026-12-18                 # enabling help ends; the skill stays
  - team: tasks-team
    mode: x-as-a-service              # consumes the tasks API, versioned contract
cognitive_load_review: quarterly

    Interactions that never end are a warning sign: a facilitating engagement that runs for a year has become a dependency, and a collaboration that never settles into X-as-a-Service usually means the interface between the teams is still unclear.

    Best practices¶

    • Start with DevOps habits in every team: own the service, automate the path to production, and learn from incidents.
    • Adopt SRE mechanisms, not just the title: SLOs with error budget policies, a cap on operational work, and measured toil.
    • Keep ownership with product teams by default; reserve dedicated SRE support for services that justify it.
    • Measure toil for a few weeks before automating, and rank work by payback.
    • Build platforms as products with user research, a roadmap, and measured adoption; start with the thinnest platform that helps.
    • Name team types and interaction modes explicitly, time-box collaboration and facilitation, and revisit them as needs change.
    • Watch cognitive load: when a team owns too much, split ownership or move undifferentiated work to a platform.

    In production¶

    • The new SRE team spends every day on tickets. Detect: a time-tracking sample shows 80% or more of SRE time on tickets and pages; no automation projects ship for a quarter. Cause: an operations team was renamed without a cap on operational work or a mechanism to push excess back. Fix: measure the ops share, agree a 50% cap with leadership, redirect excess work to the owning development teams, and fund the top toil fixes.
    • Releases are blocked by the SRE team on every launch. Detect: launch reviews queue for weeks and developers route around them with "small" changes. Cause: reliability is enforced by gatekeeping instead of an agreed error budget. Fix: define SLOs and an error budget policy with product leadership, and let the budget, not a reviewer, decide when releases slow down.
    • Developers carry the pager and burn out. Detect: rising after-hours pages per person and people leaving the rotation. Cause: ownership was handed to product teams without runbooks, sane alerts, or platform support. Fix: cap pages per shift, give the team time to fix noisy alerts, and provide platform defaults for deploys, rollbacks, and dashboards.
    • The platform team's backlog is all tickets. Detect: most platform work items are "create a database for team X" requests; developers wait days. Cause: "ticket-ops", which DORA lists as a platform pitfall. Fix: turn the most frequent requests into self-service APIs or templates, and measure time from request to use.
    • Each product team built its own pipeline and observability stack. Detect: a dozen different deploy scripts and logging formats; security fixes take months to roll out everywhere. Cause: DevOps ownership without any shared platform. Fix: extract the common path into a thin, optional platform and migrate teams by making it clearly easier than their own tooling.
    • An enabling team has been "helping" the same team for a year. Detect: the receiving team still cannot operate the practice without the enabling team. Cause: facilitation turned into dependency; the enabling team does the work instead of teaching it. Fix: agree an end date and a skill to transfer, and pair rather than do the work for them.

    Important terminology¶

    • DevOps: The culture and practices of shared ownership between building and running software.
    • Site Reliability Engineering (SRE): Google's approach of running operations as a software problem, with SLOs, error budgets, and a cap on operational work.
    • Error budget: One minus the availability target; the amount of unreliability a service may spend.
    • Toil: Manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical operational work with no enduring value that grows with the service.
    • Overhead: Administrative work that is not tied to running a production service, such as meetings.
    • Production Readiness Review (PRR): A review of a service's operational readiness before SRE support or launch.
    • Platform engineering: Building internal developer platforms as products that make delivery self-service.
    • Internal developer platform (IDP): The tools, services, and golden paths a platform team provides to developers.
    • Stream-aligned team: A team aligned to a flow of work that owns it end to end.
    • Enabling team: A team that helps others gain a capability, then moves on.
    • Complicated-subsystem team: A team owning a part that needs deep specialist knowledge.
    • Interaction modes: Collaboration, X-as-a-Service, and facilitating.
    • Cognitive load: The mental effort a team needs to own its work.
    • Conway's law: Systems mirror the communication structures of the organizations that design them.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Treating DevOps, SRE, and platform engineering as competing alternatives rather than layers that fit together.
    • Renaming an operations team "SRE" without SLOs, error budgets, or a limit on toil.
    • Believing SRE means a separate team must run every service.
    • Counting all disliked work as toil; meetings are overhead, and cleanup with lasting value is not toil.
    • Automating toil that should have been designed away.
    • Building a platform before several teams share the same need.
    • Mandating a platform instead of making it the easiest path.
    • Ignoring Conway's law: drawing an architecture that the team structure cannot produce.

    Revision questions¶

    1. Explain "class SRE implements interface DevOps". What does SRE add that DevOps leaves open?
    2. What happens at Google when operational work exceeds 50% of SRE time, and why is that a feedback mechanism rather than a punishment?
    3. Name the six attributes of toil and give a TaskFlow example of each. Why are team meetings not toil?
    4. A team of five engineers works 40 hours a week each. A toil log shows 70 hours a week of toil, including a 10-hour weekly task that would take 40 hours to automate. What share of time is toil before and after automating that task, and what is the payback period? (35% of 200 hours; 60 / 200 = 30% afterwards; 4 weeks.)
    5. Compare the kitchen sink, embedded, and consulting SRE implementations. Which would you start with in a company of 60 engineers, and why?
    6. Why did Jez Humble accept a central team that builds a self-service platform while rejecting a "DevOps team"?
    7. Describe the four Team Topologies team types and when each interaction mode is appropriate.
    8. Your organization wants to "become SRE" next quarter. What would you measure first and what would you change before the name?
