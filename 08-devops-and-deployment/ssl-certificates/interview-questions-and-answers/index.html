    SSL Certificates Interview Questions and Answers¶

    These questions review TLS and certificate fundamentals, obtaining and renewing certificates, and TLS termination and troubleshooting. Try answering each one before reading the answer.

    TLS and certificate fundamentals¶

    1. What is the difference between SSL and TLS?¶

    SSL is the original protocol (SSL 2.0 and 3.0), now prohibited because of serious weaknesses. TLS replaced it; TLS 1.0 and 1.1 are also deprecated (RFC 8996), so modern servers offer TLS 1.2 and 1.3 only. "SSL certificate" is a historical name: the same X.509 certificate is used with any TLS version.

    2. What three security properties does TLS provide?¶

    Confidentiality (traffic is encrypted with keys from the handshake), integrity (each record carries an authentication tag, so tampering is detected), and authentication (the server proves, with its certificate and a signature, that it is the named host; mutual TLS also authenticates the client).

    3. What does TLS not hide from a network observer?¶

    The IP addresses, the size and timing of traffic, and, unless Encrypted Client Hello is used, the hostname in the SNI field of ClientHello. It also says nothing about whether the server itself is trustworthy or secure.

    4. Walk through the TLS 1.3 handshake.¶

    The client sends ClientHello with supported versions, cipher suites, signature algorithms, SNI, ALPN, and a key share. The server replies with ServerHello and its key share; both derive handshake keys via ECDHE. The server then sends, encrypted, EncryptedExtensions, Certificate, CertificateVerify (a signature over the handshake), and Finished. The client verifies everything and sends Finished, and application data can flow after one round trip.

    5. Why is TLS 1.3 faster and more private than TLS 1.2?¶

    TLS 1.3 needs one round trip for a full handshake instead of two, because the client sends its key share in the first message. It encrypts the certificate and most handshake messages, removes static RSA key exchange and legacy ciphers, and makes forward secrecy mandatory.

    6. What is forward secrecy?¶

    Session keys are derived from ephemeral (EC)DHE key shares that are discarded after the handshake, so an attacker who records traffic and later steals the server's long-term private key still cannot decrypt the recorded sessions.

    7. What is 0-RTT early data, and what is its risk?¶

    With session resumption, a TLS 1.3 client can send application data in its first flight. That data can be replayed by an attacker, so it is safe only for idempotent requests, and many servers leave it disabled.

    8. What are the most important fields in an X.509 server certificate?¶

    The subject alternative name list (the hostnames it is valid for), the validity period (notBefore and notAfter), the issuer, the public key, key usage and extended key usage (serverAuth), basic constraints (CA:FALSE), and the issuer's signature. Clients match the hostname against the SAN entries, not the subject CN.

    9. Explain the chain of trust.¶

    The leaf certificate is signed by an intermediate CA, which is signed by a root CA. Roots are self-signed and trusted because they are in the client's trust store (browser, OS, runtime). The client verifies each signature up the chain and checks that it ends at a trusted root.

    10. Why must the server send intermediate certificates, and why not the root?¶

    Clients have the roots but not necessarily the intermediates, so the server sends the leaf plus all intermediates (fullchain.pem). The root is already on the client and sending it adds bytes without adding trust. Browsers can sometimes fetch missing intermediates; most API clients cannot.

    11. What are DV, OV, and EV certificates?¶

    Domain validation proves control of the domain only and is issued automatically (Let's Encrypt issues only DV). Organization validation and extended validation also verify the organization, with stricter checks for EV. The encryption is identical, and browsers no longer show special EV indicators.

    12. What does a wildcard certificate for *.example.com cover?¶

    Exactly one label: api.example.com and app.example.com, but not example.com and not v2.api.example.com. It is convenient for many subdomains, but one private key then protects them all. Let's Encrypt requires the DNS-01 challenge for wildcards.

    13. What is Certificate Transparency, and why should you monitor it?¶

    Public, append-only logs where CAs record every publicly trusted certificate; browsers require proof of logging (SCTs). Monitoring the logs for your domains reveals certificates you did not request. It also means every hostname in a public certificate is public, so internal names belong on a private CA.

    14. How do HSTS and the preload list work?¶

    The Strict-Transport-Security header tells browsers to use only HTTPS for the domain for max-age seconds, optionally for all subdomains. The preload list builds this into browsers so even the first visit is protected; it requires a long max-age, includeSubDomains, and HTTPS on every subdomain, and it is slow to undo.

    15. How long can a public TLS certificate be valid today?¶

    Under CA/Browser Forum Ballot SC-081v3, certificates issued from March 15, 2026 may be valid for at most 200 days, falling to 100 days from March 15, 2027 and 47 days from March 15, 2029 (it was 398 days before). Let's Encrypt issues 90-day certificates by default, moving to 64 days on February 10, 2027 and 45 days on February 16, 2028.

    16. Why is revocation considered unreliable, and what replaces it?¶

    Many clients soft-fail when they cannot check CRLs or OCSP, OCSP has privacy and availability problems, and Let's Encrypt shut down its OCSP service in 2025. Browsers use aggregated revocation data (CRLSets, CRLite), and the main mitigation is short certificate lifetimes.

    17. What are SNI and ALPN?¶

    Server Name Indication is the hostname the client puts in ClientHello so a server hosting many names can choose the right certificate. ALPN (Application-Layer Protocol Negotiation) lets client and server agree on the protocol inside TLS, such as h2 or http/1.1, without an extra round trip.

    18. Should you use RSA or ECDSA certificates?¶

    Both are secure at recommended sizes (RSA 2048 bits or more, ECDSA P-256). ECDSA keys and signatures are much smaller and faster, and all modern clients support them. Many sites serve ECDSA, and some serve both an ECDSA and an RSA certificate for very old clients.

    19. What are PEM, DER, and PKCS#12 files?¶

    PEM is Base64 text between -----BEGIN ...----- lines and can hold several certificates or a key (fullchain.pem, privkey.pem). DER is the same data in binary. PKCS#12 (.p12, .pfx) is a password-protected bundle of a certificate chain and its private key, common on Windows and in Java.

    Obtaining and renewing certificates¶

    20. What is ACME, and what are its main steps?¶

    The protocol (RFC 8555) for automated issuance: the client registers an account key, creates an order for identifiers, completes a challenge for each authorization, finalizes the order with a CSR, and downloads the certificate chain. Let's Encrypt, ZeroSSL, and other CAs support it.

    21. Compare HTTP-01, DNS-01, and TLS-ALPN-01 challenges.¶

    HTTP-01 serves a token file on port 80 and is simplest for public servers. DNS-01 creates a TXT record at _acme-challenge.<domain>, works for internal hosts, and is required for wildcards. TLS-ALPN-01 answers on port 443 with a special certificate and suits TLS-terminating proxies when port 80 is blocked. Only DNS-01 issues wildcards.

    22. What are the risks of DNS-01, and how do you reduce them?¶

    The client needs DNS API credentials, often able to change the whole zone, stored on a server. Reduce the risk with tokens limited to TXT records, delegating _acme-challenge via CNAME to a dedicated validation zone, or running issuance on a separate, hardened host. Also allow for DNS propagation delays.

    23. How does certbot renew certificates, and what is a deploy hook?¶

    A systemd timer or cron job runs certbot renew twice a day; since certbot 4.0, certificates are renewed by default when a third of their lifetime remains (30 days for a 90-day certificate), or earlier when the CA's ARI suggests it. A deploy hook runs only after a successful renewal, for example systemctl reload nginx, so the server actually starts using the new certificate.

    24. What is ACME Renewal Information (ARI)?¶

    An ACME extension (RFC 9773) where the CA tells the client when to renew a specific certificate. It lets the CA spread load and ask for early renewal before a mass revocation, and at Let's Encrypt, ARI-driven renewals are exempt from rate limits.

    25. What does Caddy do differently from nginx with certbot?¶

    Caddy obtains and renews certificates automatically for every site address that is a domain, redirects HTTP to HTTPS, and loads renewed certificates itself. There is no separate client, timer, or reload to configure; you only persist its data directory.

    26. How does cert-manager work in Kubernetes?¶

    Issuers and ClusterIssuers describe how to get certificates (ACME, private CA, cloud CAs). A Certificate resource, created directly or from an Ingress annotation, declares names and a Secret name. cert-manager runs the order and challenges, stores tls.crt and tls.key in the Secret, and renews at about two-thirds of the lifetime by default.

    27. What are managed certificates, and what can still go wrong with them?¶

    Certificates issued and renewed by a cloud provider for its load balancers and CDNs, such as AWS Certificate Manager with DNS validation. The private key never leaves the provider. Renewal can still fail if the validation DNS record is deleted or a name on the certificate stops resolving.

    28. When should you use a private CA?¶

    For internal services and mutual TLS: internal names should not appear in public CT logs, public CAs cannot validate internal names, and private CAs can issue very short-lived certificates at high volume. Tools include step-ca, Vault PKI, cloud private CAs, cert-manager CA issuers, and service meshes.

    29. Why do certificate expiries still cause outages?¶

    Renewal fails silently (port 80 closed, broken redirects, expired DNS tokens, removed names), renewal succeeds but the server is not reloaded, some certificates are outside the automation, monitoring checks the file rather than the served certificate or only one network path, and teams relied on CA expiry emails that no longer exist.

    30. How should certificate expiry be monitored?¶

    Probe the served certificate from outside, on every hostname and network path, with thresholds scaled to the lifetime (for 90-day certificates, a ticket under 21 days and a page under 7). Also alert directly on renewal failures, keep an inventory with owners, and monitor CT logs for certificates you did not expect.

    31. What is the ACME staging environment for?¶

    Testing issuance and renewal automation without hitting production rate limits. Its certificates are not trusted by browsers, but it exercises the same flow, which makes it ideal for new setups and CI.

    32. Why do CAs validate domain control from several network locations?¶

    An attacker who hijacks one network path (for example with a BGP route leak) could otherwise answer a challenge and obtain a certificate. Multi-perspective validation requires the challenge to succeed from several vantage points, and CA/Browser Forum rules now require it of public CAs.

    TLS termination and troubleshooting¶

    33. Where can TLS be terminated, and how do you choose?¶

    At a CDN, a cloud load balancer, a reverse proxy or ingress, or the application; or passed through to the app by an L4 load balancer. Choose based on where plaintext may exist, who should hold keys, whether you need HTTP-aware routing or a WAF at the edge, and where certificates can be automated.

    34. What is re-encryption, and what makes it secure?¶

    Terminating TLS at the load balancer and opening a new TLS connection to the backend. It is only meaningful if the load balancer verifies the backend certificate (for example proxy_ssl_verify on in nginx or a strict mode at a CDN); without verification it stops passive eavesdropping but not an active attacker.

    35. What does an app need when TLS terminates at a proxy?¶

    The original scheme and client IP from X-Forwarded-Proto and X-Forwarded-For (or Forwarded), and configuration to trust only its proxy for those headers, such as app.set("trust proxy", 1) in Express. Otherwise it sees every request as plain HTTP, causing redirect loops and missing Secure cookies.

    36. What are the key settings in a modern nginx TLS server block?¶

    listen 443 ssl with http2 on, ssl_certificate pointing at the full chain, the private key, ssl_protocols TLSv1.2 TLSv1.3, ECDHE AEAD cipher suites for TLS 1.2, a shared session cache, session tickets off unless keys are rotated, HSTS with always, and a port 80 server that redirects to HTTPS except for the ACME challenge path.

    37. What is mutual TLS, and where is it used?¶

    TLS in which the server requests a certificate from the client and verifies it against a trusted CA, so both sides are authenticated. It is used for service-to-service traffic (often via a service mesh with SPIFFE identities), partner APIs, and devices. It proves identity; authorization is still a separate decision.

    38. Which openssl s_client options matter most when debugging?¶

    -connect host:443, -servername host (send SNI), -showcerts (print the chain), -verify_return_error and -verify_hostname (fail on problems), -tls1_2 or -tls1_3 (test a protocol), -alpn h2, and -brief for a summary. Pipe the output into openssl x509 -noout -dates -ext subjectAltName to decode the leaf.

    39. What do OpenSSL verify errors 10, 18, 19, 20, and 21 mean?¶

    10: certificate has expired. 18: the leaf is self-signed. 19: a self-signed certificate (an untrusted root) is in the chain. 20: unable to get the local issuer certificate, and 21: unable to verify the first certificate, both usually meaning a missing intermediate or an unknown CA.

    40. Why does forgetting SNI cause confusing results?¶

    A server hosting several names chooses the certificate by SNI. Without it, the server returns its default certificate, so you see a hostname mismatch or a different certificate than real clients get.

    41. How do you test one backend server behind a load balancer for TLS problems?¶

    Use curl -v --resolve api.example.com:443:<backend-ip> https://api.example.com/ (or openssl s_client -connect <backend-ip>:443 -servername api.example.com), which connects to that IP while sending the real hostname for SNI and verification.

    42. How do you check that a private key matches a certificate?¶

    Compare the public keys: hash the output of openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -pubkey and of openssl pkey -in key.pem -pubout; the hashes must be identical. This works for RSA and ECDSA keys.

    43. Why is NODE_TLS_REJECT_UNAUTHORIZED=0 dangerous?¶

    It disables certificate verification for every TLS connection in the process, so any attacker on the path can impersonate any server. Fix the real cause instead: serve the full chain, or trust a private CA with NODE_EXTRA_CA_CERTS or the ca option.

    44. How can add_header in nginx silently drop HSTS?¶

    A location block inherits add_header directives from the server level only if it defines none of its own. Adding any header in a location drops the inherited HSTS header there. Repeat security headers in such locations (or use an include), and use always so they appear on error responses too.

    45. How do you rotate a certificate in a Node.js HTTPS server without a restart?¶

    Call server.setSecureContext({ key, cert }) with the new files, for example when a file watcher sees cert-manager update the mounted Secret. New connections use the new certificate; existing connections are unaffected.

    Scenario-based questions¶

    46. Browsers load https://api.example.com, but the mobile app and Node.js clients fail with certificate errors. What do you check?¶

    What to check first: the chain the server sends, with openssl s_client -connect api.example.com:443 -servername api.example.com -showcerts.

    How to confirm: only the leaf appears, and the verify return code is 21 (unable to verify the first certificate); Node.js reports UNABLE_TO_VERIFY_LEAF_SIGNATURE.

    Likely causes and fixes: the server uses cert.pem instead of fullchain.pem; browsers fill the gap from caches, other clients cannot. Configure the full chain and reload. See TLS and certificate fundamentals.

    47. The site went down at midnight with "certificate expired", although certbot is installed. What happened?¶

    What to check first: sudo certbot certificates and the certbot log in /var/log/letsencrypt/.

    How to confirm: either renewals have been failing for weeks (challenge errors), or a renewed certificate exists on disk while openssl s_client shows the old notAfter.

    Likely causes and fixes: a broken challenge (port 80 closed, redirect, DNS change) or a missing deploy hook so nginx never reloaded. Fix the challenge, add --deploy-hook "systemctl reload nginx", and monitor the served certificate with ticket and page thresholds. See obtaining and renewing certificates.

    48. After moving TLS termination from nginx on the VM to a cloud load balancer, the site loops on redirects. Why?¶

    What to check first: what scheme the app thinks requests use, and the X-Forwarded-Proto header arriving at the app.

    How to confirm: app logs show http requests and 301 redirects to https for every request.

    Likely causes and fixes: the app redirects plain HTTP to HTTPS but now receives HTTP from the load balancer and does not trust its forwarded headers. Configure trust proxy for the load balancer, or do the redirect only at the load balancer. See TLS termination and troubleshooting.

    49. A new subdomain v2.api.example.com shows NET::ERR_CERT_COMMON_NAME_INVALID, even though you have a *.example.com certificate. Why?¶

    What to check first: the SAN list of the served certificate.

    How to confirm: it contains *.example.com only, which matches one label, not v2.api.

    Likely causes and fixes: wildcards do not cover deeper subdomains. Issue a certificate that includes v2.api.example.com or *.api.example.com. See TLS and certificate fundamentals.

    50. A cert-manager Certificate has been stuck in "Issuing" for an hour. How do you debug it?¶

    What to check first: kubectl describe on the Certificate, CertificateRequest, Order, and Challenge.

    How to confirm: the Challenge shows a failing self-check (HTTP-01 path not reachable, wrong ingress class, a network policy blocking the solver) or DNS-01 permission or propagation errors.

    Likely causes and fixes: fix the solver configuration or credentials, test against the staging issuer, and alert when Certificates are not Ready. See obtaining and renewing certificates.

    51. Let's Encrypt returns a rate-limit error for a service that restarts often. What is wrong?¶

    What to check first: how often new certificates are issued for the same names, and where the client stores its certificates and account.

    How to confirm: each container start creates a new account or order because the data directory is ephemeral; the limit of 5 certificates for the same set of names in 7 days is hit.

    Likely causes and fixes: persist the ACME data (a volume or a Kubernetes Secret), rely on renewal instead of re-issuance, and use the staging environment for tests. See obtaining and renewing certificates.

    52. Only one VM reports "certificate is not yet valid" for a certificate issued an hour ago. What do you check?¶

    What to check first: the VM's clock (date -u, timedatectl status).

    How to confirm: the clock is behind by hours or days compared with other hosts.

    Likely causes and fixes: NTP is not running or blocked. Enable chrony or systemd-timesyncd, allow NTP traffic, and alert on clock offset. See TLS and certificate fundamentals.

    53. Users behind one corporate network report certificate warnings for your site, but nobody else does. Why?¶

    What to check first: the issuer of the certificate those users see.

    How to confirm: the issuer is the company's proxy CA, not your CA.

    Likely causes and fixes: a TLS-intercepting proxy re-signs traffic and some devices lack the proxy's root, or a pinned client rejects it. This is outside your server; the network's administrators must fix their trust distribution or exempt your domain. See TLS termination and troubleshooting.

    54. A security review finds that the load balancer forwards to backends over HTTPS but accepts any backend certificate. Is that acceptable?¶

    What to check first: the backend TLS verification settings of the load balancer, proxy, or CDN mode.

    How to confirm: proxy_ssl_verify is off (the nginx default), or the CDN uses "Full" rather than "Full (strict)".

    Likely causes and fixes: re-encryption without verification protects only against passive eavesdropping. Issue backend certificates from an internal CA and enable verification, or use a service mesh with mTLS. See TLS termination and troubleshooting.

    55. After rotating the internal CA, one service's mTLS calls fail with "bad certificate". What happened?¶

    What to check first: which CA issued the failing client's certificate and which CAs the server trusts.

    How to confirm: the client has a certificate from the new intermediate, and the server's CA bundle contains only the old one.

    Likely causes and fixes: trust was not distributed before issuance from the new CA. Add new trust anchors first, keep old and new during the overlap, then rotate issuance in stages. See TLS termination and troubleshooting.

    56. Certificate expiry probes are green, yet some users get an expired certificate. How can that be?¶

    What to check first: whether every network path is probed: IPv4 and IPv6, each region or edge location, each load balancer listener.

    How to confirm: probing the failing path directly (for example over IPv6 or from the affected region) shows a different, expired certificate.

    Likely causes and fixes: a separate proxy or edge configuration with its own certificate outside the automation. Probe every path separately and bring it under the same automation. See obtaining and renewing certificates.

    57. HSTS was enabled with includeSubDomains, and an old HTTP-only subdomain is now unreachable in browsers. What do you do?¶

    What to check first: the HSTS header served on the apex domain and the affected subdomain's HTTPS support.

    How to confirm: browsers refuse HTTP for the subdomain and it has no valid HTTPS endpoint.

    Likely causes and fixes: includeSubDomains forces HTTPS everywhere, and browsers cache the policy for max-age. The quick fix is to serve HTTPS on that subdomain; in future, roll out HSTS with a short max-age first and preload only when all subdomains support HTTPS. See TLS and certificate fundamentals.

    58. An internal hostname such as admin.internal.example.com appears in a public certificate search. Is that a problem?¶

    What to check first: crt.sh results for your domain and which team requested the certificate.

    How to confirm: a publicly trusted certificate lists the internal name.

    Likely causes and fixes: internal services were given public certificates, which are logged in Certificate Transparency. Treat the names as public, move internal services to a private CA or a wildcard, and keep monitoring CT for unexpected certificates. See TLS and certificate fundamentals.
