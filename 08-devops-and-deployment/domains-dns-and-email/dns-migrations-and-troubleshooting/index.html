    DNS Migrations and Troubleshooting¶

    Sooner or later TaskFlow moves: the API to a new load balancer, the web app to another platform, or the whole zone from the registrar's free DNS to Route 53 or Cloudflare. This lesson turns DNS caching into a runbook for changing records with zero downtime, moving a zone to a new provider without losing mail or certificates, and handling DNSSEC during a move. It then covers the tools for finding out what DNS is really answering (dig +trace, asking authoritative servers directly, comparing public resolvers) and a troubleshooting flow for the failures you will meet.

    Record types and TTL basics are covered in domains and DNS records; moving traffic between regions with health-checked DNS is covered in global traffic management.

    Why it matters¶

    DNS changes are rare, so teams are out of practice, and they are unforgiving: a wrong answer is cached by thousands of resolvers you do not control, for as long as the TTL you set. A nameserver change that forgets the MX records breaks inbound mail for days; a DNSSEC key that does not match its DS record makes the whole domain unresolvable for validating resolvers; and because the .com delegation is cached for two days, there is no quick undo. The outages below show that when DNS fails, everything behind it fails at once, including the tools you would use to fix it.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Meta (October 2021). In More details about the October 4 outage, Meta explains that when its backbone failed, its DNS servers withdrew their BGP route announcements because they could not reach the data centers. "The end result was that our DNS servers became unreachable even though they were still operational," which took every Meta service off the internet and slowed the recovery itself.
    • AWS (October 2025). The summary of the DynamoDB disruption in us-east-1 describes a race condition in DynamoDB's DNS automation that left the regional endpoint with an empty DNS record, so clients could not connect. DNS was restored in under three hours, but cached answers had to expire before everyone recovered, and AWS disabled the automation worldwide until it was fixed.
    • Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 (July 2025). The incident report explains how a configuration error withdrew the resolver's prefixes for 62 minutes. Users whose devices relied only on 1.1.1.1 lost all name resolution: a reminder that resolvers are dependencies too, and that checking from several resolvers is part of every diagnosis.
    • Route 53 zone import. The Route 53 guide on importing a zone file lists the rules that trip up migrations: at most 1,000 records per import, the SOA and apex NS records are ignored, names without a trailing dot get the zone appended (creating mail.example.com.example.com), and Route 53 cannot export zone files.

    Popular tools and providers: dig, delv, kdig, drill, nslookup and Resolve-DnsName, DNSViz, DNSControl and OctoDNS for DNS as code, Terraform, and the cache flush pages of Google Public DNS and Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1.

    How it works¶

    "Propagation" is cache expiry¶

    Nothing is pushed anywhere when you change a record. The authoritative servers serve the new data within seconds; every recursive resolver keeps its cached copy until that copy's TTL runs out, then asks again. Because each resolver cached the record at a different moment, users see a mix of old and new answers until the last cached copy expires. Several caches are involved:

    Cache Controlled by Typical lifetime
    Record answers in recursive resolvers The record's TTL Your TTL, sometimes clamped by the resolver
    "Does not exist" answers SOA TTL and minimum (RFC 2308) Often minutes to an hour
    The delegation (NS records at the parent) The TLD registry 172,800 s (2 days) for .com
    DS records at the parent The TLD registry 86,400 s (1 day) for .com
    Operating system and browser caches OS and browser Short, but they exist
    Application caches The runtime and your code Varies; long-lived processes may keep connections to old IPs

    Two consequences shape every runbook. A low TTL only helps if it was lowered at least one old TTL before the change. And a nameserver change cannot be rolled back quickly: whatever you publish at the registry, many resolvers keep using the previous delegation for up to two days.

    Changing a record with zero downtime¶

    1. Lower the TTL early. If the record's TTL is 86400, lower it to 300 at least a day before the change (better two). Until the old TTL has passed, some resolvers still hold the long-lived copy.
    2. Prepare the new target fully. It must serve the same hostname: certificate, virtual host or alternate domain name, firewall rules, and capacity.
    3. Test it before DNS points at it. curl --resolve sends a real request with the real hostname (and SNI) to the new address.
    4. Change the record, ideally through code review.
    5. Run old and new in parallel. For at least one new TTL, and in practice until the old target's traffic reaches zero, both serve users. The old target must keep working (and keep its certificate).
    6. Verify on the authoritative servers, on several public resolvers, over IPv4 and IPv6, and in your monitoring.
    7. Raise the TTL again, then decommission the old target (and remove any DNS records that still point at it).

    Moving a zone to a new DNS provider¶

    Changing nameservers moves every record at once, so the work is in making the new provider an exact copy before anyone notices.

    1. Inventory everything. Export a zone file where the old provider offers one, or list records through its API (aws route53 list-resource-record-sets for Route 53, which cannot export zone files). Include the records nobody remembers: DKIM selectors, the DMARC record, platform verification tokens, ACM validation CNAMEs, CAA, SRV, wildcards, and NS records that delegate subdomains to other providers.
    2. Translate provider-specific records. Route 53 alias records, Cloudflare proxied records, and flattened CNAMEs have no standard zone file form. Recreate them with the new provider's equivalent.
    3. Import and compare. Query the new provider's nameservers directly (dig @<new-ns> ... +norecurse) for every name and type and diff against the old ones (a script follows below).
    4. Handle DNSSEC first. Either remove the DS record at the registrar and wait out the DS record's TTL, a day or more, before the move (the simple path; Route 53's domain-transfer guide asks for 24 hours), or use a multi-signer setup (RFC 8901) in which both providers' keys are published. Never change nameservers while a DS record points at keys the new provider does not have.
    5. Change the nameservers at the registrar.
    6. Keep the old zone serving identical data for at least two days (the .com delegation TTL) plus a margin; a week is common. Freeze changes, or apply each change to both providers, which DNS as code makes easy.
    7. Verify and finish. dig +trace shows the new nameservers, query counts at the old provider fall to zero, the new provider signs the zone and you publish its DS, then delete the old zone.

    Moving the registration to another registrar is a separate operation that does not need to touch DNS; see domains and DNS records for its rules.

    Asking the right server¶

    Most DNS confusion comes from asking the wrong question. Separate three:

    Question Command Tells you
    What does my zone say? dig @ns1.dns-host.example.net api.example.com +norecurse The authoritative truth, no caches involved
    Where does the world look for my zone? dig +trace api.example.com The delegation at every level, from the root down
    What are users getting right now? dig @1.1.1.1 api.example.com (and 8.8.8.8, 9.9.9.9, your office resolver) Cached answers and the TTL they have left

    Supporting tools:

    • dig +dnssec shows signatures; dig +cd (checking disabled) asks a validating resolver to skip validation. An answer that fails normally but works with +cd is a DNSSEC problem. delv validates locally and explains why a chain fails, and DNSViz draws the chain.
    • dig -x 198.51.100.20 checks reverse DNS (PTR).
    • On Windows, Resolve-DnsName -Server 8.8.8.8 -Name api.example.com and nslookup api.example.com 8.8.8.8 do the same jobs; ipconfig /flushdns clears the local cache. On Linux with systemd-resolved, resolvectl flush-caches; on macOS, sudo dscacheutil -flushcache; sudo killall -HUP mDNSResponder.
    • Google Public DNS and Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1 each have a page to flush a name from their own cache. That fixes those resolvers only; every other resolver still waits for its TTL.

    A troubleshooting flow¶

    Work from the source outward: first prove the zone is right, then the delegation, then DNSSEC, then caches. Only when all four are clean is the problem somewhere else; the troubleshooting lesson for Linux servers covers the network checks that come next.

    Failure patterns¶

    Symptom Usual cause How to confirm
    Mail stops after a nameserver change MX, SPF, or DKIM records were not copied dig @<new-ns> example.com MX +norecurse is empty
    The site works but certificate renewal fails CAA does not list the CA, or an ACM validation CNAME was deleted dig example.com CAA; the CA or ACM error message
    The console refuses a record, or mail breaks after adding one A CNAME at the apex or next to other records The record list at that name
    A record exists but nobody can find it The zone name was appended: _dmarc.example.com.example.com dig the doubled name
    SERVFAIL for some users only DNSSEC: DS and keys disagree dig +cd works; DNSViz shows the break
    A new subdomain is NXDOMAIN for some users Negative caching of a lookup made before the record existed The SOA minimum and TTL; it clears by itself
    Half the answers old, half new, for days Two delegated nameserver sets with different data dig @ each NS listed in dig +trace
    Some clients use the old IP for hours Long-lived processes or connection pools that resolved once Client logs; restart or add connection max age

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Change Approach
    Point one name at a new target Lower the TTL early, test with curl --resolve, change, run old and new in parallel
    Move a zone to another DNS provider Full migration: inventory, translate, compare, DNSSEC first, keep both zones identical for the delegation TTL
    Move only the registration Registrar transfer; DNS does not change (move DNS first if the old registrar hosts it)
    Survive a DNS provider outage Two providers serving the same zone at all times, kept in sync by DNS as code, prepared in advance
    Fail over between regions automatically Health-checked DNS routing, covered in global traffic management

    When not to:

    • Do not keep very low TTLs permanently "just in case". They add query volume and cost, and make your DNS provider's outages hit users sooner.
    • Do not plan to "switch nameservers" during an outage: the delegation is cached for days. Redundant DNS must already be in place.
    • Do not change nameservers, DNSSEC, and records in the same step; each one has its own cache and its own rollback.

    Practical examples¶

    Testing the new target before the switch¶

    # Send a real request for api.example.com to the NEW load balancer's address
curl -sv --resolve api.example.com:443:198.51.100.40 \
  https://api.example.com/healthz -o /dev/null 2>&1 \
  | grep -E 'subject:|subjectAltName|HTTP/'

    --resolve host:port:address overrides DNS for that one request, so TLS uses the real server name and the request carries the real Host header. A certificate or virtual host problem shows up here, not in front of users.

    Walking the delegation with dig +trace¶

    ; Example output of: dig +trace www.example.com (trimmed, documentation names)
.                    518400  IN  NS     a.root-servers.net.
com.                 172800  IN  NS     a.gtld-servers.net.
example.com.         172800  IN  NS     ns1.dns-host.example.net.
example.com.         172800  IN  NS     ns2.dns-host.example.net.
www.example.com.     300     IN  CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.

    Read it top to bottom: the root refers the resolver to the .com servers, the .com servers refer it to your nameservers (this is the delegation, cached for two days), and your nameservers answer. During a nameserver migration the third block is what changes; if it still lists the old provider, the registrar change has not happened or has not reached the registry.

    What are different resolvers seeing?¶

    for r in 1.1.1.1 8.8.8.8 9.9.9.9; do
  printf '%-8s ' "$r"
  dig +noall +answer @"$r" api.example.com A | awk '{print "ttl=" $2, $5}'
done

    ; Example output a few minutes after a change
1.1.1.1  ttl=212 198.51.100.40
8.8.8.8  ttl=41 198.51.100.20
9.9.9.9  ttl=287 198.51.100.40

    One resolver still serves the old address with 41 seconds left; it will switch on its own. A remaining TTL far above the TTL you set means a record or a resolver you did not expect is involved.

    Comparing two providers before a cutover¶

    #!/usr/bin/env bash
# scripts/compare-zones.sh: diff answers from the old and new nameservers
# Usage: scripts/compare-zones.sh names.txt   (one fully qualified name per line)
set -euo pipefail
OLD_NS="ns1.old-dns.example.net"
NEW_NS="ns1.new-dns.example.net"
TYPES=(A AAAA CNAME MX TXT CAA SRV)   # apex NS and SOA differ by design

status=0
while read -r name; do
  [[ -z "$name" || "$name" == \#* ]] && continue
  for type in "${TYPES[@]}"; do
    old=$(dig +short +norecurse @"$OLD_NS" "$name" "$type" | sort)
    new=$(dig +short +norecurse @"$NEW_NS" "$name" "$type" | sort)
    if [[ "$old" != "$new" ]]; then
      printf 'DIFF %s %s\n  old: %s\n  new: %s\n' "$name" "$type" "${old:-<none>}" "${new:-<none>}"
      status=1
    fi
  done
done < "${1:?names file required}"
exit "$status"

    Build names.txt from the old provider's export, including underscore names such as _dmarc.example.com and s1._domainkey.example.com. Expect real differences only where a provider-specific feature answers differently by design (a proxied record returns the proxy's addresses); every other DIFF is a missing or wrong record.

    One source of truth for both providers (DNSControl)¶

    During the overlap, DNSControl can push the same records to the old and the new provider. A SERVICE() with 0 as its second argument manages records without listing that provider's nameservers in the delegation.

    // dns/dnsconfig.js: the same zone pushed to the old and the new provider
D("example.com", REGISTRAR("none"),
  SERVICE("old_dns"),     // currently delegated: serves real traffic
  SERVICE("new_dns", 0),  // kept identical, not delegated yet
  DefaultTTL(300),
  A("api", "198.51.100.20"),
  AAAA("api", "2001:db8::20"),
  CNAME("www", "taskflow-web.cdn.example.net."),
  MX("@", 10, "mx1.mail.example.net."),
  MX("@", 20, "mx2.mail.example.net."),
  TXT("@", "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all"),
  TXT("_dmarc", "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"),
  CAA("@", "issue", "letsencrypt.org"),
);

    {
  "none": { "TYPE": "NONE" },
  "old_dns": {
    "TYPE": "ROUTE53",
    "KeyId": "$AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID",
    "SecretKey": "$AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY",
    "Token": "$AWS_SESSION_TOKEN"
  },
  "new_dns": {
    "TYPE": "CLOUDFLAREAPI",
    "accountid": "$CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID",
    "apitoken": "$CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN"
  }
}

    The second file is dns/creds.json; values starting with $ are read from environment variables, so no secret is committed. dnscontrol preview prints the changes for each provider and dnscontrol push applies them. On the cutover day you change the nameservers at the registrar, swap the 0 to the old provider's SERVICE(), and after the overlap remove it.

    Running DNS changes through CI¶

    # .github/workflows/dns.yml
name: DNS

on:
  pull_request:
    paths: ["dns/**"]
  push:
    branches: [main]
    paths: ["dns/**"]

permissions:
  contents: read
  id-token: write   # OIDC for AWS

concurrency:
  group: dns
  cancel-in-progress: false

jobs:
  dns:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v7

      - uses: aws-actions/configure-aws-credentials@v6
        with:
          role-to-assume: arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/taskflow-dns
          aws-region: us-east-1

      - name: Preview DNS changes
        run: |
          docker run --rm -v "$PWD/dns:/dns" \
            -e AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID -e AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY -e AWS_SESSION_TOKEN \
            -e CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID -e CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN \
            ghcr.io/dnscontrol/dnscontrol preview
        env:
          CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID: ${{ vars.CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID }}
          CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN: ${{ secrets.CLOUDFLARE_DNS_TOKEN }}

      - name: Push DNS changes
        if: github.event_name == 'push'
        run: |
          docker run --rm -v "$PWD/dns:/dns" \
            -e AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID -e AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY -e AWS_SESSION_TOKEN \
            -e CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID -e CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN \
            ghcr.io/dnscontrol/dnscontrol push
        env:
          CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID: ${{ vars.CLOUDFLARE_ACCOUNT_ID }}
          CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN: ${{ secrets.CLOUDFLARE_DNS_TOKEN }}
    • Pull requests get a preview, so reviewers see exactly which records change at each provider; only merges to main push.
    • AWS credentials come from OIDC (configure-aws-credentials exports short-lived keys as environment variables); the Cloudflare token is scoped to DNS edits on this zone. Short-lived credentials and OIDC are covered in pipeline and supply chain security.
    • concurrency stops two pushes from racing each other.
    • The image is shown untagged, as in DNSControl's documentation; in a real pipeline pin it to a release tag or digest so a new DNSControl version cannot change behaviour unreviewed.

    Best practices¶

    • Manage zones as code and review every change; keep an export of each zone outside the provider.
    • Lower TTLs at least one old TTL before planned changes, and raise them afterwards.
    • Test new targets with curl --resolve before changing DNS.
    • Keep old targets serving until their traffic is gone.
    • For nameserver moves: inventory, translate, diff, handle DNSSEC first, and keep both zones identical for at least the delegation TTL.
    • Diagnose from the source outward: authoritative answer, delegation, DNSSEC, caches, then other layers.
    • Check from several resolvers and over IPv4 and IPv6.
    • Prepare secondary DNS before you need it; it cannot be added in the middle of an outage.

    In production¶

    • Inbound mail disappears after moving DNS to a new provider. Detect: senders' bounces or a drop in received mail; dig @<new-ns> example.com MX +norecurse returns nothing. Cause: only web records were copied before the nameserver change. Fix: add the MX, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records at the new provider immediately; diff every name and type before future cutovers.
    • The domain returns SERVFAIL for many users right after a move. Detect: validating resolvers fail, dig +cd succeeds, DNSViz shows a DS without a matching DNSKEY. Cause: the DS record at the registrar still points at the old provider's key. Fix: remove the DS (or add the new provider's key) and wait for the cached DS to expire; next time, remove DS a day before the move.
    • Half the users get old answers days after an edit. Detect: dig +trace lists nameservers from two providers, and they answer differently. Cause: the old provider's zone is still delegated (or cached as delegated) and was not updated. Fix: apply the change to both providers until the old delegation has expired everywhere.
    • The cutover went fine, but the API still gets traffic on the old load balancer. Detect: the old target's request count stays above zero long after the TTL. Cause: clients with connection pools or DNS caches that resolved once (long-running workers, mobile apps). Fix: keep the old target up longer, set a maximum connection age on clients, and restart long-lived workers.
    • A freshly created subdomain is NXDOMAIN for some people. Detect: authoritative servers answer, some resolvers say the name does not exist. Cause: those resolvers looked it up before it existed and cached the negative answer. Fix: wait out the negative TTL (the SOA minimum), flush the public resolvers you can, and create records before announcing them.
    • Certificate renewals fail after the DNS move. Detect: ACM shows renewal pending, or the ACME client reports a CAA or validation error. Cause: the ACM validation CNAME or a CAA entry was left behind. Fix: restore the record, and include underscore names in every inventory.

    Important terminology¶

    • Propagation: The informal name for resolvers' caches expiring at different times after a change.
    • Delegation TTL: How long resolvers cache the parent's NS records for your zone (two days at .com).
    • Negative caching: Caching the answer that a name does not exist.
    • Authoritative answer: An answer from a server that holds the zone, marked with the aa flag in dig output.
    • Lame delegation: A nameserver listed in the delegation that does not actually serve the zone.
    • dig +trace: Resolving a name step by step from the root, showing each delegation.
    • --resolve (curl): Overriding DNS for one request to test a target with the real hostname.
    • Multi-signer DNSSEC: Two providers serving one signed zone with both sets of keys published (RFC 8901).
    • DNS as code: Managing zones in version-controlled files applied by tools such as DNSControl, OctoDNS, or Terraform.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Believing "DNS propagation takes 48 hours" for every change; ordinary record changes take one TTL, while nameserver changes are bounded by the delegation TTL.
    • Lowering the TTL at the same moment as the change.
    • Copying only web records when changing nameservers, forgetting MX, TXT, DKIM, CAA, and validation records.
    • Changing nameservers while a DS record points at the old provider's keys.
    • Testing only from one's own laptop, whose resolver may have been flushed or may have just cached the new answer.
    • Shutting down the old target as soon as DNS is changed.
    • Expecting to switch DNS providers quickly during an outage.

    Revision questions¶

    1. Why is "propagation" a misleading word? What actually happens after you change a record?
    2. A record has a TTL of 86400. You plan to change it on Thursday at 10:00. When must you lower the TTL, and to what? (By Wednesday 10:00 at the latest, so every cached copy of the long TTL has expired; 300 seconds is typical.)
    3. Why must the old zone stay identical for about two days after a .com nameserver change, and what sets that number?
    4. Describe the safe order of steps for moving a DNSSEC-signed zone to a new provider.
    5. Which three questions separate "zone wrong", "delegation wrong", and "caches stale", and which commands answer each?
    6. A domain fails with SERVFAIL on 8.8.8.8 but resolves with dig +cd. What do you suspect, and what tool shows the broken link?
    7. Why test a new target with curl --resolve rather than by editing DNS and waiting?
    8. Your DNS provider is down. Why can you not simply switch the nameservers to another provider, and what should have been prepared?
