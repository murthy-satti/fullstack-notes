    Domains, DNS and Email Interview Questions and Answers¶

    These questions review domains and DNS records, connecting domains to hosting, DNS migrations and troubleshooting, CDN setup and edge caching, and email deliverability with SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. Try answering each one before reading the answer.

    Domains and DNS records¶

    1. What is the difference between a registrar, a registry, and a DNS host?¶

    The registry operates a top-level domain: its database of names and the TLD's nameservers. The registrar is the company you pay; it registers and renews the name with the registry and sends it your nameservers and DS records. The DNS host runs the authoritative nameservers that serve your zone's records. One company can be registrar and DNS host, but the roles stay separate.

    2. What is a delegation, and where does it live?¶

    The NS records for your domain in the parent zone (for example.com, in the .com zone), plus glue addresses when the nameservers are inside the zone. It tells resolvers which servers are authoritative. The .com servers publish it with a 172,800-second (two-day) TTL, which is why nameserver changes settle slowly.

    3. What replaced WHOIS, and why does registration data matter to operators?¶

    RDAP, which returns registration data as JSON over HTTPS; since 28 January 2025, gTLD registries and registrars must provide RDAP and are mostly no longer required to run WHOIS. Operators use it to monitor expiry dates and lock statuses, and must keep the registrant email real, because renewal notices, transfer approvals, and verification requests go there.

    4. What happens when a generic TLD domain expires?¶

    The registrar must send notices about a month and a week before expiry. After expiry it interrupts DNS (often showing a parking page) while you can still renew. Once deleted, the name enters a 30-day redemption grace period with DNS off, where only the previous registrant can restore it, usually for a fee. After a short pending-delete stage anyone can register it.

    5. How do you protect a production domain from hijacking or accidental changes?¶

    Auto-renew with a valid payment method, a registrar transfer lock (clientTransferProhibited, plus update and delete locks), a registry lock for the most important domain, MFA on registrar and DNS accounts, zone-scoped API tokens, and alerts on expiry dates, NS and DS changes, and Certificate Transparency entries for your names.

    6. What are the rules of a CNAME record?¶

    A name with a CNAME can hold no other records, so the zone apex (which needs SOA and NS) can never be a CNAME. MX and NS targets must not be aliases. A CNAME applies to every query type, including TXT lookups. Each hop in a chain can cost another lookup.

    7. How do you point the apex at a CDN or hosting platform?¶

    With a provider feature that resolves the target on the authoritative side: a Route 53 alias record, Cloudflare's CNAME flattening (automatic at the apex), or an ALIAS/ANAME record at other hosts. Alternatives are the platform's documented static or anycast IPs, or making www the canonical host and redirecting the apex to it.

    8. What is the trade-off of CNAME flattening and alias records?¶

    The DNS host resolves the target from its own servers, so a platform that steers users by DNS sees the DNS host's location rather than the user's resolver. That is why Netlify recommends a subdomain as the primary domain with external DNS, and Vercel recommends www with a CNAME.

    9. What should a domain that never sends or receives email publish?¶

    A null MX (MX 0 ., RFC 7505) so senders fail fast, v=spf1 -all so no server is authorized to send, and _dmarc with p=reject so receivers reject mail claiming to be from it. example.com itself publishes exactly these records.

    10. What does a CAA record do, and how does a CA find the relevant one?¶

    It lists the certificate authorities allowed to issue for a name (issue, issuewild, iodef). The CA looks at the exact name, then climbs toward the root and uses the first name that has CAA records. No CAA anywhere means any CA may issue; issue ";" means none may. Each CA has an identifier, such as letsencrypt.org or amazon.com for ACM.

    example.com.  CAA  0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  CAA  0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"

    11. What is negative caching?¶

    Resolvers cache the answer that a name or record does not exist. Per RFC 2308 the duration is the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and the SOA minimum field. A record created after someone looked it up can stay invisible to that resolver until the negative entry expires.

    12. How does TTL affect a change? Give an example.¶

    A resolver may answer from cache until the TTL expires. With a TTL of 3600, a resolver that cached the record at 09:59:30 serves the old value until 10:59:30 even if you change it at 10:00. Lowering the TTL helps only if done at least one old TTL before the change.

    13. What does DNSSEC protect, and what is its main operational risk?¶

    It lets validating resolvers verify, through a chain of DS and DNSKEY records from the root, that answers came from the zone owner unaltered. It does not encrypt queries. The risk is a broken chain: if the DS at the parent does not match the zone's key, validating resolvers return SERVFAIL, and the cached DS (a day at .com) makes the outage outlive the fix.

    14. What is an SRV record, and where might a Node.js developer meet one?¶

    It publishes priority, weight, port, and target host for a service under _service._proto.name. MongoDB's mongodb+srv:// connection strings make the driver look up _mongodb._tcp.<host> SRV records for the members and a TXT record for options such as replicaSet, and turn TLS on by default.

    Connecting domains to hosting¶

    15. Why do platforms recommend www as the canonical host?¶

    www can be a plain CNAME, so the platform controls where it points and can steer traffic by DNS for performance and DDoS mitigation; the apex needs an A record or flattening. The apex then redirects to www with a permanent redirect that browsers cache.

    16. How do you attach a subdomain and the apex to Vercel or Netlify?¶

    Subdomain: a CNAME to the target the dashboard shows (a project-specific Vercel DNS name, or the site's netlify.app name). Apex: the A record value Vercel shows or its nameservers; for Netlify an ALIAS, ANAME, or flattened CNAME to apex-loadbalancer.netlify.com, or an A record as a fallback. Always copy values from the dashboard.

    17. What does CloudFront need before it serves a custom domain?¶

    The name must be an alternate domain name on exactly one distribution, covered by a trusted certificate (an ACM certificate in us-east-1), and DNS must point at the distribution: a CNAME for subdomains, or Route 53 alias A and AAAA records, including at the apex.

    18. Why should you never create A records with a load balancer's IP addresses?¶

    Managed load balancers scale and replace nodes, so their IPs change; AWS load balancers publish their DNS name with a 60-second TTL for exactly this reason. Use an alias record or a CNAME to the load balancer's DNS name.

    19. What are verification records, and why must you keep them?¶

    TXT or CNAME records that prove domain control to a platform or CA, such as Vercel's _vercel TXT, Azure's asuid TXT, or ACM's validation CNAMEs. Deleting them can block later renewals (ACM stops renewing when its CNAME disappears) or let another account claim the domain. Record which service owns each one.

    20. How does a Route 53 alias record differ from a CNAME?¶

    It can sit at the apex, answers with the current addresses of an AWS resource (so it looks like an A or AAAA record to clients), only matches the record type you created, is free for queries to AWS resources, and takes its TTL from the target instead of your setting.

    21. What changes when a Cloudflare record is switched from DNS-only to proxied?¶

    DNS answers with Cloudflare anycast IPs and an automatic 300-second TTL, TLS for visitors ends at Cloudflare with an edge certificate, caching, WAF, and DDoS rules apply, the origin sees Cloudflare's addresses (the visitor's is in CF-Connecting-IP), and only HTTP and HTTPS are carried. Only A, AAAA, and CNAME records can be proxied; MX and TXT stay DNS-only.

    22. What causes a redirect loop after enabling Cloudflare, and how do you fix it?¶

    Flexible mode connects to the origin over plain HTTP; if the origin redirects HTTP to HTTPS, every request loops. Switch to Full (strict) with a valid origin certificate, so Cloudflare connects over HTTPS and validates the origin.

    23. How do DNS wildcards and TLS wildcards differ?¶

    A DNS wildcard (*.preview.example.com) answers for any name below it that does not exist, even several labels deep, but not for names that exist, including names that exist only because something below them has records. A TLS wildcard certificate covers exactly one label: pr-7.preview.example.com, not api.pr-7.preview.example.com.

    24. What is subdomain takeover, and how do you prevent it?¶

    A DNS record (usually a CNAME) points at a cloud resource that was deleted; an attacker creates a resource with the same provider name and serves content, with a valid certificate, on your subdomain. Prevent it by removing DNS records before deleting resources, keeping verification records, and regularly checking that every CNAME target still exists and belongs to you.

    25. In what order should example.com redirect when you use HSTS preload?¶

    http://example.com first redirects to https://example.com on the same host, which serves the HSTS header (max-age of at least a year, includeSubDomains, preload), and only then redirects to https://www.example.com. Preloading forces HTTPS on every subdomain, so audit them first.

    DNS migrations and troubleshooting¶

    26. What does "DNS propagation" actually mean?¶

    Nothing is pushed. The authoritative servers serve a change within seconds; each resolver keeps its cached copy until that copy's TTL expires, at different moments, so users see a mix of old and new answers for up to one TTL (or up to the delegation TTL for nameserver changes).

    27. How do you change a record with zero downtime?¶

    Lower the TTL at least one old TTL ahead, prepare the new target fully, test it with curl --resolve using the real hostname, change the record, keep the old target serving until its traffic is gone, verify from several resolvers over IPv4 and IPv6, then raise the TTL and decommission the old target.

    28. How do you move a zone to a new DNS provider safely?¶

    Inventory every record (including MX, DKIM, DMARC, verification, CAA, SRV, and subdomain delegations), translate provider-specific records such as aliases, import, diff both providers' answers name by name, handle DNSSEC first, change the nameservers at the registrar, and keep both zones identical for at least the delegation TTL before deleting the old one.

    29. Why can't you quickly roll back a .com nameserver change?¶

    Resolvers cache the delegation from the .com servers for up to two days (172,800 seconds), and you cannot shorten that TTL. Whatever you publish at the registry, some resolvers keep using the previous nameservers until their cached delegation expires.

    30. How do you handle DNSSEC when changing DNS providers?¶

    Either remove the DS record at the registrar and wait at least a day for cached copies to expire before moving (then sign at the new provider and publish its DS), or use a multi-signer setup (RFC 8901) in which both providers' keys are published. Never move while the DS points at keys the new provider does not have.

    31. How do you tell whether a problem is in the zone, the delegation, or caches?¶

    Ask the authoritative server directly (dig @ns1... name +norecurse) to check the zone, walk the delegation with dig +trace, and query several public resolvers (dig @1.1.1.1, @8.8.8.8) to see cached answers and their remaining TTL. Work outward from the zone.

    32. What does it mean when dig returns SERVFAIL but dig +cd works?¶

    +cd asks the resolver to skip DNSSEC validation. If the answer only works without validation, the DNSSEC chain is broken, typically a DS that does not match the zone's keys. delv and DNSViz show where the chain breaks.

    33. How do you test a new server before DNS points at it?¶

    curl --resolve api.example.com:443:198.51.100.40 https://api.example.com/healthz sends a real request to the new address with the real hostname in SNI and Host, so certificate and virtual host problems appear before users are switched.

    34. How does DNS as code help a migration?¶

    The zone lives in version control and changes go through review. Tools like DNSControl can push the same records to the old and new providers during the overlap (SERVICE("new_dns", 0) manages records without delegating), and dnscontrol preview in CI shows each provider's changes before push.

    CDN setup and edge caching¶

    35. What is a cache key, and what belongs in it?¶

    The request properties that decide whether two requests share a cached object. Include every input that changes the response (path, meaningful query parameters, headers the response really varies by) and nothing else: drop tracking parameters and high-variety headers, and never key shared content on session cookies or Authorization.

    36. What is the difference between CloudFront's cache policy and origin request policy?¶

    The cache policy defines what goes into the cache key; the origin request policy defines extra values forwarded to the origin without being keyed. If the origin varies its response on a value that is forwarded but not keyed, CloudFront serves the first variant to everyone.

    37. Why can a policy with a minimum TTL above zero leak private data?¶

    CloudFront caches for at least the minimum TTL even when the origin sends no-cache, no-store, or private; the managed CachingOptimized policy has a one-second minimum. Attached to personalized API paths, it can serve one user's response to another. Use CachingDisabled there.

    38. What does an origin shield or tiered cache do? Give the arithmetic.¶

    It adds a mid-tier cache near the origin that collapses misses from many edge locations. With a 90% edge hit ratio and an 80% shield hit ratio on the misses, the origin sees 0.10 × 0.20 = 2% of requests. CloudFront Origin Shield, Cloudflare Tiered Cache, Fastly shielding, Akamai Tiered Distribution, and Bunny Origin Shield all do this.

    39. How should you invalidate cached content?¶

    Prefer versioned (hashed) URLs so only entry points need invalidating. Purge groups by tag (Cloudflare Cache-Tag, Fastly Surrogate-Key, CloudFront cache tags) when content changes, avoid purge-everything, which turns every edge cold at once, and remember purges never reach browsers.

    40. How do you protect an origin that sits behind a CDN?¶

    Use CloudFront origin access control for S3, allow only the CDN's published ranges at the firewall or load balancer, require a secret header that only the CDN adds, or connect through a tunnel. Otherwise attackers bypass the cache and the WAF by hitting the origin directly.

    41. How do you put an API behind a CDN safely?¶

    Disable caching for everything except explicitly public GET endpoints, forward Authorization, cookies, and query strings, cache public responses with short s-maxage, never cache responses that depend on the caller, check body size and timeout limits, and read the client IP from the CDN's header.

    42. When would you use CloudFront Functions instead of Lambda@Edge?¶

    CloudFront Functions run sub-millisecond JavaScript on viewer requests and responses only, without network access, at very high scale: redirects, header changes, cache key normalization, token checks. Lambda@Edge (Node.js or Python) adds origin-side events, network and body access, and longer execution for heavier logic.

    43. How do you keep edge image transformations under control?¶

    Allow only a small set of widths and formats, reject others at the edge, and include the allowed parameters in the cache key. Free-form parameters let anyone create unlimited variants, and services such as Cloudflare bill each unique transformation per month.

    44. What do Cloudflare's DYNAMIC and BYPASS cache statuses mean?¶

    DYNAMIC means the request was never eligible for caching under the current rules, so it went straight to the origin. BYPASS means it was eligible, but the origin's response was not cacheable (for example because of its Cache-Control header).

    Email deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC¶

    45. Why does application email land in spam?¶

    Mailbox providers decide on authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC with alignment) and reputation: complaints, bounces, spam traps, engagement, and the history of the domain and IPs. Authentication is required to be eligible for the inbox; reputation decides the folder. Google asks for a spam rate below 0.1% and never reaching 0.3%.

    46. Why separate transactional and marketing email?¶

    Marketing draws more complaints. Sending it from its own subdomain and provider stream or IP pool keeps a campaign's complaints from pushing password resets into spam. Note that Gmail still counts subdomains toward the primary domain for its bulk-sender threshold.

    47. What does SPF check, and what is the 10-lookup limit?¶

    SPF checks the envelope MAIL FROM (Return-Path) domain against the servers listed in its TXT record, not the visible From. RFC 7208 limits an evaluation to 10 lookups from include, a, mx, ptr, exists, and redirect, including nested includes; ip4, ip6, and all are free. Exceeding it is a permerror, so SPF fails for every message.

    48. How does DKIM work, and how are its keys rotated?¶

    The sender signs selected headers and a body hash with a private key and adds a DKIM-Signature with d= (domain) and s= (selector); the receiver fetches the public key from <selector>._domainkey.<domain>. Keys should be 2048-bit RSA (RFC 8301 requires at least 1024 and forbids rsa-sha1). Rotate by publishing a new selector, switching signing, and removing the old key later.

    49. What does a message need to pass DMARC?¶

    SPF or DKIM must pass, and the passing domain must align with the header From domain: in relaxed mode (the default) it must share the organizational domain, in strict mode it must match exactly. The policy (p=none, quarantine, reject) says what to do when neither aligned check passes.

    50. Why can SPF and DKIM both pass while DMARC fails?¶

    Because they passed for someone else's domain: the provider's default Return-Path (for example amazonses.com) and DKIM signed with the provider's domain. Neither aligns with example.com. Fix it with custom DKIM for your domain and a custom MAIL FROM or Return-Path subdomain.

    51. How do you roll out DMARC safely?¶

    Publish p=none with rua, read aggregate reports for a few weeks, inventory and fix every legitimate sender (aligned DKIM, custom Return-Path, or its own subdomain), then move to quarantine and later reject, optionally with t=y first. Advance only when reports are clean, and keep reading them afterwards.

    52. What changed when DMARC was updated in 2026?¶

    RFC 9989 (May 2026) put DMARC on the Standards Track, obsoleting RFC 7489 and RFC 9091, and moved reporting to RFC 9990 and RFC 9991. It removed pct, rf, and ri, added t (test mode, which applies the next weaker policy), np, and psd, and replaced the Public Suffix List with a DNS tree walk for finding the organizational domain. Receivers still on RFC 7489 ignore the new tags.

    53. What do Gmail, Yahoo, and Outlook.com require from bulk senders?¶

    Gmail (close to 5,000+ messages a day to personal accounts, a permanent status) and Yahoo require SPF and DKIM, DMARC of at least p=none with alignment, one-click unsubscribe honored within about two days, and a spam rate below 0.3%. Outlook.com requires SPF and DKIM to pass and an aligned DMARC record for domains above 5,000 messages a day, enforced since May 5, 2025 with 550 5.7.515 rejections.

    54. What is one-click unsubscribe, and how do you implement it?¶

    RFC 8058: the message carries List-Unsubscribe with an HTTPS URL and List-Unsubscribe-Post: List-Unsubscribe=One-Click, both covered by DKIM. The mail client sends a POST without cookies; the endpoint must unsubscribe from a token in the URL and must not redirect. The GET link should show a confirmation page, because link scanners follow links.

    55. How should bounces and complaints be handled?¶

    Suppress hard bounces and complaints immediately, retry soft bounces and suppress after repeats, and keep your own suppression store per category as well as the provider's. Watch rates: SES reviews accounts at 5% bounces or 0.1% complaints. Gmail does not send complaint data to SES, so watch Postmaster Tools too.

    56. When is a dedicated IP worth it, and what is warm-up?¶

    Only for high, steady volume: a dedicated IP carries your reputation alone and needs regular sending (SES suggests around 1,000 emails a day per mailbox provider). A new IP has no history, so volume must rise gradually over weeks, starting with engaged recipients; SES's automatic warm-up takes 45 days.

    Scenario-based questions¶

    57. After moving DNS from the registrar to a new provider, the website works but nobody receives email. What do you do?¶

    What to check first: the MX records at the new provider, queried directly: dig @<new-ns> example.com MX +norecurse.

    How to confirm: the old provider's export lists MX, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records that the new zone lacks; senders report bounces.

    Likely causes and fixes: only web records were copied before the nameserver change. Add the mail records immediately, then diff every name and type between the providers. See DNS migrations and troubleshooting.

    58. The whole product is unreachable and the domain now shows a parking page. The servers are healthy. What happened?¶

    What to check first: the domain's registration status and expiry date with an RDAP lookup.

    How to confirm: RDAP shows an expiration date in the past and a status such as redemptionPeriod; the A record points at the registrar's parking IPs.

    Likely causes and fixes: the domain expired because auto-renew was off, the card failed, or notices went to a former employee. Renew or restore it, move contacts to a role mailbox, and alert on expiry dates. See domains and DNS records.

    59. After moving TaskFlow Web to a new platform, its certificate cannot be issued: the platform reports a CAA error. What do you change?¶

    What to check first: dig example.com CAA and dig app.example.com CAA.

    How to confirm: the relevant CAA set lists only the old platform's CA, not the one the new platform uses.

    Likely causes and fixes: CAA was never updated. Add the new CA's identifier (keeping any still in use), and add a CAA review to the hosting change checklist. See connecting domains to hosting.

    60. Right after a DNS provider change, some users get SERVFAIL while others resolve fine. Why?¶

    What to check first: compare dig example.com and dig +cd example.com through a validating resolver such as 8.8.8.8.

    How to confirm: the answer works only with +cd; DNSViz shows the parent's DS record matching no DNSKEY at the new provider.

    Likely causes and fixes: the DS record still points at the old provider's key. Remove it (or publish matching keys) and wait for the cached DS to expire; next time remove DS a day before the move. See DNS migrations and troubleshooting.

    61. An hour after switching api.example.com to a new load balancer, a third of requests still reach the old one. Is DNS broken?¶

    What to check first: the record's TTL before the change and the remaining TTL at public resolvers.

    How to confirm: resolvers still hold the old address with a large remaining TTL, or the old load balancer's traffic comes from a few long-lived clients.

    Likely causes and fixes: the TTL was not lowered beforehand, or clients reuse old connections or cached lookups. Keep the old target serving, restart long-lived workers or limit connection age, and lower TTLs a full TTL ahead next time. See DNS migrations and troubleshooting.

    62. After turning on Cloudflare's proxy, browsers show "too many redirects". What do you check?¶

    What to check first: the zone's SSL/TLS encryption mode and the origin's HTTP-to-HTTPS redirect.

    How to confirm: curl -sI shows 301 responses to the same HTTPS URL repeatedly, and the mode is Flexible.

    Likely causes and fixes: Cloudflare fetches the origin over HTTP and the origin redirects to HTTPS. Install a valid origin certificate and use Full (strict). See connecting domains to hosting.

    63. A security researcher reports that promo.example.com serves a phishing page. Nobody on the team deployed it. What happened?¶

    What to check first: dig promo.example.com and what its CNAME target points to.

    How to confirm: the CNAME targets a provider-assigned name for a resource your account no longer owns; Certificate Transparency shows a certificate you did not request.

    Likely causes and fixes: subdomain takeover through a dangling CNAME. Delete the record, treat it as a security incident (cookies scoped to the parent domain may have leaked), audit all CNAMEs, and remove DNS before deleting resources. See connecting domains to hosting.

    64. The platform shows "invalid configuration" for www.example.com, and some users reach an old server. What is wrong?¶

    What to check first: all records at www: dig www.example.com CNAME, A, and AAAA.

    How to confirm: an old AAAA record or a stray A record still points at the previous host; IPv6 users (curl -6) reach it.

    Likely causes and fixes: leftover records from the previous host. Remove them, use exactly the target the dashboard shows, and test over IPv4 and IPv6. See connecting domains to hosting.

    65. The CDN hit ratio for TaskFlow's public pages is 15%. How do you raise it?¶

    What to check first: the cache key configuration and the variety of query strings, cookies, and headers in requests.

    How to confirm: CDN logs show many distinct keys per path that differ only by utm_* parameters, a cookie, or User-Agent.

    Likely causes and fixes: unnecessary inputs in the key or a Vary: User-Agent from the origin. Remove them from the key, strip tracking parameters at the edge, and add a shield or tiered cache. See CDN setup and edge caching.

    66. A user reports seeing another user's task list. Responses pass through CloudFront. What do you check?¶

    What to check first: the cache behavior and cache policy that match /api/*, and the response headers (Age, X-Cache).

    How to confirm: the API path uses a policy with a minimum TTL above zero, or the response lacks private, no-store, and X-Cache shows a hit.

    Likely causes and fixes: a personalized path is cached. Invalidate it, attach CachingDisabled to API behaviors, send private, no-store, test with two users, and handle it as a security incident. See CDN setup and edge caching.

    67. Every deploy is followed by a spike that overloads the origin for several minutes. Why?¶

    What to check first: the deploy's invalidation paths and whether a shield or tiered cache is enabled.

    How to confirm: origin requests per second jump right after an invalidation of /*.

    Likely causes and fixes: purge-everything makes every edge miss at once. Hash asset filenames, invalidate only entry points, and enable Origin Shield or tiered caching. See CDN setup and edge caching.

    68. Password reset emails land in spam at Gmail but arrive fine elsewhere. Where do you look?¶

    What to check first: the Authentication-Results header of a message delivered to a Gmail test account, and Postmaster Tools.

    How to confirm: dmarc=fail, or SPF and DKIM passing only for the provider's domains; or a rising spam rate caused by marketing mail on the same domain and IPs.

    Likely causes and fixes: missing alignment or shared reputation. Set up custom DKIM and a custom Return-Path for example.com, and move marketing to its own subdomain and stream. See email deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

    69. After the marketing team added a new tool, every message from example.com fails SPF. What happened?¶

    What to check first: dig +short example.com TXT for SPF records.

    How to confirm: headers show spf=permerror; there are two SPF records, or counting nested includes gives more than 10 lookups.

    Likely causes and fixes: a second SPF record or the lookup limit. Merge into one record, remove unused mechanisms such as a and mx, and give the tool its own subdomain with its own SPF. See email deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

    70. Mail to hotmail.com and outlook.com addresses now bounces with 550 5.7.515. What does it mean?¶

    What to check first: your daily volume to Outlook.com addresses and the SPF, DKIM, and DMARC results on those messages.

    How to confirm: the domain sends more than 5,000 messages a day there, and messages fail SPF or DKIM, or have no aligned DMARC record.

    Likely causes and fixes: Outlook.com's high-volume sender requirements. Make SPF and DKIM both pass for the sending domain and publish an aligned DMARC record of at least p=none. See email deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
