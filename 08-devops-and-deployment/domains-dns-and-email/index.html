    Domains, DNS and Email¶

    A domain name such as example.com is the address people and other systems use to reach everything you run: the website, the API, the mail servers, and the email your application sends. You rent the name from a registrar, the registry for its top-level domain points the internet at your nameservers, and those nameservers publish the zone: records that send app.example.com to a CDN, api.example.com to a load balancer, inbound mail to a mail provider, and the policies that let mailbox providers trust TaskFlow's email. When this is right nobody notices; when it is wrong the site disappears, password-reset emails land in spam, or certificate renewals start failing.

    What domains, DNS and email setup provide¶

    • A stable name that outlives servers: moving TaskFlow API to a new load balancer or host means changing one record, not every client.
    • Routing per hostname: each name (app, api, www, the apex) can point at a different service, provider, or region.
    • Proof of control: verification records (TXT or CNAME) prove to hosting platforms and certificate authorities that you own the domain, and CAA records limit which authorities may issue certificates for it.
    • Email trust: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records tell receiving servers which senders may use your domain, so your mail can reach the inbox and spoofed mail can be rejected.
    • Edge delivery: pointing a hostname at a CDN puts caching, compression, and protection in front of your origin.

    These pieces also bring duties: renewing the domain, keeping an inventory of records and their owners, planning TTLs before changes, and monitoring both DNS answers and email reputation.

    How a typical domain setup works¶

    1. You register example.com at a registrar and choose a DNS host (it can be the same company). The registrar sends the DNS host's nameservers to the .com registry.
    2. The registry publishes the delegation: NS records for example.com in the .com zone, plus a DS record if DNSSEC is on.
    3. In the DNS host you create the zone's records: app as a CNAME to the CDN, api as an address or alias for the load balancer, MX records for inbound mail, TXT records for SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and verification, and a CAA record for certificates.
    4. A browser asks a recursive resolver for app.example.com. The resolver follows the root, the .com servers, and your nameservers, returns the answer, and caches it for the record's TTL.
    5. The browser connects to the CDN edge, which serves TaskFlow Web from its cache or fetches from the origin. Mail servers sending to @example.com look up the MX records, and mailbox providers that receive TaskFlow's email check its SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records.

    Practical example¶

    A zone excerpt for TaskFlow in standard zone-file format (the addresses and provider names are documentation placeholders):

    ; example.com zone (excerpt): one domain, several services
$TTL 3600
example.com.        IN  NS     ns1.dns-host.example.net.
example.com.        IN  NS     ns2.dns-host.example.net.
www.example.com.    300  IN  CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.
app.example.com.    300  IN  CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.
api.example.com.    60   IN  A      198.51.100.20
example.com.        IN  MX     10 mx1.mail.example.net.
example.com.        IN  MX     20 mx2.mail.example.net.
example.com.        IN  TXT    "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all"
_dmarc.example.com. IN  TXT    "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"
example.com.        IN  CAA    0 issue "letsencrypt.org"

    Each line answers one question: where the web app lives, where the API lives, who receives mail, who may send mail as example.com, and which certificate authority may issue certificates. The deep pages explain each record type, how to choose TTLs, and why the apex (example.com itself) cannot simply be a CNAME.

    Developer perspective and common misconceptions¶

    Treat DNS as production configuration. Keep the zone in code (Terraform or DNSControl), review changes like any other deploy, lower TTLs before planned changes, give every record an owner, and protect the registrar account with MFA, a transfer lock, and auto-renewal. The protocol itself (resolvers, root and TLD servers, record basics) is covered in DNS in the Networking section, and routing users to regions with DNS is covered in global traffic management.

    • "Propagation" is not a push. Your change is live on the authoritative servers within seconds; resolvers keep serving their cached copy until its TTL runs out.
    • The registrar and the DNS host are different roles. Changing nameservers moves the whole zone, so every record (including MX and TXT) must exist at the new host first.
    • A CNAME cannot sit at the zone apex. Use an A or AAAA record, or a provider feature such as an alias record or CNAME flattening.
    • DNS only says where to connect. TLS certificates, redirects, and which site a server shows for a hostname are configured on the host or CDN.
    • SPF, DKIM, and DMARC do not guarantee the inbox; they make mail eligible for it. Complaint rates, list quality, and sending patterns decide the rest.
