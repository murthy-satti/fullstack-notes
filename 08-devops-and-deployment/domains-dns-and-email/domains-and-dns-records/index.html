    Domains and DNS Records¶

    Every hostname TaskFlow uses, from app.example.com to the address its password-reset emails come from, hangs off one registered domain and one DNS zone. This lesson explains who does what in the domain system (registrants, registrars, registries, and DNS hosts), how registration data is published and protected, the domain lifecycle from registration to expiry and transfer, every record type you will meet in practice, how TTLs decide how long an answer lives, and what DNSSEC adds.

    The resolution path itself (recursive resolvers, root and TLD servers, caching) is covered in DNS in the Networking section; this lesson builds on it from the operator's side.

    Why it matters¶

    A domain is a single point of failure that most teams never test. If it expires, the website, the API, inbound mail, and outbound mail all stop at once. If someone takes over the registrar account, they control where every name points and can pass the DNS checks certificate authorities use, so they can obtain valid certificates for your domain. And DNS mistakes have long tails: a wrong answer is cached by thousands of resolvers for as long as its TTL allows, so a five-minute typo can mean an hour of partial outage.

    Records also carry security policy, not only addresses. CAA decides which certificate authorities may issue for you, SPF and DMARC decide whether mail claiming to be from you is accepted, and DNSSEC decides whether validating resolvers trust your answers at all. Knowing each record's exact rules is what separates a routine change from an incident.

    Real-world usage¶

    • ICANN and RDAP. ICANN's RDAP page explains that the Registration Data Access Protocol was created as the replacement for WHOIS and gives registration data in standardized formats. Since 28 January 2025, gTLD registries and registrars must provide RDAP and are, with few exceptions, no longer required to run WHOIS services.
    • Slack's DNSSEC rollout (2021). In The Case of the Recursive Resolvers, Slack describes how enabling DNSSEC on slack.com caused about 24 hours of failures for less than 1% of users. A signing bug in how wildcard records were denied made some resolvers cache "nothing exists here", and when the team rolled back, the DS record was already cached: the .com zone asks resolvers to keep it for 24 hours, and the domain owner cannot shorten that.
    • Let's Encrypt and CAA. The Let's Encrypt CAA documentation says it checks CAA records before every certificate it issues, follows CNAMEs while doing so, and supports the accounturi and validationmethods parameters that pin issuance to one ACME account or challenge type.
    • Route 53 alias records. The Route 53 guide Choosing between alias and non-alias records documents the provider-specific answer to the "no CNAME at the apex" rule: an alias record can sit at the zone apex, answers with the target resource's current IP addresses, and is not charged for queries to AWS resources.

    Popular tools and providers: registrars such as Cloudflare Registrar, Amazon Route 53 Domains, Namecheap, GoDaddy, and Gandi; DNS hosts such as Amazon Route 53, Cloudflare DNS, Google Cloud DNS, Azure DNS, and DNSimple; and tools such as dig, delv, nslookup, PowerShell's Resolve-DnsName, ICANN's RDAP lookup, and DNSViz.

    How it works¶

    Registrant, registrar, registry, and DNS host¶

    Role Who What it does
    Registrant You (TaskFlow's company) Holds the right to use example.com; its contact details receive renewal and transfer messages
    Registrar The company you pay Registers the name with the registry, renews it, and sends your nameservers (and DS records) to the registry
    Registry operator The operator of .com, .org, a country code TLD, and so on Keeps the database of names in its TLD and runs the TLD's nameservers, which publish the delegation
    DNS host Route 53, Cloudflare, your registrar's DNS, your own servers Runs the authoritative nameservers that serve your zone's records
    Recursive resolver Your ISP, 1.1.1.1, 8.8.8.8, a corporate resolver Looks up names for clients and caches the answers

    ICANN accredits registrars for generic TLDs (.com, .org, .dev, .app, and over a thousand more), while country code TLDs (.uk, .in, .de) are run by national registries with their own rules on who may register and how transfers work. One company can be both your registrar and your DNS host, but the two jobs stay separate: you can keep the registration at one company and point the nameservers at another.

    When you choose a domain, check the renewal price (not only the first year), whether the TLD has residency or eligibility rules, and register the variants you care about before launch. Never invent private top-level domains for internal services; use a subdomain of a domain you own, such as internal.example.com, so nobody else can ever register it.

    Registration data: WHOIS, RDAP, and privacy¶

    Registries and registrars publish registration data: creation and expiry dates, status codes, nameservers, and the registrar. RDAP returns it as JSON over HTTPS, with a standard bootstrap file at IANA that tells clients which server to ask for each TLD. Personal contact details are usually redacted (data protection laws such as the GDPR pushed the industry this way), and many registrars offer a privacy or proxy service on top.

    Two practical consequences:

    • The registrant email must be real and monitored. Renewal notices, transfer approvals, and registrant verification emails go there. Some registries require the registrant to confirm the address, and an unverified contact can get the domain suspended. Use a role mailbox such as domains@example.com, never one person's inbox.
    • Monitor the expiry date like a certificate. RDAP gives you the expiration event in machine-readable form, so a scheduled check can alert months ahead.

    Domain lifecycle, locks, and transfers¶

    ICANN's Expired Registration Recovery Policy sets the minimum rules for generic TLDs: the registrar must send notices about a month and about a week before expiry, interrupt the domain's DNS after it expires (while still letting the registrant renew), and registries must offer a 30-day redemption grace period after deletion. Registrars set their own prices and exact windows inside those rules, so read yours before you need it.

    Locks protect a domain from hijacking and mistakes. Their state shows up as EPP status codes in RDAP:

    Status Set by Effect
    clientTransferProhibited Registrar (the "transfer lock" toggle) Blocks transfers to another registrar
    clientUpdateProhibited / clientDeleteProhibited Registrar Blocks changes to nameservers or contacts, and deletion
    serverTransferProhibited and other server* codes Registry The same, enforced at the registry; with a registry lock, changes need an out-of-band verification
    redemptionPeriod, pendingDelete Registry The domain is expired and being deleted

    Moving the registration to another registrar is a transfer: unlock the domain, get the authorization (EPP) code from the old registrar, request the transfer at the new one, and approve it. Typical rules, as listed in the Route 53 transfer guide: no transfer within 60 days of registration or a previous transfer, a 60-day lock after a change of registrant (some registrars let you opt out before the change), and for most generic TLDs the transfer adds a year to the registration. Two traps:

    • If your old registrar also hosts your DNS for free, it may switch that DNS off when the transfer starts. Move DNS hosting first, then the registration.
    • If the domain uses DNSSEC and the DNS host will change, remove the DS record first and wait at least a day for cached copies to expire.

    Record types¶

    Type Holds TaskFlow example
    A An IPv4 address api.example.com A 198.51.100.20
    AAAA An IPv6 address api.example.com AAAA 2001:db8::20
    CNAME Another name; the resolver restarts the lookup there app.example.com CNAME taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.
    ALIAS / ANAME / alias record Provider feature: the DNS host resolves a target and answers with its addresses example.com alias to a CDN distribution
    MX Priority and mail server hostname example.com MX 10 mx1.mail.example.net.
    TXT Text strings: SPF, DMARC, DKIM keys, verification tokens _dmarc.example.com TXT "v=DMARC1; p=none; ..."
    CAA Which certificate authorities may issue example.com CAA 0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
    NS Nameservers for a zone (at the apex and at the parent) example.com NS ns1.dns-host.example.net.
    SOA Zone metadata: primary server, contact, serial, timers One per zone, managed by most providers
    SRV Priority, weight, port, and target for a service _mongodb._tcp.db.example.com SRV 0 0 27017 db-1.example.com.
    PTR Reverse DNS: the name for an IP address 20.100.51.198.in-addr.arpa PTR api.example.com.
    HTTPS / SVCB Connection hints such as supported protocols Covered in DNS
    DS, DNSKEY, RRSIG DNSSEC keys and signatures See DNSSEC below

    A and AAAA¶

    Address records are the end of every lookup. Publish AAAA alongside A when the target supports IPv6, and point them only at addresses you control for as long as the record exists: a static or reserved IP for a VPS, never an address a provider can hand to someone else.

    CNAME and its rules¶

    A CNAME says "this name is an alias; ask for that one instead". The rules come from the core DNS specifications (RFC 1034 and RFC 2181):

    • No other data at the same name. A name with a CNAME cannot also have MX, TXT, or A records. This is why the zone apex, which must hold SOA and NS records, can never be a CNAME.
    • MX and NS targets must not be aliases. Point them at names with real A/AAAA records.
    • A CNAME applies to every query type. A TXT lookup for a CNAMEd name follows the alias, which surprises people adding verification records.
    • Chains cost time. Each hop can be another lookup; keep chains short.

    The apex problem: alias records and CNAME flattening¶

    Hosting platforms and CDNs want you to point at a hostname they control, so they can change IP addresses and steer traffic. Subdomains can do that with a CNAME; the apex cannot. DNS hosts solve it with non-standard features that resolve the target on the authoritative side:

    • Route 53 alias records answer with the current addresses of an AWS resource (CloudFront, a load balancer, an S3 website, and others), can sit at the apex, and appear to clients as plain A or AAAA records.
    • Cloudflare CNAME flattening happens automatically for a CNAME at the apex on all plans and for proxied records; paid plans can flatten every CNAME.
    • ALIAS and ANAME records at other DNS hosts work the same way.

    Because the DNS host resolves the target from its own servers, a platform that steers users by DNS no longer sees the user's resolver. That is why Netlify recommends a subdomain such as www as the primary domain when you use external DNS, and Vercel recommends www with a CNAME so its CDN keeps more control over traffic. The apex then becomes a redirect, as shown in connecting domains to hosting.

    MX: where mail for the domain goes¶

    Each MX record has a preference (lower is tried first) and a hostname that must have address records. Several MX records with different preferences give fallbacks. A domain that should never receive mail publishes a null MX (MX 0 ., RFC 7505) so senders fail fast instead of retrying for days. example.com itself is a good model of a domain that neither sends nor receives mail:

    $ dig +short example.com MX
0 .
$ dig +short example.com TXT
"v=spf1 -all"
$ dig +short _dmarc.example.com TXT
"v=DMARC1;p=reject;sp=reject;adkim=s;aspf=s"

    These are the real answers for example.com in November 2026 (TXT output trimmed to the SPF record): no mail exchanger, no authorized sender, and a DMARC policy telling receivers to reject anything claiming to come from it. Every parked or redirect-only domain you own should publish the same three records so spammers cannot borrow it.

    TXT: the general-purpose record¶

    TXT records carry SPF (at the domain itself), DMARC (at _dmarc), DKIM public keys (at <selector>._domainkey), and verification tokens from platforms and certificate authorities. Details that matter:

    • One TXT record can hold several strings of up to 255 bytes each; receivers join them without spaces. Long DKIM keys are split this way.
    • A name can have many TXT records, but only one SPF record (v=spf1 ...). Two SPF records make every SPF check a permanent error.
    • Some DNS consoles want quotes around the value, some add them, and some append the zone name to whatever you type in the name field. Always check the published result with dig.

    SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are explained in depth in email deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

    CAA: which CAs may issue¶

    A CAA record (RFC 8659) lists the certificate authorities allowed to issue for a name. Public CAs must check it before issuing.

    example.com.  CAA  0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  CAA  0 issue "amazon.com"
example.com.  CAA  0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  CAA  0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"
    • issue allows a CA for normal certificates; issuewild controls wildcard certificates, and when any issuewild record exists, issue records are ignored for wildcard requests.
    • The CA looks for CAA at the exact name, then climbs toward the root and uses the first name that has any CAA records. A CAA set at example.com therefore governs api.example.com too, unless api.example.com has its own.
    • No CAA records anywhere means any CA may issue. issue ";" means none may.
    • Each CA documents its identifier: Let's Encrypt uses letsencrypt.org, and AWS Certificate Manager needs one of amazon.com, amazontrust.com, awstrust.com, or amazonaws.com. Cloudflare adds CAA records for its own CAs automatically once you create any CAA record, so its Universal SSL keeps renewing.
    • 0 issue "letsencrypt.org; accounturi=<account-url>" (RFC 8657) restricts issuance to one ACME account.

    CAA is cheap protection, but it is also the classic cause of "renewal suddenly fails after we moved hosting": the new platform's CA is not on the list. Certificate issuance itself is covered in obtaining and renewing certificates.

    NS and SOA¶

    NS records appear twice: at the parent (the .com servers' delegation, plus glue addresses when the nameservers are inside the zone) and at the apex of your zone. They should match. The SOA record holds the primary nameserver, a contact mailbox, a serial number, timers for secondary servers, and the value used for negative caching: per RFC 2308, a "no such name" answer is cached for the smaller of the SOA record's TTL and its minimum field. Managed DNS hosts maintain SOA for you.

    SRV and PTR¶

    SRV records publish a service's priority, weight, port, and host under _service._protocol.name. TaskFlow meets them through MongoDB: a mongodb+srv://db.example.com/ connection string makes the driver look up _mongodb._tcp.db.example.com SRV records for the replica set members and a TXT record on db.example.com for options such as replicaSet and authSource, and it turns TLS on by default. Keep such internal names in a private zone.

    PTR records live in the reverse zones in-addr.arpa and ip6.arpa, which belong to whoever owns the IP address. For a cloud server you set them through the provider. Reverse DNS matters mostly for mail: Gmail requires sending IPs to have a PTR record whose hostname resolves back to the same IP.

    TTL: how long an answer lives¶

    Every record has a TTL in seconds. A resolver that fetched the record may answer from its cache until the TTL runs out, then must ask again.

    TTL Good for Trade-off
    60 to 300 s Records you may change soon: api, app, anything before a migration More queries, and an outage of your DNS host hurts sooner
    3600 s Most stable records A mistake lingers for up to an hour
    86400 s NS, MX, and other rarely changed records Changes take up to a day to reach everyone

    Worked example: api.example.com has a TTL of 3600. A resolver caches it at 09:59:30, and you change the record at 10:00. That resolver keeps returning the old address until 10:59:30, so the worst case is one full TTL after the change, plus whatever applications cache on top. Lowering the TTL helps only if you do it at least one old TTL before the change; DNS migrations and troubleshooting turns this into a runbook. Alias records inherit their TTL from the target (AWS load balancers use 60 seconds), and Cloudflare proxied records use an automatic TTL of 300 seconds.

    DNSSEC at a high level¶

    DNSSEC adds signatures so a validating resolver can prove an answer came from the zone owner and was not altered. It does not encrypt anything; DNS over HTTPS or TLS does that, between client and resolver.

    • DNSKEY records hold the zone's public keys, RRSIG records hold signatures over each record set, and NSEC or NSEC3 records prove that a name does not exist.
    • The parent zone holds a DS record, a hash of your key, which you submit through the registrar. That link is what connects your zone to the chain of trust.
    • If the DS at the parent and the key in your zone disagree, every validating resolver (and that includes the large public resolvers) returns SERVFAIL. Because the DS is cached for a day at .com, the failure outlives the fix, as Slack's incident showed.

    Managed providers (Route 53, Cloudflare, Google Cloud DNS, Azure DNS) sign the zone for you, but how much key work remains varies: Route 53, for example, manages the zone-signing key while the key-signing key is a customer managed key in AWS KMS that you own and rotate. Your other jobs are publishing the DS at the registrar and planning provider migrations around it. Enable DNSSEC for high-value domains when signing is automated and your team has a written migration procedure.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Need Use Avoid
    Point a subdomain at a platform or CDN CNAME to the hostname the platform gives you Copying the platform's current IPs into A records
    Point the apex at a platform, CDN, or load balancer Alias record, flattened CNAME, the platform's documented static IPs, or a redirect to www A CNAME at the apex
    A VPS with a fixed address A and AAAA to a reserved static IP Ephemeral IPs that change on restart
    A load balancer Alias or CNAME to its DNS name A records to its current IPs (they change)
    A domain that sends and receives no mail Null MX, v=spf1 -all, DMARC p=reject Leaving it without email records
    Restrict certificate issuance CAA listing every CA you actually use Forgetting the CA of a new hosting platform
    Discovering a service's hosts and ports SRV (for example MongoDB's +srv strings) Hard-coding host lists in many clients
    High-value domain integrity DNSSEC with provider-managed signing DNSSEC without a migration plan

    Do not use DNS round robin as load balancing or failover: resolvers cache answers and DNS does not know whether a server is healthy. Use a load balancer or health-checked DNS routing instead (see load balancing and global traffic management). Avoid wildcard records unless you need them: they make typos resolve and hide missing records.

    Practical examples¶

    A complete zone for TaskFlow¶

    ; example.com zone file (BIND format); documentation addresses only
$ORIGIN example.com.
$TTL 3600
@       IN SOA  ns1.dns-host.example.net. hostmaster.example.com. (
                2026111301 ; serial (YYYYMMDDnn)
                7200       ; refresh
                3600       ; retry
                1209600    ; expire
                300 )      ; minimum: negative-caching TTL
@       IN NS     ns1.dns-host.example.net.
@       IN NS     ns2.dns-host.example.net.

; Web: www is the primary site; the apex redirects to it
@       300  IN A      203.0.113.10        ; redirect service
@       300  IN AAAA   2001:db8::10
www     300  IN CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.
app     300  IN CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.

; API behind a load balancer with fixed addresses
api     60   IN A      198.51.100.20
api     60   IN AAAA   2001:db8::20

; Inbound mail and sender policy
@       IN MX     10 mx1.mail.example.net.
@       IN MX     20 mx2.mail.example.net.
@       IN TXT    "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all"
_dmarc  IN TXT    "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"

; Certificates
@       IN CAA    0 issue "letsencrypt.org"
@       IN CAA    0 iodef "mailto:security@example.com"
    • $ORIGIN makes unqualified names relative: www means www.example.com.. A name with a trailing dot is absolute; a name without one gets the origin appended, which is the source of records such as mx1.example.com.example.com.
    • Record-level TTLs override $TTL: names you may change soon get 60 to 300 seconds.
    • The SOA minimum (300) bounds how long resolvers remember that a name does not exist, which matters when you create a record someone already looked up.

    Querying records with dig and Resolve-DnsName¶

    dig +short api.example.com A                 # just the answer
dig +noall +answer app.example.com           # answer section with TTLs; shows the CNAME chain
dig +short example.com MX
dig +short _dmarc.example.com TXT
dig +short example.com CAA
dig example.com SOA +multiline               # serial and timers, readable
dig @ns1.dns-host.example.net api.example.com A +norecurse   # ask the authoritative server directly

    ; Example output of: dig +noall +answer app.example.com
app.example.com.                300  IN  CNAME  taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.
taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.    60  IN  A      203.0.113.50
taskflow-web.cdn.example.net.    60  IN  A      203.0.113.51

    The TTL column counts down in a resolver's cache: run the command twice a few seconds apart and a cached answer shows a smaller number. On Windows:

    Resolve-DnsName -Name app.example.com -Type A -Server 1.1.1.1
Resolve-DnsName -Name example.com -Type MX
nslookup -type=TXT _dmarc.example.com

    Checking registration data with RDAP¶

    # The IANA bootstrap file says which RDAP server serves each TLD
curl -s https://data.iana.org/rdap/dns.json | jq -r '.services[] | select(.[0][] == "com") | .[1][]'

# Ask that server about the domain, keep the fields worth monitoring
curl -s https://rdap.verisign.com/com/v1/domain/example.com \
  | jq '{name: .ldhName, status, events: [.events[] | {(.eventAction): .eventDate}], dnssec: .secureDNS.delegationSigned}'

    {
  "name": "EXAMPLE.COM",
  "status": ["client delete prohibited", "client transfer prohibited", "client update prohibited"],
  "events": [
    { "registration": "1995-08-14T04:00:00Z" },
    { "expiration": "2027-08-13T04:00:00Z" },
    ...
  ],
  "dnssec": true
}

    This is the real (trimmed) answer for example.com in November 2026: all three client locks are on, the delegation is signed, and the expiry date is machine-readable. A scheduled job that alerts when expiration is less than 60 days away, for every domain you own, prevents the most embarrassing outage in this lesson.

    Managing core records in Terraform (Route 53)¶

    # infra/dns/records.tf: core records for example.com in Route 53
terraform {
  required_version = ">= 1.6"
  required_providers {
    aws = {
      source  = "hashicorp/aws"
      version = "~> 6.0"
    }
  }
}

provider "aws" {
  region = "us-east-1"
}

resource "aws_route53_zone" "main" {
  name = "example.com"
}

resource "aws_route53_record" "mx" {
  zone_id = aws_route53_zone.main.zone_id
  name    = "example.com"
  type    = "MX"
  ttl     = 3600
  records = ["10 mx1.mail.example.net.", "20 mx2.mail.example.net."]
}

# All TXT strings for one name live in ONE record set
resource "aws_route53_record" "apex_txt" {
  zone_id = aws_route53_zone.main.zone_id
  name    = "example.com"
  type    = "TXT"
  ttl     = 3600
  records = [
    "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all",
    "example-site-verification=<token>",
  ]
}

resource "aws_route53_record" "caa" {
  zone_id = aws_route53_zone.main.zone_id
  name    = "example.com"
  type    = "CAA"
  ttl     = 3600
  records = [
    "0 issue \"letsencrypt.org\"",
    "0 issue \"amazon.com\"",
    "0 issuewild \"letsencrypt.org\"",
    "0 iodef \"mailto:security@example.com\"",
  ]
}

output "nameservers" {
  value = aws_route53_zone.main.name_servers # give these to the registrar
}
    • Route 53 stores one record set per name and type, so the SPF record and a verification token must share the apex_txt resource; a second resource for the same name and type fails.
    • The nameservers output is what you enter at the registrar to delegate the domain to this zone.
    • Keeping records in code gives you review, history, and a quick way to rebuild the zone at another provider.

    Locking down a domain¶

    • Turn on auto-renew, keep a valid payment method, and register important domains for several years.
    • Enable the registrar's transfer lock, and a registry lock for the main production domain if your registrar offers it.
    • Protect the registrar and DNS accounts with MFA (security keys if possible), few administrators, and API tokens scoped to one zone.
    • Use a role mailbox for registrant and technical contacts.
    • Alert on expiry dates (RDAP), on unexpected NS or DS changes, and on certificates issued for your names (Certificate Transparency monitoring).

    Best practices¶

    • Separate the questions "who registers the domain" and "who hosts DNS", and document both, with account owners.
    • Manage zones as code and review every change.
    • Point names at hostnames the platform controls (CNAME or alias), not at IP addresses you copied.
    • Choose TTLs per record: short for names you may move, long for stable ones, and lower them a full TTL before planned changes.
    • Publish null MX, v=spf1 -all, and DMARC p=reject on domains that send no mail.
    • Keep CAA in sync with every CA your platforms use.
    • Keep internal names in private zones and out of public DNS.
    • Enable DNSSEC only with provider-managed signing and a migration plan.
    • Verify every change with dig against the authoritative servers and at least one public resolver.

    In production¶

    • The whole product is down, and DNS returns a parking page. Detect: RDAP shows an expiration date in the past and a status such as redemptionPeriod; the A record now points at the registrar's parking IPs. Cause: auto-renew was off, the card expired, or the renewal notices went to a former employee. Fix: renew immediately (or restore during redemption, usually with a fee), move contacts to a role mailbox, and add expiry alerts.
    • Certificate renewal fails with "CAA record prevents issuance". Detect: the ACME client or the platform reports a CAA error; dig example.com CAA lists only another CA. Cause: a new hosting platform or CDN uses a CA missing from the CAA set. Fix: add that CA's identifier, and make CAA review part of every hosting change.
    • Some users get SERVFAIL, others resolve fine. Detect: dig example.com returns SERVFAIL through a validating resolver but works with dig +cd; DNSViz shows a broken chain. Cause: the DS record at the registry does not match the zone's keys, often after a DNS provider change. Fix: publish the matching DNSKEY or remove the DS, then wait out the DS TTL; plan DNSSEC into migrations.
    • Mail stops arriving after a new record is added at the apex. Detect: MX lookups now return a CNAME, or the DNS console silently replaced records. Cause: a CNAME was created at a name that also had MX and TXT records. Fix: use the provider's alias or flattening feature at the apex and restore the mail records.
    • A verification record "exists" but the platform cannot see it. Detect: dig _dmarc.example.com TXT is empty, while dig _dmarc.example.com.example.com TXT returns the value. Cause: the console appended the zone name to a fully qualified name. Fix: enter the relative name (_dmarc) or add a trailing dot, and verify with dig.
    • Changed records keep returning old values for hours. Detect: dig @<authoritative> shows the new value; public resolvers show the old one with a large remaining TTL. Cause: the old TTL was long and was not lowered beforehand. Fix: wait it out (flush the public resolvers' caches where they offer it) and lower TTLs a full TTL ahead next time.

    Important terminology¶

    • Registrant: The holder of a domain registration.
    • Registrar: The company that registers and renews domains with the registry for you.
    • Registry: The operator of a TLD's database and nameservers.
    • Delegation: NS records in the parent zone that point a domain at its authoritative nameservers.
    • Authoritative nameserver: A server that holds a zone's records and answers for them.
    • Zone apex: The domain's own name (example.com), which holds the SOA and NS records.
    • TTL: How many seconds a resolver may cache an answer.
    • Negative caching: Caching the fact that a name or record does not exist.
    • RDAP: The JSON-over-HTTPS protocol for registration data that replaced WHOIS.
    • EPP status code: A registry status such as clientTransferProhibited that shows locks and lifecycle state.
    • Redemption grace period: A 30-day window after deletion when only the previous registrant can restore a gTLD domain.
    • CNAME flattening / alias record: A DNS host feature that resolves a target hostname and answers with its addresses, allowed at the apex.
    • CAA: A record listing the certificate authorities allowed to issue for a name.
    • DS record: The parent zone's hash of a child zone's DNSSEC key.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Treating the registrar and the DNS host as the same thing, and losing records when changing nameservers.
    • Putting a CNAME at the apex, or next to MX and TXT records.
    • Publishing two SPF records at one name.
    • Copying a platform's current IP addresses into A records instead of using its hostname.
    • Believing a low TTL helps a change that is already live; it must be lowered beforehand.
    • Thinking DNSSEC encrypts DNS. It signs answers; encryption is DoH or DoT.
    • Assuming a domain renews itself with a lapsed card or a dead contact email.
    • Leaving CAA records unchanged when moving to a platform that uses a different CA.
