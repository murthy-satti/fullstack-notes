    Email Deliverability: SPF, DKIM and DMARC¶

    TaskFlow sends invitations, password resets, and "tasks due today" reminders. Whether those messages reach the inbox depends less on the code that sends them than on DNS records, sending reputation, and how bounces and complaints are handled. This lesson covers why application email lands in spam, transactional versus marketing email, choosing a sending provider (Amazon SES, SendGrid, Postmark, Mailgun, Resend), SPF and its 10-lookup limit, DKIM signing, DMARC alignment, policies, reports, and a safe rollout, the 2026 update to the DMARC standard, the sender requirements of Gmail, Yahoo, and Outlook.com, bounce and complaint handling, and dedicated IPs and warm-up.

    How mail moves between servers (SMTP, MX lookups, IMAP) is covered in email protocols, and designing a notification service with queues, retries, and provider failover in the notification system case study.

    Why it matters¶

    A password reset that lands in spam is an outage for that user, and support usually hears about it before monitoring does. Mailbox providers decide where a message goes from two kinds of evidence: authentication (is this message really from example.com?) and reputation (does mail from this domain and these IPs get marked as spam, bounce, or hit spam traps?). Since 2024 Gmail and Yahoo, and since 2025 Outlook.com, require authentication from bulk senders and reject or junk mail that lacks it.

    There is a security side too. Without SPF, DKIM, and an enforced DMARC policy, anyone can send phishing email that claims to come from example.com, and your users have no way to tell.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Gmail's sender guidelines. Google's Email sender guidelines require every sender to personal Gmail accounts to use SPF or DKIM, valid forward and reverse DNS, TLS, and a spam rate below 0.3%; bulk senders (close to 5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail accounts) also need SPF and DKIM, a DMARC policy (at least p=none), alignment, and one-click unsubscribe. The FAQ adds that bulk status is permanent and that from November 2025 Gmail is ramping up enforcement, with temporary and permanent rejections.
    • Yahoo's Sender Hub. Yahoo's sender best practices set the same baseline: SPF or DKIM for everyone; SPF, DKIM, and a passing DMARC policy of at least p=none for bulk senders; one-click unsubscribe honored within two days; and a spam rate below 0.3%.
    • Outlook.com high-volume senders. Microsoft's announcement requires domains sending more than 5,000 messages a day to Outlook.com consumer addresses (outlook.com, hotmail.com, live.com) to pass SPF and DKIM and publish DMARC of at least p=none, aligned with SPF or DKIM, from May 5, 2025. An April 2025 update dropped the planned Junk-folder stage: non-compliant mail is rejected with 550 5.7.515.
    • DMARC as a proposed standard. The original DMARC specification, RFC 7489, was published as an informational document in 2015. In May 2026 the IETF published RFC 9989 ("DMARCbis") on the Standards Track, obsoleting RFC 7489 and RFC 9091, with aggregate and failure reporting split into RFC 9990 and RFC 9991.

    Popular tools and providers: Amazon SES, SendGrid, Postmark, Mailgun, and Resend for sending; Google Postmaster Tools, Yahoo's Sender Hub, and Microsoft's SNDS for reputation data; DMARC report processors such as the open-source parsedmarc or a hosted service; and DNSControl's SPF_BUILDER for managing SPF records in code.

    How it works¶

    Why application email lands in spam¶

    Signal What receivers look at What you control
    Authentication SPF, DKIM, DMARC results and alignment with the visible From DNS records and provider configuration
    Domain and IP reputation Spam complaints, bounces, spam traps, history of the domain and IPs List quality, consent, volume patterns, shared or dedicated IPs
    Engagement Opens, replies, deletions without reading, "not spam" clicks Sending only mail people asked for and want
    Infrastructure Reverse DNS, TLS, standards-compliant messages Mostly the provider's job
    Content Links, formatting, misleading subjects Honest, plain templates

    Authentication is the entry ticket; reputation decides the folder. Google asks senders to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.1% and never to reach 0.3%.

    Transactional and marketing email¶

    Transactional Marketing (broadcast)
    Examples Password reset, invitation, receipt, security alert Newsletter, product announcement, promotion
    Trigger One user's action A campaign to many people
    Expectation Must arrive within seconds Can wait; must respect consent
    Unsubscribe Not for security mail; offer it for optional notifications Required, including one-click for bulk senders
    Risk Low complaint rate when done right Higher complaint rate

    Keep the two apart so a campaign's complaints cannot sink password resets: send marketing from its own subdomain (for example news.example.com) and its own provider stream or IP pool. Postmark, for example, runs transactional and broadcast message streams on separate infrastructure. Note that Gmail counts mail from subdomains toward the primary domain when deciding whether you are a bulk sender.

    Choosing a sending provider¶

    Provider DNS you add Notable details
    Amazon SES Three Easy DKIM CNAMEs (2048-bit keys by default); a custom MAIL FROM subdomain with an MX to feedback-smtp.<region>.amazonses.com and SPF include:amazonses.com New accounts start in a sandbox (verified recipients only, 200 messages a day, 1 per second); account-level suppression list on by default; review at 5% bounces or 0.1% complaints, possible pause at 10% or 0.5%
    SendGrid Domain authentication with automated security: CNAMEs em<number>, s1._domainkey, s2._domainkey, plus link-branding CNAMEs SendGrid updates the SPF and DKIM targets behind the CNAMEs for you
    Postmark A DKIM TXT record; a custom Return-Path CNAME such as pm-bounces pointing to pm.mtasv.net Separate transactional and broadcast streams on separate IP pools
    Mailgun A sending subdomain such as mg.example.com with SPF include:mailgun.org, a DKIM TXT key, and an optional tracking CNAME Mailgun recommends a subdomain rather than the root domain
    Resend DKIM and SPF records (TXT and MX or CNAME) generated per domain; Return-Path defaults to send.<your domain> Recommends sending from subdomains; do not proxy its CNAMEs through Cloudflare

    Whichever you choose, the pattern is the same: prove the domain with DKIM, give the provider a bounce (Return-Path) subdomain so SPF aligns, and publish DMARC yourself.

    The three identities in every message¶

    • Header From (reminders@example.com): what the user sees. DMARC protects this domain.
    • Envelope MAIL FROM (the Return-Path, bounce@bounce.example.com): where bounces go. SPF checks this domain, not the header From.
    • DKIM signing domain (d=example.com in the DKIM-Signature header): the domain whose key signed the message.

    SPF: which servers may send¶

    An SPF record is a TXT record on the envelope sender's domain:

    bounce.example.com.  TXT  "v=spf1 include:amazonses.com ~all"
example.com.         TXT  "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all"
    • Mechanisms are checked left to right: ip4: and ip6: ranges, include: another domain's SPF, a and mx (the domain's own hosts), and all at the end. The qualifier on all decides the default: -all fails anything not listed, ~all soft-fails it.
    • One SPF record per name. Two records make every check a permanent error.
    • SPF breaks when mail is forwarded, because the forwarder's IP is not in your record. DKIM survives forwarding, which is why DMARC accepts either.

    The 10-lookup limit. RFC 7208 caps the DNS lookups an SPF check may trigger at 10. The include, a, mx, ptr, and exists mechanisms and the redirect modifier each count, including those inside included records; ip4, ip6, and all do not. Going over the limit is a permerror, so SPF fails for every message, not just the last sender. RFC 7208 also asks receivers to allow at most two "void" lookups (names that return nothing).

    Ways to stay under the limit: remove mechanisms for hosts that do not send mail (a, mx), never use ptr, give each third-party sender its own subdomain (each name has its own budget of 10), and use the custom MAIL FROM or Return-Path subdomain your provider offers. Flattening includes into IP ranges works but breaks silently when the provider changes its IPs; DNSControl's documentation recommends flattening only when it is the only way under 10.

    DKIM: a signature from your domain¶

    The provider signs selected headers and a hash of the body with a private key and adds a header such as:

    DKIM-Signature: v=1; a=rsa-sha256; d=example.com; s=s1; c=relaxed/relaxed;
        h=from:to:subject:date:message-id:list-unsubscribe:list-unsubscribe-post;
        bh=<body-hash>; b=<signature>

    The receiver fetches the public key from s1._domainkey.example.com:

    s1._domainkey.example.com.  TXT  "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkq...truncated...IDAQAB"
    • Key size: RFC 8301 requires at least 1024-bit RSA keys and recommends 2048; rsa-sha1 must no longer be used. SES Easy DKIM uses 2048-bit keys by default.
    • Selectors (s=) let several keys coexist, which is how keys are rotated: publish a new selector, switch signing to it, and remove the old key after mail in transit has been delivered. When the provider gives you CNAMEs for the selectors (SES, SendGrid), it can rotate keys without you touching DNS.
    • What breaks DKIM: anything that changes signed headers or the body, such as mailing lists that add footers. ARC (RFC 8617) lets such intermediaries vouch for the original results.

    DMARC: alignment, policy, and reports¶

    DMARC is a TXT record at _dmarc.<domain>:

    _dmarc.example.com.  TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; sp=quarantine; adkim=r; aspf=r; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"

    A message passes DMARC when SPF or DKIM passes and the passing domain aligns with the header From domain.

    Tag Meaning
    p Policy for the domain: none (monitor), quarantine (treat as suspicious, usually the spam folder), reject
    sp Policy for existing subdomains (defaults to p)
    np Policy for subdomains that do not exist (from RFC 9091, now part of RFC 9989)
    adkim, aspf Alignment mode: r relaxed (same organizational domain, the default) or s strict (exact match)
    rua Where to send daily aggregate reports
    ruf Where to send per-message failure reports (few receivers send them)
    t Test mode in RFC 9989: t=y asks receivers to apply the next weaker policy

    Reports are what make DMARC safe to enforce. Aggregate reports are XML files, usually daily, listing every IP that sent mail as your domain with counts and SPF and DKIM results. They reveal the CRM, the support desk, and the forgotten script that send as example.com. If rua points at another domain (a reporting service), that domain must publish a record authorizing it, which hosted services set up for you.

    What changed in RFC 9989. The pct tag (apply the policy to a percentage of mail) was removed because receivers rarely applied it accurately except at 0 and 100; t=y replaces its main use. The rf and ri tags were removed, np and psd were added, and receivers now find the organizational domain with a DNS tree walk instead of the Public Suffix List. It also makes explicit (Section 7.4) that domains whose users might post to mailing lists should not publish p=reject; any such domain that still wants it should first run p=none, then p=quarantine, for at least a month each. Receivers ignore tags they do not know, so existing records keep parsing, but receivers that still implement RFC 7489 ignore t, so treat every step to a stricter p as real.

    Rolling DMARC out safely¶

    1. Publish p=none with rua and collect reports for a few weeks.
    2. For each legitimate source, make it align: DKIM with d=example.com (or the subdomain it sends from) and a custom Return-Path where possible. Move hard-to-fix senders to their own subdomain.
    3. Move to p=quarantine (optionally first with t=y), and watch.
    4. Move to p=reject (optionally first with t=y), and set sp and np deliberately.
    5. Keep reading reports; a new SaaS tool sending as your domain shows up there first.

    Domains that never send mail should go straight to p=reject together with a null MX and v=spf1 -all, as shown in domains and DNS records.

    What Gmail, Yahoo, and Outlook.com require¶

    Requirement Gmail (personal accounts) Yahoo Outlook.com (consumer)
    Who is covered All senders; extra rules for bulk senders (close to 5,000+ messages a day, status permanent) All senders; extra rules for bulk senders Domains sending more than 5,000 messages a day
    SPF and DKIM All: SPF or DKIM. Bulk: both All: SPF or DKIM. Bulk: both SPF must pass and DKIM must pass
    DMARC Bulk: published (p=none allowed), with alignment Bulk: at least p=none, must pass; relaxed alignment is fine At least p=none, aligned with SPF or DKIM
    Unsubscribe Bulk marketing and subscribed mail: one-click (RFC 8058) plus a visible link; honor within 48 hours Bulk: one-click (POST highly recommended, mailto accepted) plus a visible link; honor within 2 days A clear, working unsubscribe is recommended
    Spam rate Below 0.3%, aim below 0.1% Below 0.3% Not stated as a number
    Other Valid forward and reverse DNS, TLS, RFC 5322 format Valid forward and reverse DNS, RFC 5321 and 5322 Valid, reply-capable From or Reply-To
    Enforcement Since February 2024; ramping up from November 2025 with rejections Since February 2024 Since May 5, 2025: rejected with 550 5.7.515

    These rules do not apply to mail sent to Google Workspace business accounts, but meeting them everywhere is simpler than tracking exceptions.

    Bounces, complaints, and suppression lists¶

    Event Meaning Action
    Hard bounce Permanent failure, such as an address that does not exist Stop sending to the address immediately
    Soft bounce Temporary failure, such as a full mailbox Retry; suppress after repeated failures
    Complaint The recipient marked the message as spam (reported through a feedback loop) Stop sending that kind of mail to them immediately
    Unsubscribe The recipient opted out of a category Stop that category; keep security mail

    Providers keep their own suppression lists (SES's account-level list holds addresses that hard-bounced or complained until you remove them), but keep yours too, per message category, so it survives a provider change. Two details matter. SES places an account under review at a 5% bounce rate or a 0.1% complaint rate, and recommends staying below 2% bounces. And Gmail does not send complaint data to SES, so for Gmail users your signals are the spam rate in Postmaster Tools and the unsubscribes you receive, which is one more reason to offer one-click unsubscribe on optional mail.

    Shared and dedicated IPs, and warm-up¶

    Providers send most customers' mail from shared IP pools whose reputation they manage. A dedicated IP gives you your own reputation, good or bad, and needs steady volume: SES advises sending around 1,000 emails a day to each mailbox provider you want to keep a good reputation with. New IPs have no history, so they must be warmed up: volume rises gradually over weeks (SES's automatic warm-up for standard dedicated IPs takes 45 days, and mailbox providers need roughly two to six weeks), starting with the most engaged recipients. Choose dedicated IPs only for high, steady volume; for most applications a reputable shared pool plus good domain reputation is the better choice.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation Recommended setup
    Password resets and invitations for TaskFlow A transactional provider (Postmark, SES, Resend, SendGrid, or Mailgun) on a shared pool, DKIM with d=example.com, a custom Return-Path, DMARC
    Optional reminders and digests The transactional setup plus List-Unsubscribe and List-Unsubscribe-Post headers and per-category preferences
    Newsletters and announcements A separate subdomain and broadcast stream, explicit consent, one-click unsubscribe
    High, steady volume Dedicated IPs with warm-up and monitoring per mailbox provider
    Third-party tools sending as you (CRM, help desk) Their custom DKIM for your domain, or their own subdomain
    Domains that send no mail Null MX, v=spf1 -all, DMARC p=reject

    Do not:

    • Send marketing through the transactional stream or IPs.
    • Buy, rent, or scrape lists; spam traps and complaints follow.
    • Send from a no-reply address that bounces replies; Microsoft asks for a valid, reply-capable From or Reply-To.
    • Jump to p=reject before reports show every legitimate sender aligned.

    Practical examples¶

    TaskFlow's email DNS records¶

    ; Inbound mail (a hosted mailbox provider)
example.com.                    MX     10 mx1.mail.example.net.
example.com.                    MX     20 mx2.mail.example.net.
; Only the mailbox provider sends directly as @example.com
example.com.                    TXT    "v=spf1 include:_spf.mail.example.net -all"

; Transactional mail through Amazon SES, From: reminders@example.com
; Easy DKIM: three CNAMEs; copy the exact names and targets from the SES console
<token1>._domainkey.example.com. CNAME  <token1>.dkim.amazonses.com.
<token2>._domainkey.example.com. CNAME  <token2>.dkim.amazonses.com.
<token3>._domainkey.example.com. CNAME  <token3>.dkim.amazonses.com.
; Custom MAIL FROM, so SPF aligns with example.com
bounce.example.com.             MX     10 feedback-smtp.us-east-1.amazonses.com.
bounce.example.com.             TXT    "v=spf1 include:amazonses.com ~all"

; Newsletters from a separate subdomain and provider
news.example.com.               TXT    "v=spf1 include:_spf.news-provider.example.net -all"
nl1._domainkey.news.example.com. TXT   "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkq...truncated...IDAQAB"

; One DMARC policy for the domain and its subdomains
_dmarc.example.com.             TXT    "v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; sp=quarantine; np=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com"
    • SES's MAIL FROM subdomain must be used only for that purpose (not for sending from or receiving mail) and must have exactly one MX record.
    • news.example.com has its own SPF budget and its own reputation, and the _dmarc record at example.com covers it through sp.
    • np=reject tells receivers to reject mail from subdomains that do not exist, which phishers like to invent; receivers that only implement RFC 7489 ignore it.

    Sending through SES with unsubscribe headers (Node.js)¶

    // src/email/send-reminder.js: send a "tasks due today" email via Amazon SES (API v2)
import { SESv2Client, SendEmailCommand } from "@aws-sdk/client-sesv2";
import { isSuppressed } from "./suppressions.js";
import { renderReminder } from "./templates.js";

const ses = new SESv2Client({ region: process.env.AWS_REGION });

export async function sendReminder({ to, tasks, unsubscribeUrl }) {
  if (await isSuppressed(to, "reminders")) return null; // bounced, complained, or opted out

  const { subject, text, html } = renderReminder(tasks, unsubscribeUrl);
  const result = await ses.send(new SendEmailCommand({
    FromEmailAddress: "TaskFlow <reminders@example.com>",
    ReplyToAddresses: ["support@example.com"],
    Destination: { ToAddresses: [to] },
    ConfigurationSetName: "taskflow-transactional", // publishes bounce and complaint events
    Content: {
      Simple: {
        Subject: { Data: subject },
        Body: { Text: { Data: text }, Html: { Data: html } },
        Headers: [
          { Name: "List-Unsubscribe", Value: `<${unsubscribeUrl}>` },
          { Name: "List-Unsubscribe-Post", Value: "List-Unsubscribe=One-Click" },
        ],
      },
    },
  }));
  return result.MessageId; // store it to match later bounce and complaint events
}
    • The unsubscribe headers go on optional mail such as reminders, never on password resets or security alerts.
    • RFC 8058 requires both headers to be covered by the DKIM signature, and receivers should not offer one-click unsubscribe otherwise. Check the h= list in the DKIM-Signature of a delivered message to confirm your provider signs them.
    • Run sends from a background job with retries rather than inside the HTTP request, as described in background jobs and scheduling.

    The one-click unsubscribe endpoint (Express)¶

    // src/routes/unsubscribe.js: RFC 8058 one-click unsubscribe plus a page for humans
import express from "express";
import { verifyUnsubscribeToken } from "../email/tokens.js";
import { Preferences } from "../models/preferences.js";

export const router = express.Router();
const CATEGORIES = new Map([["reminders", "task reminders"], ["digests", "weekly digests"]]);

async function optOut(req) {
  const { category } = req.params;
  if (!CATEGORIES.has(category)) return false; // never use raw input as a field name
  const userId = verifyUnsubscribeToken(req.query.t, category); // signed, expiring token
  if (!userId) return false;
  await Preferences.updateOne(
    { userId },
    { $set: { [`email.${category}`]: false } },
    { upsert: true },
  );
  return true;
}

// Mail clients POST the body "List-Unsubscribe=One-Click" with no cookies or login
router.post("/unsubscribe/:category", async (req, res) => {
  res.status((await optOut(req)) ? 200 : 400).end(); // no redirect: RFC 8058 forbids one
});

// The visible link in the email body opens a confirmation page instead
router.get("/unsubscribe/:category", (req, res) => {
  const label = CATEGORIES.get(req.params.category);
  if (!label) return res.status(404).end();
  res.send(`<form method="post"><button>Unsubscribe from ${label}</button></form>`);
});

    The token in the URL identifies the user and category, because the request carries no cookies or authentication. Categories come from an allowlist, so URL input is never echoed into HTML or used as a database field name. The GET route does not unsubscribe by itself: link scanners and prefetchers follow links in emails, and must not opt users out. The form has no action, so it posts back to the same URL, token included.

    Handling SES bounce and complaint events (Lambda)¶

    // src/email/ses-events.js: SES events delivered through SNS to a Lambda function
import { Suppressions } from "../models/suppressions.js";

export async function handler(event) {
  for (const record of event.Records) {
    const msg = JSON.parse(record.Sns.Message);
    const type = msg.eventType ?? msg.notificationType; // configuration set events vs identity notifications

    if (type === "Bounce" && msg.bounce.bounceType === "Permanent") {
      for (const r of msg.bounce.bouncedRecipients) {
        await suppress(r.emailAddress, "hard-bounce", msg.mail.messageId);
      }
    } else if (type === "Complaint") {
      for (const r of msg.complaint.complainedRecipients) {
        await suppress(r.emailAddress, "complaint", msg.mail.messageId);
      }
    }
    // Transient bounces are retried by SES; count them and suppress after repeats
  }
}

async function suppress(email, reason, messageId) {
  await Suppressions.updateOne(
    { email: email.toLowerCase() },
    { $setOnInsert: { reason, messageId, createdAt: new Date() } },
    { upsert: true },
  );
}

    SES reports bounceType as Permanent, Transient, or Undetermined. Many mailbox providers redact who complained, so complainedRecipients lists every recipient of that message on the complaining domain; for one-recipient transactional mail that is exactly the person.

    Reading the verdict on a received message¶

    ; Example header added by a receiving server
Authentication-Results: mx.receiver.example.org;
       dkim=pass header.i=@example.com header.s=s1;
       spf=pass smtp.mailfrom=bounce.example.com;
       dmarc=pass (p=QUARANTINE sp=QUARANTINE) header.from=example.com

    Send a test message to a mailbox you control and open the raw headers: all three should pass, and the domains after header.i, smtp.mailfrom, and header.from should share example.com.

    Reading an aggregate report¶

    ; Excerpt of a DMARC aggregate report (XML), one record
<record>
  <row>
    <source_ip>203.0.113.77</source_ip>
    <count>1250</count>
    <policy_evaluated><disposition>none</disposition><dkim>fail</dkim><spf>fail</spf></policy_evaluated>
  </row>
  <identifiers><header_from>example.com</header_from></identifiers>
  <auth_results>
    <dkim><domain>crm.example.net</domain><result>pass</result></dkim>
    <spf><domain>crm.example.net</domain><result>pass</result></spf>
  </auth_results>
</record>

    1,250 messages claiming to be from example.com came from a CRM: SPF and DKIM passed for the CRM's own domain, but neither aligned, so DMARC failed (disposition is none only because the policy was still p=none). Enabling the CRM's custom DKIM for example.com, or moving it to crm.example.com, fixes it before enforcement.

    Best practices¶

    • Authenticate everything: DKIM with your domain, a custom Return-Path for SPF alignment, and DMARC with rua reporting.
    • Roll DMARC out from none to reject on evidence from reports, and give non-sending domains p=reject from day one.
    • Separate transactional and marketing mail by subdomain and stream.
    • Keep each name's SPF under 10 lookups; prefer subdomains to flattening.
    • Use 2048-bit DKIM keys and rotate them through selectors.
    • Add one-click unsubscribe to optional mail and honor it within two days.
    • Suppress hard bounces and complaints immediately, in your own store as well as the provider's.
    • Watch Postmaster Tools, your provider's reputation metrics, and DMARC reports; alert on bounce and complaint rates.
    • Use dedicated IPs only with steady volume and a warm-up plan.

    In production¶

    • Password resets go to spam at Gmail only. Detect: raw headers show dmarc=fail or spf=pass with smtp.mailfrom on the provider's domain; Postmaster Tools shows a rising spam rate. Cause: no alignment (default provider Return-Path and DKIM signed with the provider's domain), or a marketing stream sharing the domain and IPs. Fix: custom DKIM and Return-Path for example.com, separate marketing onto its own subdomain and stream.
    • Every message fails SPF after a new tool was added. Detect: reports and headers show spf=permerror. Cause: the new include pushed the record past 10 lookups, or a second SPF record was created. Fix: merge into one record, remove unused mechanisms, and move the tool to its own subdomain.
    • Mail to Outlook.com addresses bounces with 550 5.7.515. Detect: bounce events carry that code for hotmail.com and outlook.com recipients. Cause: the domain sends more than 5,000 messages a day and fails SPF, DKIM, or aligned DMARC. Fix: make both SPF and DKIM pass and publish an aligned DMARC record.
    • The provider pauses sending. Detect: an account review or pause notice; SES's Reputation.BounceRate above 5%. Cause: sending to stale or imported addresses, or ignoring bounce events. Fix: suppress bounces automatically, confirm addresses with double opt-in, and stop sending to inactive recipients.
    • Legitimate mail is rejected right after moving to p=reject. Detect: complaints from a team whose tool's mail vanished; reports show a source with failing alignment. Cause: a sender that was never fixed during the p=none phase. Fix: step back to quarantine, fix the sender, and only advance when reports are clean.
    • Email stops entirely after a DNS migration. Detect: DKIM fail (no key) or missing SPF in headers. Cause: DKIM selectors, SPF, or the MAIL FROM MX were not copied to the new DNS provider. Fix: restore them; include underscore names in every zone inventory (see DNS migrations and troubleshooting).

    Important terminology¶

    • Header From: The address the recipient sees; DMARC protects its domain.
    • Envelope MAIL FROM (Return-Path): The bounce address used in SMTP; SPF checks its domain.
    • SPF: A TXT record listing which servers may send for a domain.
    • DKIM: A signature over headers and body, verified with a public key published under a selector.
    • Selector: The label (s=) that tells receivers which DKIM key to fetch.
    • DMARC: A policy that requires SPF or DKIM to pass and align with the header From domain, plus reporting.
    • Alignment: The authenticated domain matching the From domain, exactly (strict) or by organizational domain (relaxed).
    • Aggregate report (rua): Daily XML summaries of authentication results per sending IP.
    • One-click unsubscribe: RFC 8058's List-Unsubscribe-Post mechanism that lets mail clients unsubscribe with one POST.
    • Hard and soft bounce: Permanent and temporary delivery failures.
    • Feedback loop: A mailbox provider's channel for reporting spam complaints to the sender.
    • IP warm-up: Gradually increasing volume on a new IP to build reputation.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Believing SPF protects the visible From address; it checks the envelope sender, and only DMARC ties authentication to the From.
    • Publishing two SPF records, or one with more than 10 lookups.
    • Letting providers sign with their own domain, so DKIM passes but DMARC fails.
    • Jumping to p=reject without reading aggregate reports.
    • Sending newsletters and password resets from the same stream.
    • Treating "accepted by the provider" as "delivered to the inbox".
    • Unsubscribing users on GET requests that link scanners trigger.
    • Assuming a dedicated IP improves deliverability by itself.
