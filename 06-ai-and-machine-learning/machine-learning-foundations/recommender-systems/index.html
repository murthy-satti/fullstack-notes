    Recommender Systems¶

    A recommender system chooses, for one user at one moment, a short list from a catalog too large to browse: the videos on a home page, the products under "customers also bought", or, in TaskFlow, the project templates offered when someone starts a new project. This lesson builds that TaskFlow template recommender from simple to modern.

    It covers implicit feedback, the two-stage retrieval and ranking architecture, collaborative filtering (item-item similarity and matrix factorization), content-based filtering, embedding-based retrieval with a two-tower model trained from scratch in NumPy, offline evaluation with recall@k and NDCG, and the cold-start problem for new items and new users. Embedding models and vector indexes are covered in depth in vector databases and indexes.

    Why it matters¶

    Recommendations decide what users discover. For TaskFlow, a good template suggestion gets a team productive in minutes and spreads the templates that work; a bad one teaches users to ignore the panel. The same techniques power "related tasks", "suggested assignees", and "docs you may need" in the project-knowledge assistant.

    Recommenders also fail in characteristic ways that are easy to miss offline: they recommend only what is already popular, never surface new items, trap users in more of the same, and train on their own output. Knowing the mechanics (what each method can and cannot see) is how you avoid shipping a system that looks accurate on a dashboard and feels stale to users.

    Real-world usage¶

    • YouTube: two stages. Covington, Adams, and Sargin, Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations (RecSys 2016), describe one of the largest recommenders in industry, split according to the classic two-stage information retrieval dichotomy: a deep candidate generation model, then a separate deep ranking model.
    • YouTube: two-tower retrieval and sampling bias. Yi and others, Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations (RecSys 2019), train two-tower networks with in-batch negatives, show that the batch loss is biased against popular items under a power-law item distribution, correct it with streaming item-frequency estimates, and deploy the result to retrieve from tens of millions of videos.
    • Amazon. Smith and Linden, Two Decades of Recommender Systems at Amazon.com (IEEE Internet Computing, 2017), revisit their original article on Amazon's recommendations and how the approach changed as the store grew.
    • Implicit feedback. Hu, Koren, and Volinsky, Collaborative Filtering for Implicit Feedback Datasets (ICDM 2008), point out that clicks and views carry no negative feedback (a user who never watched a show may simply not know it) and treat observations as preferences with varying confidence, in a system that recommended television shows.
    • Cold start by design. Volkovs, Yu, and Poutanen, DropoutNet: Addressing Cold Start in Recommender Systems (NIPS 2017), show that neural recommenders can be trained explicitly for cold start by applying dropout to the preference inputs, so content features learn to stand in for missing history.

    Popular tools and providers: scikit-learn and SciPy sparse matrices for baselines, the implicit library (ALS and BPR), LightFM, TensorFlow Recommenders and TorchRec for two-tower and ranking models, LightGBM's LGBMRanker for learning to rank, FAISS, ScaNN, and vector databases for approximate nearest-neighbour retrieval; managed options include Amazon Personalize and Google Cloud's AI Commerce Search (formerly Vertex AI Search for commerce), which covers product recommendations as well as search.

    How it works¶

    Feedback: explicit and implicit¶

    Explicit feedback (stars, thumbs) says what users think but is rare. Implicit feedback (used, opened, starred, completed, dwell time) is plentiful but one-sided: an unused template may be unwanted or simply unseen. TaskFlow records "team created a project from this template", a strong positive signal. Everything else is unknown, not negative, which is why recommenders sample negatives and evaluate by ranking rather than by classification accuracy.

    Two stages: retrieval, then ranking¶

    A large catalog cannot be scored with an expensive model for every request. Production recommenders work in stages:

    Stage Job Typical method Measured by
    Candidate generation (retrieval) Find a few hundred plausible items fast Two-tower plus ANN, item-item CF, popularity, content Recall@k
    Ranking Order the candidates precisely Gradient-boosted trees or neural rankers with many features NDCG, AUC, online metrics
    Re-ranking Apply product rules Filters, diversity, freshness, business constraints Guardrail metrics

    Retrieval only needs to keep the right items in the candidate set; ranking spends its feature budget on a few hundred items instead of the whole catalog. For a catalog of 600 templates TaskFlow could score everything, but the structure is the same at a million items.

    Collaborative filtering¶

    Collaborative filtering (CF) recommends from behaviour alone: users who used the same things in the past will want similar things next.

    • Item-item CF: compute the similarity between items from the users who used both (cosine similarity between columns of the user-item matrix), then score each item by its summed similarity to the user's history. It is simple, explainable ("because you used Sprint board"), and easy to precompute.
    • Matrix factorization: approximate the sparse user-item matrix as the product of a user-factor matrix and an item-factor matrix with a few dozen dimensions. Each factor captures a hidden pattern, such as "teams that run formal sprints", that no text describes. Methods include truncated SVD, alternating least squares weighted for implicit feedback, and BPR.

    CF's blind spots are the reverse of its strengths: it knows nothing about an item until people use it (item cold start), nothing about a user without history (user cold start), and it amplifies whatever is already popular.

    Content-based filtering¶

    Content-based filtering describes items by their own features (title, tags, topic, category, author, embeddings of the description) and builds each user's profile from the items they used. Scoring is a similarity between the profile and each item. It handles new items from day one and can explain itself ("matches your engineering templates"), but it only finds more of what the user already did, and it cannot see what the text does not say: two "Sprint board" templates may differ completely in quality.

    Hybrids combine both: blend scores, use content features inside a factorization model, or let a neural model learn from IDs and content together. The two-tower model below is one such hybrid.

    Embedding-based retrieval and the two-tower model¶

    A two-tower model has a user (query) tower and an item (candidate) tower. Each maps its inputs to an embedding of the same size, and the score is their dot product. Because the towers do not interact until that dot product, item embeddings can be computed offline, stored in an approximate nearest-neighbour index, and searched in milliseconds with the user embedding computed at request time.

    Training details that matter:

    • In-batch negatives. Each training example is a (user, item) pair that happened. The other items in the same batch serve as negatives, and a softmax over the batch is the loss. It is cheap and works well.
    • Sampling-bias correction. Popular items appear in many batches, so they are used as negatives far more often than rare ones and get pushed down. Subtracting log(frequency) of each item from its logit during training (the "logQ correction") removes that bias.
    • ID embeddings and cold start. A learned ID embedding per item captures what content cannot, but new items have none. Training some examples with the ID hidden (ID dropout) teaches the content path to carry the prediction alone, which is what new items need.
    • No leakage from the target. The user features for a training pair must not include the target item itself.

    Serving is a vector search: see vector databases and indexes for HNSW and IVF indexes, recall trade-offs, and filtering. Text embeddings from a modern embedding model can replace TF-IDF as item features without changing the architecture.

    Ranking¶

    The ranking stage scores a few hundred candidates with a richer model, often gradient-boosted trees trained with a learning-to-rank objective (LightGBM's LGBMRanker with objective="lambdarank") or a neural model. It can use features retrieval cannot afford: how recently the team used a similar template, the project's size, the time of year, the candidate's retrieval scores from each source, and its historical click rate in this position. Labels come from logged impressions: shown and used, shown and ignored.

    Evaluation¶

    Offline, hold out each user's most recent interactions, recommend from the rest, and check whether the held-out items appear near the top.

    Metric Measures Note
    Recall@k Share of held-out items found in the top k The main retrieval metric
    Precision@k Share of the top k that were held-out items Bounded by how many items are held out
    NDCG@k Recall weighted by position (higher is better) Rewards putting hits at the top
    MRR Average of 1 / rank of the first hit When one good item is enough
    Coverage Share of the catalog ever recommended Low values mean popularity bias
    Novelty and diversity How unpopular or varied the lists are Guardrails against "more of the same"

    Split by time, not at random: a random split lets the model see a user's future to predict their past. Exclude items the user already has from the candidates, compare everything against a popularity baseline, and report cold-start segments separately.

    Online, the real measure is behaviour: click-through and adoption rate of recommended templates, projects that stay active, and longer-term retention, compared in an A/B test. Offline gains often shrink online because offline data only contains items the old system showed (exposure bias), and a model trained on its own recommendations keeps reinforcing them (feedback loops). Keep a small share of traffic for exploration (random or diverse candidates) so the logs contain evidence about items the current model would never show.

    Cold start¶

    Case What is missing Strategies
    New item Interaction history Content features or embeddings, ID dropout in training, a guaranteed exploration slot, editorial picks
    New user History Popular items in the user's context (team size, industry), an onboarding question ("what do you manage?"), session-based signals from the first clicks
    New system Everything Popularity and rules first; log impressions from day one so a model has data later

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation Approach
    A small catalog and little data Popularity per segment, rules, and editorial lists
    Plenty of interactions, mostly known items Item-item CF or matrix factorization
    Many new or long-tail items, rich item text Content-based or a hybrid with content features
    Large catalog, many signals, strict latency Two-tower retrieval with ANN, then a ranking model
    Recommendations with legal or safety impact Transparent rules and human review; a model only as an input
    Very few users or items Do not build a recommender; a curated list is better

    Practical examples¶

    Tested with Python 3.12, NumPy 2.5, pandas 3.1.1, and scikit-learn 1.9.1 in November 2026. The data is synthetic: each simulated user has a preference over 8 topics, visible in template titles, and over 6 hidden "ways of working" that no text reveals, and template popularity follows a long-tailed distribution.

    A synthetic template catalog and usage log¶

    # ml/make_interactions.py: synthetic TaskFlow template catalog and who used which template
from pathlib import Path

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
TOPICS = {
    "engineering": ["sprint", "bug", "release", "code review", "incident", "API"],
    "marketing": ["campaign", "content", "SEO", "launch", "newsletter", "social"],
    "sales": ["pipeline", "lead", "deal", "account", "quota", "demo"],
    "design": ["wireframe", "user research", "design review", "prototype", "brand", "UX"],
    "hr": ["onboarding", "hiring", "interview", "offboarding", "review cycle", "policy"],
    "finance": ["budget", "invoice", "expense", "forecast", "audit", "payroll"],
    "support": ["ticket", "escalation", "knowledge base", "SLA", "customer", "FAQ"],
    "operations": ["vendor", "inventory", "facilities", "procurement", "travel", "office"],
}
KINDS = ["template", "checklist", "board", "tracker", "playbook", "plan"]
names = list(TOPICS)

n_items, n_new, n_users = 600, 60, 2_000
item_topic = rng.integers(0, len(names), n_items)
item_style = rng.integers(0, 6, n_items)                          # hidden: way of working, not in the text
items = pd.DataFrame({
    "item": np.arange(n_items),
    "topic": [names[t] for t in item_topic],
    "title": [f"{rng.choice(TOPICS[names[t]])} {rng.choice(KINDS)}".capitalize() for t in item_topic],
    "is_new": np.arange(n_items) >= n_items - n_new,              # added after the training cutoff
})
popularity = rng.pareto(1.2, n_items) + 1                          # a long tail of rarely used items

rows = []
for user in range(n_users):
    taste = rng.dirichlet(np.full(len(names), 0.15))              # most users care about 1-2 topics
    style = rng.dirichlet(np.full(6, 0.2))                        # and work in one or two styles
    weights = taste[item_topic] * style[item_style] * popularity ** 0.7
    n_events = int(rng.integers(8, 40))
    chosen = rng.choice(n_items, size=n_events, replace=False, p=weights / weights.sum())
    old = chosen[~items["is_new"].to_numpy()[chosen]]
    new = chosen[items["is_new"].to_numpy()[chosen]]
    order = list(rng.permutation(old)) + list(new)                # new items can only come last
    for step, item in enumerate(order):
        rows.append((user, int(item), step))

events = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["user", "item", "step"])
Path("data").mkdir(exist_ok=True)
items.to_csv("data/items.csv", index=False)
events.to_csv("data/events.csv", index=False)
print(f"{len(items)} items ({n_new} new), {n_users} users, {len(events)} events")
print(items.sample(4, random_state=1)[["item", "topic", "title"]].to_string(index=False))

    The split, item features, and the metrics¶

    # ml/rec_common.py: leave-last-two-out split, item text features, and top-k evaluation
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

items = pd.read_csv("data/items.csv")
events = pd.read_csv("data/events.csv")
n_users, n_items = events["user"].max() + 1, len(items)
is_new = items["is_new"].to_numpy()

last = events.groupby("user")["step"].transform("max")
test = events[events["step"] > last - 2]                        # each user's two most recent
train = events[(events["step"] <= last - 2) & ~is_new[events["item"]]]   # new items: unseen

X = np.zeros((n_users, n_items), dtype=np.float32)              # user x item, 1 = used
X[train["user"], train["item"]] = 1.0
truth = test.groupby("user")["item"].apply(set)

text = items["title"] + " " + items["topic"]                    # what a new template comes with
content = TfidfVectorizer().fit_transform(text).toarray().astype(np.float32)
content /= np.linalg.norm(content, axis=1, keepdims=True)


def evaluate(scores, k=10):
    """Recall@k and NDCG@k over each user's two held-out items, plus catalog coverage."""
    scores = np.where(X > 0, -np.inf, scores)                   # never re-recommend used items
    top = np.argsort(-scores, axis=1)[:, :k]
    recall, ndcg, cold_hits, cold_total = [], [], 0, 0
    for u, held in truth.items():
        ranked = list(top[u])
        hits = [1 if i in held else 0 for i in ranked]
        recall.append(sum(hits) / len(held))
        ideal = sum(1 / np.log2(r + 2) for r in range(len(held)))
        ndcg.append(sum(h / np.log2(r + 2) for r, h in enumerate(hits)) / ideal)
        for i in held:
            if is_new[i]:
                cold_total += 1
                cold_hits += i in ranked
    return {f"recall@{k}": np.mean(recall), f"NDCG@{k}": np.mean(ndcg),
            "coverage": len(np.unique(top)) / n_items, "new-item recall": cold_hits / cold_total}

    Every user's two most recent templates are the test set. The 60 new templates never appear in training: they model templates published after the cutoff, the item cold-start case. Item features are TF-IDF vectors of title and topic, normalized so dot products are cosine similarities.

    Popularity, collaborative filtering, and content-based filtering¶

    # ml/recommend.py: popularity, item-item collaborative filtering, matrix factorization, content-based
import numpy as np
from sklearn.decomposition import TruncatedSVD

from rec_common import X, content, evaluate, n_users, test, train

print(f"train events {len(train)}, test events {len(test)}, users {n_users}")
results = {}

popularity = X.sum(axis=0)                                       # how many users used each item
results["most popular"] = np.tile(popularity, (n_users, 1))

norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=0) + 1e-9
item_sim = (X.T @ X) / np.outer(norms, norms)                    # cosine between item columns
np.fill_diagonal(item_sim, 0)
results["item-item CF"] = X @ item_sim                           # sum of similarities to my items

svd = TruncatedSVD(n_components=32, random_state=0).fit(X)      # low-rank user and item factors
V = svd.components_.T                                            # item factors (n_items x 32)
results["matrix factorization"] = (X @ V) @ V.T

profile = X @ content                                            # the user's taste in text space
results["content-based"] = profile @ content.T

print(f"{'model':22} {'recall@10':>9} {'NDCG@10':>8} {'coverage':>8} {'new-item recall':>15}")
for name, scores in results.items():
    m = evaluate(scores)
    print(f"{name:22} {m['recall@10']:9.3f} {m['NDCG@10']:8.3f} {m['coverage']:8.2f} "
          f"{m['new-item recall']:15.3f}")

    $ python recommend.py
train events 41548, test events 4000, users 2000
model                  recall@10  NDCG@10 coverage new-item recall
most popular               0.019    0.013     0.03           0.000
item-item CF               0.113    0.081     0.90           0.000
matrix factorization       0.106    0.079     0.88           0.000
content-based              0.077    0.041     1.00           0.079

    Reading the results:

    • Popularity is a weak baseline here (recall@10 0.019) because users care about different topics, and it recommends the same 3% of the catalog to everyone.
    • Item-item CF and matrix factorization are about six times better. They see the hidden "way of working" that no title reveals, because users who share it use the same templates.
    • Content-based is weaker on known items (0.077) but is the only one of the four that ever recommends a new template (new-item recall 0.079).
    • Coverage shows how much of the catalog each method uses; all but popularity spread recommendations widely.

    The matrix factorization here is truncated SVD of the 0/1 matrix, a strong and simple baseline; weighted ALS (the implicit library) usually does better on real implicit data.

    A two-tower model in NumPy¶

    The model is small enough to train with plain NumPy and hand-written gradients, which makes every moving part visible. In production use TensorFlow Recommenders, TorchRec, or PyTorch.

    # ml/two_tower.py: a small two-tower retrieval model in NumPy, trained with in-batch negatives
import argparse

import numpy as np

from rec_common import X, content, evaluate, n_items, train

parser = argparse.ArgumentParser()
parser.add_argument("--id-dropout", type=float, default=0.3)   # share of examples trained on text only
parser.add_argument("--no-logq", action="store_true")          # turn off the popularity correction
args = parser.parse_args()
ID_DROPOUT, LOGQ = args.id_dropout, not args.no_logq
D, BATCH, EPOCHS, LR = 32, 256, 15, 0.01
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_words = content.shape[1]
pairs = train[["user", "item"]].to_numpy()
freq = np.bincount(pairs[:, 1], minlength=n_items) + 1
log_q = np.log(freq / freq.sum())                             # how often each item is a negative


def user_input(history):
    """User tower input: the items used (multi-hot) and their average text, both length-normalized."""
    n = np.maximum(history.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), 1)
    return np.hstack([history / n, (history @ content) / n])


params = {"Wu": rng.normal(0, 0.05, (n_items + n_words, D)),   # user tower: one linear layer
          "Wi": rng.normal(0, 0.05, (n_words, D)),             # item tower: text ...
          "E": np.zeros((n_items, D))}                         # ... plus a learned ID embedding
moments = {k: (np.zeros_like(v), np.zeros_like(v)) for k, v in params.items()}
step = 0
for epoch in range(EPOCHS):
    order, total = rng.permutation(len(pairs)), 0.0
    for start in range(0, len(pairs), BATCH):
        u, i = pairs[order[start:start + BATCH]].T
        history = X[u].copy()
        history[np.arange(len(u)), i] = 0                     # leave the target out of the history
        keep_id = (rng.random(len(u)) >= ID_DROPOUT)[:, None]  # sometimes hide the ID: cold-start practice
        xu, xi = user_input(history), content[i]
        eu = xu @ params["Wu"]
        ei = xi @ params["Wi"] + keep_id * params["E"][i]
        logits = eu @ ei.T                                     # other items in the batch act as negatives
        if LOGQ:
            logits -= log_q[i][None, :]                        # correct for popular items' extra negatives
        logits -= logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        prob = np.exp(logits) / np.exp(logits).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        total -= np.log(np.diag(prob) + 1e-12).sum()
        g = (prob - np.eye(len(u))) / len(u)                   # gradient of the softmax loss
        g_eu, g_ei = g @ ei, g.T @ eu
        grads = {"Wu": xu.T @ g_eu, "Wi": xi.T @ g_ei, "E": np.zeros_like(params["E"])}
        np.add.at(grads["E"], i, keep_id * g_ei)
        step += 1
        for k, (m, v) in moments.items():                      # Adam
            m[:] = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * grads[k]
            v[:] = 0.999 * v + 0.001 * grads[k] ** 2
            params[k] -= LR * (m / (1 - 0.9 ** step)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999 ** step)) + 1e-8)
    if epoch in (0, EPOCHS - 1):
        print(f"epoch {epoch + 1:2}: mean in-batch loss {total / len(pairs):.3f}")

user_emb = user_input(X) @ params["Wu"]                       # computed per request in production
item_emb = content @ params["Wi"] + params["E"]                # precomputed, indexed for ANN search
m = evaluate(user_emb @ item_emb.T)
print(f"two-tower: recall@10 {m['recall@10']:.3f}  NDCG@10 {m['NDCG@10']:.3f}  "
      f"coverage {m['coverage']:.2f}  new-item recall {m['new-item recall']:.3f}")

    What each part does:

    • The user tower sees the multi-hot list of templates used (the collaborative signal) and their average text (the content signal), through one linear layer. During training the target template is removed from the history so the model cannot simply look it up.
    • The item tower maps the template's text through a linear layer and adds a learned ID embedding. New templates have an all-zero ID embedding, so their vectors come from text alone.
    • The loss is a softmax over the batch, with gradients written out (prob - I), and Adam updates the three parameter matrices.
    • Serving computes item_emb once for the whole catalog, ready for an ANN index, and only user_emb per request.

two-tower: recall@10 0.080  NDCG@10 0.050  coverage 0.92  new-item recall 0.001

    $ python two_tower.py --id-dropout 0
epoch  1: mean in-batch loss 4.805
epoch 15: mean in-batch loss 3.377
two-tower: recall@10 0.088  NDCG@10 0.060  coverage 0.93  new-item recall 0.002

    $ python two_tower.py
epoch  1: mean in-batch loss 4.893
epoch 15: mean in-batch loss 3.819
two-tower: recall@10 0.164  NDCG@10 0.099  coverage 1.00  new-item recall 0.106
    • Without ID dropout, the model leans on per-item IDs, memorizes the training pairs, and generalizes worse than plain item-item CF; new templates, which have no trained ID, are almost never recommended.
    • The logQ correction adds a little on its own (recall@10 0.080 to 0.088).
    • With 30% ID dropout, the text path has to work on its own for some examples. The result is the best model on both known items (0.164, against 0.113 for item-item CF) and new items (0.106, against 0.079 for content-based). Its training loss is higher; the metric that matters is better. That is a regularization lesson as much as a recommender one.

    These numbers come from synthetic data with a known structure; on real data, compare the same way against the same baselines before adopting a more complex model.

    Best practices¶

    • Start with popularity per segment and item-item CF; adopt anything more complex only when it beats them on the same split.
    • Treat implicit feedback as positive-only; sample negatives and evaluate by ranking.
    • Split by time, hold out each user's latest interactions, and exclude already-used items from the candidates.
    • Report recall@k and NDCG@k with coverage, and report new-item and new-user segments separately.
    • Use a two-stage design for large catalogs: high-recall retrieval from several sources, then a feature-rich ranker.
    • In two-tower training, remove the target from the user features, correct for in-batch sampling bias, and train part of the time without IDs.
    • Keep an exploration slot so new and long-tail items earn data.
    • Log impressions with position and source, not just clicks; they are the ranker's training data.
    • Apply permission filters before showing anything: never recommend a template or document the user cannot open.
    • Confirm offline gains with an A/B test on adoption, not only click-through.

    In production¶

    • The same few templates are recommended to everyone. Detect: catalog coverage and novelty fall; most clicks go to the top 1% of items. Cause: popularity bias from CF and in-batch training without correction, reinforced by a feedback loop. Fix: logQ correction, diversity re-ranking, an exploration share, and coverage as a guardrail metric.
    • New templates never get recommended. Detect: new items have near-zero impressions for weeks; new-item recall offline is zero. Cause: ID-only models with no content path. Fix: content features in the item tower, ID dropout during training, and a guaranteed exploration slot for fresh items.
    • Offline recall rose but the A/B test is flat. Detect: a new model wins offline and loses or ties online. Cause: exposure bias (logs only contain what the old model showed), a random instead of temporal split, or an offline metric unlinked to adoption. Fix: temporal splits, exploration traffic for unbiased data, and an agreed online success metric.
    • A user sees a recommendation for something they cannot access. Detect: support tickets, or 403 responses after clicks on recommended items. Cause: candidates from a global index without permission filtering. Fix: filter by permissions in retrieval and again before rendering, and test that path (see privacy, PII, and data retention).
    • Recommendations go stale after a product change. Detect: click-through drops after a catalog reorganization or a new feature. Cause: item embeddings and similarity tables computed before the change; retrieval index not rebuilt. Fix: scheduled retraining, index rebuilds tied to catalog changes, and freshness monitoring of embeddings.
    • Latency spikes on the home page. Detect: p99 of the recommendation call grows with catalog size. Cause: scoring every item with the ranking model, or exact nearest-neighbour search. Fix: two stages, an ANN index, a candidate cap, and caching per user for a few minutes.

    Important terminology¶

    • Implicit feedback: Behaviour such as use or clicks, positive-only.
    • Explicit feedback: Ratings or votes stated by the user.
    • Candidate generation (retrieval): Selecting a few hundred plausible items from the whole catalog.
    • Ranking: Ordering the candidates with a richer model.
    • Collaborative filtering: Recommending from patterns in many users' behaviour.
    • Item-item CF: Scoring items by similarity to items the user used.
    • Matrix factorization: Approximating the user-item matrix with low-dimensional user and item factors.
    • Content-based filtering: Recommending items whose features match the user's profile.
    • Two-tower model: Separate user and item networks whose embeddings meet in a dot product.
    • In-batch negatives: Using the other items in a training batch as negative examples.
    • LogQ correction: Subtracting each item's log sampling frequency from its logit to remove popularity bias.
    • Recall@k and NDCG@k: The share of held-out items in the top k, and that share weighted by position.
    • Cold start: Missing history for a new user or item.
    • Exposure bias: Logs that only contain items the previous system chose to show.
    • Feedback loop: A model trained on data shaped by its own earlier recommendations.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • Treating unused items as disliked.
    • Evaluating with a random split, so the model sees each user's future.
    • Recommending items the user already used and counting them as hits.
    • Comparing a new model only against other complex models, not against popularity and item-item CF.
    • Reporting average recall only, hiding that new items are never shown.
    • Training two-tower models with the target item inside the user features.
    • Ignoring permissions and privacy in candidate generation.
    • Assuming offline gains will carry over to the A/B test.
    • Judging a model by its training loss rather than the ranking metric.

    Revision questions¶

    2. Why do large recommenders use separate retrieval and ranking stages?
    3. A user has two held-out templates and one of them appears at rank 3 of the top 10. What are recall@10 and NDCG@10 for that user? (Recall 0.5; NDCG = (1 / log2(4)) / (1 + 1 / log2(3)) = 0.5 / 1.631 = 0.307.)
    4. Why can item-item CF not recommend a template published yesterday, and what can?
