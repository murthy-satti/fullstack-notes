    Privacy, PII, and Data Retention¶

    Every call to a hosted model copies data out of your system. The meeting notes TaskFlow summarizes contain names, email addresses, phone numbers, and sometimes things nobody meant to paste, such as card numbers or API keys. Those notes travel to the model provider, and from there into abuse-monitoring logs, caches, and stored state, while your own system adds copies in logs, traces, vector stores, and evaluation sets.

    This lesson shows how to map those copies, how to minimize and redact personal data before it leaves the API (with redaction code that was run and tested), what the major providers say about retention, training, and zero-retention options as of November 2026, how to keep logs and traces free of content, how to erase one user's data everywhere, and how data residency works in practice. It describes engineering controls; what the law requires of your product depends on where you operate, so confirm obligations with your privacy or legal team.

    Why it matters¶

    Privacy law and customer contracts turn these copies into obligations. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation requires personal data to be "limited to what is necessary" (data minimisation, Article 5), kept no longer than needed (storage limitation), and erased on request in many cases (Article 17). Enterprise customers ask the same questions in security reviews: which providers see our data, is it used for training, how long is it kept, where is it processed, and can you delete it?

    The technical risk is just as concrete. Prompts often contain more than the feature needs, logs keep content long after the request, and derived copies such as embeddings and cached answers are easy to forget when a user asks to be deleted. A secret pasted into a note and sent to a provider is a leaked secret. Answering these questions well is mostly a matter of design: send less, keep less, and know where every copy is.

    Real-world usage¶

    • OpenAI. The data controls guide states that data sent to the API "is not used to train or improve OpenAI models" unless you opt in, that abuse-monitoring logs are kept "for up to 30 days", and that Zero Data Retention and Modified Abuse Monitoring require prior approval. Stored responses are kept for at least 30 days unless store: false; files, vector stores, and conversations are kept until you delete them. The data residency guide lists ten regions; a project's region is chosen when it is created.
    • Anthropic. The API and data retention page says retained data "is never used for model training without your express permission" and offers ZDR by agreement. Anthropic's privacy center says inputs and outputs are deleted "within 30 days", except, for example, content flagged for policy violations (up to two years).
    • Google. The Gemini API terms (effective March 23, 2026) separate unpaid services, where content may be used to improve products and read by human reviewers, from paid services, where Google "doesn't use your prompts" or responses to improve products. Apps offered to users in the EEA, Switzerland, or the UK must use paid services. Google Cloud's Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (the new name of the Vertex AI Platform) describes the steps to reach zero data retention.
    • Microsoft and AWS. Azure's data privacy page says prompts and completions are not available to OpenAI and are not used to improve the models, and explains how deployment types change where processing happens. Amazon Bedrock's data retention settings let an account or project choose a retention mode per Region, including none for zero data retention.

    Popular tools and providers: Microsoft Presidio, Amazon Bedrock Guardrails sensitive-information filters, Google Cloud Model Armor with Sensitive Data Protection, OpenTelemetry's GenAI content-capture settings, and provider zero-retention and data-residency programs.

    How it works¶

    Map where the data goes¶

    Start with an inventory. For each AI feature, list every place a copy of the user's text can land, how long it stays, and how you would delete it.

    Copy Who controls it Typical lifetime How it is deleted
    Provider abuse logs Provider Up to about 30 days Expires; ZDR or modified monitoring prevents it
    Provider stored state You, through the API Until deleted Delete calls for responses, files, vector stores
    Provider prompt cache Provider Minutes to about a day Expires; some providers let you turn it off
    Answer cache You Minutes to hours TTL, plus delete by user key
    App logs and traces You Your retention setting TTL; keep content out of them
    Vector store chunks You Until the source changes Delete by source document id
    Evaluation and feedback sets You Often forever, by accident Delete by user id; prefer synthetic data

    Minimize before you redact¶

    The cheapest protection is not sending data at all. The meeting-notes summary needs the text of the notes, not the author's email address, the attendee list, or the workspace billing contact. Build prompts from the fields a feature needs, refer to people by role or internal id where the output allows it, and truncate long histories. Minimization also makes prompts shorter, so it saves tokens and latency.

    Redact what must be sent¶

    When personal data has to be in the text, there are three common treatments:

    Treatment Example Use when
    Masking [EMAIL] The model never needs to tell values apart
    Pseudonymization with placeholders [EMAIL_1], [EMAIL_2] The model must keep track of who is who, and you restore values afterwards
    Generalization "a customer in Berlin" instead of a street address Analytics or prompts where exact values add nothing

    Detection methods differ in what they catch:

    • Patterns with checksums find structured data reliably: emails, phone numbers, IP addresses, card numbers (Luhn check digit), IBANs (mod-97 checksum), and secret-key formats. They are fast and run in your process.
    • Named-entity recognition finds names, places, and organizations, which patterns cannot. Microsoft's Presidio combines both.
    • Provider filters such as Bedrock Guardrails' sensitive-information filters and Model Armor's Sensitive Data Protection mask data inside the provider's pipeline, which helps for output but means the input has already left your system.

    Pseudonymized text is still personal data under the GDPR (Recital 26), because you hold the key that maps placeholders back to people. Its value is that the provider, the logs, and the traces see placeholders; it does not take the feature out of scope.

    What providers keep, and for how long¶

    Provider policies change, so read the linked pages and your contract before relying on this summary, which reflects their published documentation as of November 2026:

    Provider Training on API data Retention of prompts and outputs Zero-retention option Residency controls
    OpenAI API No, unless you opt in Abuse logs up to 30 days; stored responses at least 30 days unless store: false; stateful objects until deleted ZDR or Modified Abuse Monitoring, after approval Regional projects and base URLs; processing in some regions, storage only in others
    Anthropic API Not without express permission Deleted within 30 days by default; flagged content up to 2 years ZDR by agreement; some models require 30-day retention See Anthropic's documentation
    Gemini API Unpaid: may be used, with human review. Paid: no Paid: logged "for a limited period" for abuse Not offered in the API terms; Google's enterprise platform documents ZDR Data "may be stored transiently or cached in any country"
    Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (Vertex AI) Not without prior permission Cache deleted within 24 hours; abuse logging may apply Disable caching, set store to false, avoid storing features; abuse logging does not apply under a Cloud Master Agreement See Google's location documentation
    Azure (models sold by Azure) No Flagged content in a per-geography abuse store; stateful features until deleted Modified abuse monitoring through a Limited Access application Standard (geography), DataZone (US or EU), Global
    Amazon Bedrock Providers cannot access prompts or logs Per-Region retention mode: none, default, or aws_review none mode, enforceable with service control policies Per Region; cross-region inference changes where data is processed

    Three patterns stand out. Zero retention applies to stateless calls only: anything you ask the provider to store (files, vector stores, batches, conversations) stays until you delete it. Zero retention usually needs approval or a contract, and some models are excluded from it (Anthropic lists models that require 30-day retention, and Bedrock models that require aws_review are unavailable in none mode). And content flagged for abuse can be kept longer even under zero-retention arrangements.

    Logging and tracing without content¶

    Logs and traces are the copies teams forget. They are replicated to log platforms, kept for months, and read by more people than the database. Log what a call did, not what it said:

    Log this Keep out of default logs
    Request id, trace id, feature name Prompt text and completions
    Model, token counts, latency, cost estimate Retrieved chunks and note text
    Guardrail decisions and policy version Tool arguments containing personal data
    Provider response id (for deletion and support) Raw user ids where a pseudonym will do

    The OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions (still marked Development) make content capture "gated by an explicit user opt-in". Instrumentations that support it read OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_CAPTURE_CONTENT, whose default is NO_CONTENT; the other values capture content on spans, events, or both. The conventions also describe an upload hook that stores content elsewhere and records only a reference, which lets you keep a small, access-controlled, short-lived content store for debugging while traces stay clean. Logging practice in general is covered in structured logging, and LLM tracing in LLM observability and tracing.

    Erasing one user's data¶

    A deletion request is a job, not a query. It needs the inventory above, an index of provider-side objects created for the user, retries, and a date by which every copy is gone.

    Two copies need special care. Backups expire on their own schedule, so a restore must re-apply the deletions recorded since the backup was taken (see backups). Fine-tuned model weights cannot forget one person's examples; keep personal data out of fine-tuning sets, or plan to retrain without it (see when to fine-tune).

    Data residency¶

    Residency questions are about two things: where data is stored and where it is processed, and providers treat them separately.

    • OpenAI offers ten regions for storage; inference runs in-region only in some of them (as of November 2026: the US, Europe, Australia, Japan, India, and the UAE). The region is fixed when a project is created, and requests go to a regional base URL such as https://eu.api.openai.com/v1. Some endpoints, such as files and vector stores, have no regional processing.
    • Azure deployment types decide processing location: standard deployments stay in the resource's geography, DataZone deployments within the US or the EU, and Global deployments anywhere the model is deployed, while stored data stays in the resource's geography.
    • Amazon Bedrock processes requests in the Region you call, unless cross-region inference routes them elsewhere; retained data is then stored in the destination Region.
    • Your own stores count too. A vector database, log platform, or backup bucket in another region undoes a careful provider choice.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Data in the prompt Minimum treatment
    Task titles and notes with occasional names Minimize fields; no content in logs; paid or enterprise API tier
    Contact details the output must refer to Placeholders with restore for allowed types
    Card numbers, bank details, secrets Mask, never restore; consider blocking the request and warning the user
    Health, legal, or HR content Zero-retention arrangement, residency matched to the customer, legal review
    Data from users in a regulated region Provider region and your own stores in that region; documented transfers

    When not to over-engineer:

    • Do not redact data the model needs to do the job; a summary full of placeholders the user cannot resolve is a broken feature.
    • Do not pay for zero retention on features that send no personal or confidential data; spend the effort on minimization instead.
    • Do not build a custom NER pipeline before measuring what your traffic contains; structured patterns often cover most of the risk.
    • Do not keep a "debug copy" of every prompt "just in case"; sample, redact, and expire it.

    Practical examples¶

    All code below is TypeScript for the TaskFlow API. It was type-checked with tsc, and the redaction and erasure code was run with the Node test runner; sample values use example.com addresses, the fictional 555-01xx phone range, documentation IP addresses, and published test card and IBAN numbers.

    A redactor with placeholders and checksums¶

    // src/privacy/redact.ts: replace personal data with numbered placeholders before text leaves the API
export type PiiType = "SECRET" | "EMAIL" | "IBAN" | "CARD" | "PHONE" | "IP";

// One vault per request: placeholder -> original value. It stays on the server.
export interface Vault {
  values: Map<string, string>;
  byValue: Map<string, string>;            // "TYPE:value" -> placeholder, so repeats reuse one placeholder
  counters: Partial<Record<PiiType, number>>;
}

export const createVault = (): Vault => ({ values: new Map(), byValue: new Map(), counters: {} });

// Card numbers carry a Luhn check digit; testing it removes most false positives.
export function luhnValid(raw: string): boolean {
  const digits = raw.replace(/[ -]/g, "");
  if (!/^\d{13,19}$/.test(digits)) return false;
  let sum = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < digits.length; i++) {
    let n = Number(digits[digits.length - 1 - i]);
    if (i % 2 === 1) {
      n *= 2;
      if (n > 9) n -= 9;
    }
    sum += n;
  }
  return sum % 10 === 0;
}

// IBANs carry a mod-97 checksum: move the first four characters to the end, letters become 10-35.
export function ibanValid(raw: string): boolean {
  const s = raw.replace(/ /g, "").toUpperCase();
  if (!/^[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{11,30}$/.test(s)) return false;
  let remainder = 0;
  for (const ch of s.slice(4) + s.slice(0, 4)) {
    const value = ch >= "A" && ch <= "Z" ? String(ch.charCodeAt(0) - 55) : ch;
    for (const digit of value) remainder = (remainder * 10 + Number(digit)) % 97;
  }
  return remainder === 1;
}

interface Detector {
  type: PiiType;
  pattern: RegExp;
  valid?: (match: string) => boolean;
}

// Order matters: specific patterns run before general ones that could match parts of them.
const DETECTORS: Detector[] = [
  { type: "SECRET", pattern: /\b(?:sk|pk|rk)[-_][A-Za-z0-9_-]{16,}|\bAKIA[0-9A-Z]{16}\b/g },
  { type: "EMAIL", pattern: /\b[A-Za-z0-9._%+-]+@[A-Za-z0-9.-]+\.[A-Za-z]{2,}\b/g },
  { type: "IBAN", pattern: /\b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}(?: ?[A-Z0-9]{4}){2,7}(?: ?[A-Z0-9]{1,3})?\b/g, valid: ibanValid },
  { type: "CARD", pattern: /\b\d(?:[ -]?\d){12,18}\b/g, valid: luhnValid },
  { type: "PHONE", pattern: /(?<![\w+])(?:\+\d{1,3}(?:[ .-]?\d{2,4}){2,4}|(?:\(\d{3}\)[ .-]?|\d{3}[ .-])\d{3}[ .-]\d{4})\b/g },
  { type: "IP", pattern: /\b(?:(?:25[0-5]|2[0-4]\d|1?\d?\d)\.){3}(?:25[0-5]|2[0-4]\d|1?\d?\d)\b/g },
];

const PLACEHOLDER = /\[([A-Z]+)_(\d+)\]/g;

export function redact(text: string, vault: Vault = createVault()): { text: string; vault: Vault } {
  // Text that already looks like a placeholder is neutralized, so restore() cannot be tricked.
  let out = text.replace(PLACEHOLDER, "($1_$2)");
  for (const detector of DETECTORS) {
    out = out.replace(detector.pattern, (match) => {
      if (detector.valid && !detector.valid(match)) return match;
      const key = `${detector.type}:${match}`;
      let placeholder = vault.byValue.get(key);
      if (placeholder === undefined) {
        const n = (vault.counters[detector.type] ?? 0) + 1;
        vault.counters[detector.type] = n;
        placeholder = `[${detector.type}_${n}]`;
        vault.byValue.set(key, placeholder);
        vault.values.set(placeholder, match);
      }
      return placeholder;
    });
  }
  return { text: out, vault };
}

// Put original values back, but only for the types this screen may show.
export function restore(text: string, vault: Vault, allow: PiiType[]): string {
  return text.replace(PLACEHOLDER, (placeholder: string, type: string) => {
    if (!allow.includes(type as PiiType)) return placeholder;
    return vault.values.get(placeholder) ?? placeholder;
  });
}

    Key decisions in this code:

    • Checksums before replacement. A 16-digit invoice number that fails the Luhn check stays as it is, which keeps false positives down.
    • Detector order. Secrets and emails run first, so later patterns cannot match pieces of them; cards run before phone numbers for the same reason.
    • Consistent placeholders. The same value gets the same placeholder across all notes in a request, so the model can tell that two notes mention the same person.
    • Placeholder injection. Text that already looks like [EMAIL_1] is rewritten to (EMAIL_1) first, so nobody can type a placeholder to make restore reveal a value.
    • Restore by type. The caller decides which types a screen may show; cards, IBANs, and secrets are never restored.

    The redactor in action¶

    // test/privacy.test.ts (excerpt): run with node --test
const NOTE =
  "Call Lee at +1 202 555 0199 or lee@example.com. Card 4111 1111 1111 1111 was charged twice; " +
  "refund to GB82 WEST 1234 5698 7654 32. Server 203.0.113.7 logged it. " +
  "Order 2026-000123 is unrelated. Key sk-EXAMPLE-NOT-A-REAL-KEY-000 was pasted by mistake.";

test("personal data becomes numbered placeholders; ordinary numbers stay", () => {
  const { text } = redact(NOTE);
  assert.equal(
    text,
    "Call Lee at [PHONE_1] or [EMAIL_1]. Card [CARD_1] was charged twice; refund to [IBAN_1]. " +
      "Server [IP_1] logged it. Order 2026-000123 is unrelated. Key [SECRET_1] was pasted by mistake.",
  );
});

test("restore brings back only the allowed types", () => {
  const { vault } = redact(NOTE);
  const modelOutput = "Email [EMAIL_1] and call [PHONE_1]; do not repeat [CARD_1].";
  assert.equal(
    restore(modelOutput, vault, ["EMAIL", "PHONE"]),
    "Email lee@example.com and call +1 202 555 0199; do not repeat [CARD_1].",
  );
});

test("names are not detected by patterns (use NER for them)", () => {
  assert.equal(redact("Lee Chen approved the budget.").text, "Lee Chen approved the budget.");
});

    All seven privacy tests passed on Node 22.12, including checksum rejection of a mistyped card and IBAN, placeholder reuse across notes, and the placeholder-injection case. The last test above documents a limit rather than a feature: patterns do not find names, so a feature that must hide names needs named-entity recognition as well.

    Summarizing with redaction and a residency base URL¶

    // src/privacy/summarize-private.ts: redact notes, summarize them, and restore only what the user may see
import OpenAI from "openai";
import { createVault, redact, restore } from "./redact.js";

const client = new OpenAI({
  // A project created in a residency region uses that region's base URL, for example the EU one.
  baseURL: process.env.OPENAI_BASE_URL ?? "https://api.openai.com/v1",
});

export async function summarizeMeetingNotes(notes: string[]) {
  const model = process.env.CHAT_MODEL; // pick from the provider's current model list
  if (!model) throw new Error("CHAT_MODEL is not set");

  const vault = createVault(); // shared across notes, so one person keeps one placeholder
  const redacted = notes.map((note) => redact(note, vault).text);

  const response = await client.responses.create({
    model,
    instructions:
      "Summarize the meeting notes as action items. Keep placeholders such as [EMAIL_1] exactly as written.",
    input: redacted.join("\n---\n"),
    store: false, // no stored copy of the response on the provider side
  });

  // Contact details may be shown to the signed-in user; cards, IBANs, and secrets never come back.
  return restore(response.output_text, vault, ["EMAIL", "PHONE"]);
}

    store: false matters on the Responses API: without it, OpenAI keeps the response for at least 30 days so it can be retrieved later. The base URL comes from configuration, so the same code serves a global project and an EU-resident one (https://eu.api.openai.com/v1), each with its own API key. With Anthropic or Gemini the shape is the same: redact before building the request and restore after reading the text, because redaction does not depend on the provider.

    Logging an AI call without its content¶

    // src/privacy/log-ai-call.ts: log what an AI call did, never what it said
import { createHmac } from "node:crypto";

export interface AiCallRecord {
  feature: string;             // "meeting_summary", "assistant", ...
  model: string;
  requestId: string;           // joins the log line to the trace
  providerResponseId?: string; // lets support find the provider-side object, and delete it
  inputTokens: number;
  outputTokens: number;
  latencyMs: number;
  guard: string;               // the guardrail decision, for example "allow"
}

// A keyed hash keeps raw user ids out of log tools, yet lets you find one user's lines for deletion.
export function pseudonym(userId: string, key = process.env.LOG_PSEUDONYM_KEY): string {
  if (!key) throw new Error("LOG_PSEUDONYM_KEY is not set");
  return createHmac("sha256", key).update(userId).digest("hex").slice(0, 16);
}

export function logAiCall(userId: string, record: AiCallRecord): void {
  console.info(JSON.stringify({ event: "ai_call", user: pseudonym(userId), ...record }));
}

    A run with a throwaway key printed:

    {"event":"ai_call","user":"64fbfb527c8100d9","feature":"meeting_summary","model":"example-model","requestId":"req_8f2c","inputTokens":1840,"outputTokens":212,"latencyMs":2310,"guard":"allow"}

    The key lives in the secrets manager (secrets management). For tracing, keep content capture off explicitly, so a library default or a copied configuration cannot turn it on:

    # .env for the TaskFlow API: traces carry timings and token counts, not prompt text
OTEL_SERVICE_NAME=taskflow-api
OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_CAPTURE_CONTENT=NO_CONTENT

    Older instrumentation libraries used other variable names for the same switch, so check the one your tracing library reads.

    Erasing a user's AI data¶

    // src/privacy/erase-user.ts: delete one user's AI data everywhere TaskFlow keeps a copy
import OpenAI from "openai";

// Every provider-side object we create is recorded with its owner, so it can be found again.
export interface ProviderObject {
  kind: "response" | "file" | "vector_store" | "conversation";
  id: string;
}

export interface ErasureDeps {
  listProviderObjects(userId: string): Promise<ProviderObject[]>;
  forgetProviderObjects(userId: string): Promise<void>;
  deleteVectorsBySource(userId: string): Promise<number>;   // chunks embedded from the user's notes
  deleteCachedAnswers(userId: string): Promise<number>;
  deleteFeedbackAndEvalRows(userId: string): Promise<number>;
  deleteProviderObject(object: ProviderObject): Promise<void>;
}

export interface ErasureReport {
  userId: string;
  providerDeleted: number;
  providerAlreadyGone: number;
  failures: string[];
  vectors: number;
  cache: number;
  evalRows: number;
}

const isNotFound = (err: unknown) => err instanceof OpenAI.NotFoundError;

// Idempotent: running it twice is safe, and objects that are already gone count as done.
export async function eraseUserAiData(userId: string, deps: ErasureDeps): Promise<ErasureReport> {
  const report: ErasureReport = { userId, providerDeleted: 0, providerAlreadyGone: 0, failures: [], vectors: 0, cache: 0, evalRows: 0 };

  for (const object of await deps.listProviderObjects(userId)) {
    try {
      await deps.deleteProviderObject(object);
      report.providerDeleted += 1;
    } catch (err) {
      if (isNotFound(err)) report.providerAlreadyGone += 1;
      else report.failures.push(`${object.kind}:${object.id}`);
    }
  }
  report.vectors = await deps.deleteVectorsBySource(userId);
  report.cache = await deps.deleteCachedAnswers(userId);
  report.evalRows = await deps.deleteFeedbackAndEvalRows(userId);

  // Keep the index of provider objects until every delete succeeded, so a retry can finish the job.
  if (report.failures.length === 0) await deps.forgetProviderObjects(userId);
  return report;
}

// The provider half of the dependencies, using the OpenAI SDK's delete endpoints
export function openAiDeleter(client: OpenAI) {
  return async (object: ProviderObject): Promise<void> => {
    switch (object.kind) {
      case "response": await client.responses.delete(object.id); break;
      case "file": await client.files.delete(object.id); break;
      case "vector_store": await client.vectorStores.delete(object.id); break;
      case "conversation": await client.conversations.delete(object.id); break;
    }
  };
}

    The test runs the job twice against fakes: the first run hits a 404 (the response was already gone, which counts as done) and a simulated outage on one file, so it keeps the index; the second run finishes and clears it. The index of provider objects is the piece most teams lack: without recording each response, file, and vector store id against its owner when you create it, there is nothing to delete later. Run the job from a queue with retries, and record completion in an audit log that holds ids and dates, not content.

    Best practices¶

    • Keep an inventory of every copy of AI data, with an owner, a lifetime, and a deletion path.
    • Minimize prompts to the fields a feature needs before thinking about redaction.
    • Redact structured personal data with checksummed patterns, add NER where names matter, and never restore secrets or payment data.
    • Use paid or enterprise API tiers for user data, set store: false (or the provider's equivalent) when you do not need stored state, and request zero retention where the data justifies it.
    • Record every provider-side object id against its user when you create it.
    • Log metadata, not content; pseudonymize user ids in logs; keep tracing content capture off by default.
    • Keep evaluation and fine-tuning sets free of real personal data, or track their provenance so you can remove a user's rows.
    • Choose provider regions and your own storage regions together, and document where processing happens.
    • Re-read provider data policies when you adopt a new model; retention terms can differ by model.

    In production¶

    • A customer's security review finds prompts in the log platform. Detect: a search for a known test email finds full prompts in application logs. Cause: a debug log line left in the AI client wrapper, or tracing content capture switched on in one environment. Fix: remove the line, set content capture to NO_CONTENT explicitly, purge the affected log indexes, and add a CI check that fails on logging calls with prompt variables.
    • A deleted user's text still appears in answers. Detect: project Q&A cites a note that no longer exists. Cause: the erasure job deleted the note but not its vector chunks or the cached answer. Fix: delete chunks by source id and cache entries by user as part of the job, and add a test that queries for the deleted content.
    • An API key pasted into a note is now at the provider. Detect: a secret scanner on outgoing prompts, or the redactor's SECRET count, fires. Cause: users paste credentials into notes. Fix: revoke and rotate the key immediately, keep the redactor's secret patterns in front of every model call, and warn users when a note contains one.
    • Residency drifts after a model upgrade. Detect: a review finds requests going to the global endpoint, or a new model available only in another region. Cause: the base URL or deployment type was copied from an example during the upgrade. Fix: configure the base URL and deployment type per tenant region, and alert when a request goes to an unexpected endpoint.
    • Requests fail after switching to zero data retention. Detect: errors on file uploads or stored conversations right after the change. Cause: those features are stateful and not covered by zero retention (on Bedrock, store=true is rejected in none mode). Fix: move state into your own database, pass context explicitly, and set store: false everywhere.
    • Placeholders leak into the UI. Detect: users see [EMAIL_2] in summaries. Cause: the model invented or altered a placeholder, or a restore call used the wrong vault. Fix: keep one vault per request, instruct the model to copy placeholders exactly, and render unknown placeholders as "(hidden)".

    Important terminology¶

    • PII (personally identifiable information): Data that identifies a person, directly or in combination with other data. GDPR's broader term is "personal data".
    • Data minimisation: Using only the personal data a purpose needs.
    • Redaction / masking: Removing or replacing sensitive values.
    • Pseudonymization: Replacing identifiers with placeholders that can be mapped back with separately held information.
    • Zero data retention (ZDR): A provider arrangement under which prompts and outputs are not stored after the response, with documented exceptions.
    • Abuse monitoring: A provider's retention and review of traffic to detect policy violations.
    • Data residency: Keeping data storage, processing, or both within a chosen geography.
    • Right to erasure: A person's right, under laws such as the GDPR, to have their personal data deleted in many circumstances.
    • Content capture: Recording prompts and completions in telemetry, off by default in the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • "We use the API, so nothing is stored." Abuse logs, stored responses, files, and caches exist unless you change settings or agreements.
    • "Zero data retention means nothing is ever kept." Stateful features and flagged content are outside it.
    • "Pseudonymized data is anonymous." Not while you hold the mapping.
    • "Free tier for prototypes is fine with real data." Unpaid tiers may use content for product improvement with human review.
    • Deleting the source note but forgetting embeddings, caches, eval sets, and provider objects.
    • Logging full prompts "temporarily" and keeping them for a year.
    • Choosing an EU model region while the vector store and logs stay in the US.
    • Fine-tuning on raw production conversations without a way to remove one person's data.
