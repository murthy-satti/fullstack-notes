    AI Regulation and Governance¶

    AI features are now regulated in their own right, not only through privacy and consumer law. The EU AI Act sorts AI systems by risk and attaches duties to each level; US frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework describe how to manage AI risk voluntarily; and some US states regulate automated decisions about people. For a team building TaskFlow's Assistant, meeting summaries, and agent, the practical questions are which rules apply to which feature, what evidence to keep, and how to stop a harmless feature from drifting into a regulated use.

    This lesson explains the EU AI Act's roles, risk tiers, and timeline as of November 2026 (including the 2026 Digital Omnibus changes), the transparency duties that apply to chat assistants and generated content, the NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001, recent US state laws, and how to run governance as an engineering process with system cards checked in CI.

    Not legal advice

    This page summarizes public sources as of November 2026 so that engineers can ask the right questions. It is not legal advice. Laws, guidance, and dates change, and how they apply depends on your product and where you operate; confirm obligations with qualified counsel.

    Why it matters¶

    Regulation decides what you may build, not only how you document it. Under the EU AI Act, a TaskFlow feature that infers a team's mood from meeting notes is a prohibited practice in the workplace, while a feature that suggests who should get a task based on past performance falls into a high-risk category with a full compliance regime. Both could be built in an afternoon with the same API call. Engineers are often the first to see that a feature has crossed a line, so they need the map.

    The cost of getting it wrong is large. Fines under the AI Act reach EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices, and customers increasingly ask for AI documentation in procurement. Good governance is also good engineering: an inventory of AI features, a system card per feature, evaluation evidence, and an incident process make AI features easier to debug, review, and change.

    Real-world usage¶

    • European Commission. The AI Act policy page (updated October 19, 2026) states that the Act entered into force on August 1, 2024, "became applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions", and that the Digital Omnibus on AI, which entered into force on July 27, 2026, moved high-risk rules for Annex III areas to December 2, 2027 and for regulated products to August 2, 2028.
    • AI Office codes of practice. The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice (July 10, 2025) has chapters on transparency, copyright, and safety and security; the Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content (final June 10, 2026) covers marking and labelling. The Commission and the AI Board assessed both as adequate voluntary tools for showing compliance.
    • NIST. The AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) is "intended for voluntary use", is organized around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, and has a Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024). NIST says the framework "is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan".
    • Colorado. SB26-189, signed May 14, 2026, repealed and re-enacted the 2024 Colorado AI Act as a law on automated decision-making technology used in consequential decisions such as employment, housing, and lending, with developer documentation duties starting January 1, 2027.
    • Model cards. Model Cards for Model Reporting (Mitchell et al., 2018) proposed a short standard document for each model; Hugging Face model cards made the idea routine for published models.

    Popular tools and providers: the EU AI Act Service Desk, the NIST AI RMF Playbook, ISO/IEC 42001 certification, Hugging Face model cards, and provider-published model and system cards.

    How it works¶

    Roles under the EU AI Act¶

    Duties attach to roles, so start by naming yours for each feature:

    Role Who TaskFlow example
    Provider of a general-purpose AI model Develops a model that many systems build on The model vendor behind CHAT_MODEL
    Provider of an AI system Develops an AI system and places it on the market under its own name TaskFlow, for the Assistant and meeting summaries
    Deployer Uses an AI system under its authority in a professional activity A company that rolls out TaskFlow's Assistant to its staff
    Affected person Someone the system's output is about An employee mentioned in a meeting summary

    Roles can shift along the value chain: a company that rebrands a high-risk system or substantially modifies it can take on provider duties. TaskFlow is usually a provider of AI systems built on someone else's model, which means it depends on the model provider's documentation and owns the documentation of its own features.

    Risk tiers¶

    The Act regulates uses, not models. The same API call can be minimal risk in one feature and prohibited in another.

    • Prohibited practices (Article 5). Banned since February 2, 2025. The list includes manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, social scoring, untargeted scraping of facial images, and, directly relevant to workplace software, AI systems "to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions", except for medical or safety reasons. The Omnibus added bans on generating non-consensual intimate imagery of identifiable people and child sexual abuse material, applying from December 2, 2026.
    • High-risk systems (Article 6). Two routes: AI that is a safety component of products covered by EU product law (Annex I), and AI used in eight sensitive areas (Annex III), such as biometrics, education, employment, and access to essential services. Annex III point 4(b) covers AI used "to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits or characteristics or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour" of workers, which is exactly where a task-management product can wander. Providers of high-risk systems need risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, instructions for deployers, human oversight, and accuracy and robustness (Articles 9 to 15), plus a quality management system, conformity assessment, registration, post-market monitoring, and serious-incident reporting. Deployers have their own duties (Article 26), including informing workers before using such a system at work.
    • Transparency duties (Article 50). Apply from August 2, 2026, and are covered below.
    • Minimal risk. Everything else, such as spam filters and autocomplete, has no new obligations; voluntary codes apply.

    General-purpose AI models have a separate track, applying since August 2, 2025: their providers document the model, give information to downstream providers, keep a copyright policy, and publish a summary of training content, with extra duties for models with systemic risk.

    The timeline after the Digital Omnibus¶

    The Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus on AI on November 19, 2025, reached political agreement on May 7, 2026, and the regulation entered into force on July 27, 2026. Its main effect for builders is time: the Annex III high-risk rules that were due on August 2, 2026 moved to December 2, 2027, and the rules for AI in regulated products (Annex I) now apply from August 2, 2028. The transparency duties still apply from August 2, 2026; the only change is that generative systems already on the market before that date have until December 2, 2026 to mark their output under Article 50(2) (new Article 111(4)). It also softened the AI literacy duty in Article 4, which has applied since February 2025: providers and deployers must "take measures to support the development of AI literacy" of their staff, without having to guarantee any specific level, while the Commission and the Member States support those efforts (consolidated text on the AI Act Service Desk, as at July 27, 2026).

    Penalties are set as a fixed amount or a share of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (for SMEs, whichever is lower), according to the Commission's enforcement page:

    Breach Maximum fine
    Prohibited practices EUR 35 million or 7% of turnover
    Most other obligations, including transparency EUR 15 million or 3%
    Incorrect or misleading information to authorities EUR 7.5 million or 1%

    Transparency duties in practice¶

    Article 50 has five parts that matter to application builders:

    1. People must know they are talking to an AI. Providers design systems that interact with people so that users are "informed that they are interacting with an AI system", unless that is obvious to a reasonably observant person. The TaskFlow Assistant says so in its interface, not only in the terms of service.
    2. Synthetic content must be marked. Providers of systems that generate audio, images, video, or text mark outputs "in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated", with exceptions for assistive editing that does not substantially change the input.
    3. Emotion recognition and biometric categorization require informing the people exposed (and emotion inference at work is banned outright, as above).
    4. Deepfakes and public-interest text must be disclosed by deployers; text that has undergone human review or editorial control, with a person taking editorial responsibility, is exempt.
    5. Timing and form. The information must be clear and given at the latest at the first interaction or exposure.

    For TaskFlow, part 1 is the clear duty: the Assistant and the agent show that they are AI. For AI summaries, the safe engineering choice is to label them in the UI, keep a machine-readable flag in the API response, and follow the Commission's guidelines and the transparency code for marking techniques, rather than guessing which outputs count as "substantially" generated.

    Beyond the EU¶

    Framework Binding? What it gives you TaskFlow use
    EU AI Act Law in the EU Roles, risk tiers, duties, fines Classify every feature; Article 50 for the Assistant
    NIST AI RMF 1.0 and AI 600-1 Voluntary Govern, Map, Measure, Manage; generative AI risks and actions The structure of the internal governance process
    ISO/IEC 42001:2023 Voluntary, certifiable Requirements for an AI management system Evidence for enterprise customers who ask for a certificate
    Colorado SB26-189 Law in Colorado Notice, explanations, and human review for automated consequential decisions May apply if TaskFlow features materially influence employment decisions about Colorado residents
    California SB 53 Law in California Transparency duties for frontier model developers Applies to model vendors, not to TaskFlow

    Privacy law runs alongside all of these: the GDPR applies to personal data in prompts regardless of the AI Act (see privacy, PII, and data retention).

    Governance as an engineering loop¶

    The NIST AI RMF's four functions work well as a loop per feature, with artifacts that live in the repository:

    • Govern: an AI policy, named owners, an inventory of every AI feature, and a review step for new features or new uses.
    • Map: a system card per feature: purpose, users, uses it must not be put to, data, model, risk tier, and applicable laws.
    • Measure: evaluation suites, red-team results, and privacy checks, stored as evidence (see evals in CI and regression testing).
    • Manage: launch decisions, monitoring, incident handling, and retirement, with changes feeding back into the card.

    Model cards and system cards¶

    A model card, as proposed by Mitchell et al., documents a trained model: details, intended use, factors, metrics, evaluation and training data, quantitative analyses, ethical considerations, and caveats. Model providers publish model or system cards for their models; read them before choosing a model, and link them from your own documentation.

    TaskFlow does not train the models it uses, so its unit of documentation is the AI feature: a system card that records what the feature is for, what it must not be used for, which model and data it uses, how it was evaluated, where people oversee it, and who to contact when it goes wrong. The "not intended for" list matters most for regulation, because it is where a team writes down, for example, that meeting summaries must not be used to evaluate employees.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation What to do
    Internal prototype on synthetic data Inventory entry and a short card; no launch without review
    Customer-facing assistant or generated content Card, evals, Article 50 disclosure and labels, privacy review
    Feature that ranks, scores, or allocates work to people Treat as high-risk in the EU and as a consequential decision in Colorado; legal review before design, or redesign
    Inferring emotions or mood of workers or students Do not build it for EU workplaces or schools
    Minimal-risk helpers such as autocomplete Inventory entry and a lightweight card

    When not to add process:

    • Do not run a full high-risk process for minimal-risk features; it slows teams down and teaches them to treat governance as paperwork.
    • Do not write documentation that nobody checks; a card that CI does not read goes stale within a quarter.
    • Do not rely on a vendor's compliance claims for your own feature; the model provider's documentation covers the model, not your use of it.

    Practical examples¶

    A system card as data¶

    Keeping cards as JSON next to the code means they are reviewed in pull requests, versioned with the feature, and checkable by machines:

    {
  "id": "meeting-summary",
  "name": "Meeting-notes summary",
  "owner": "ai-platform-team",
  "purpose": "Summarize a project's meeting notes into action items for members of that project.",
  "intendedUsers": "Members of the project the notes belong to",
  "notIntendedFor": [
    "Evaluating, ranking, or monitoring individual employees",
    "Inferring the emotions or mood of meeting participants"
  ],
  "riskTier": "transparency",
  "transparency": "The UI labels every summary as AI-generated and shows the notes it cites.",
  "model": {
    "provider": "openai",
    "configKey": "CHAT_MODEL",
    "changePolicy": "A new model ships only after the evaluation suite passes on it."
  },
  "data": {
    "inputs": ["meeting notes the user can read"],
    "personalData": "Names and contact details; emails and phone numbers are redacted before sending.",
    "providerRetention": "store=false; abuse-monitoring logs per the provider's terms",
    "residency": "EU workspaces use the EU project and base URL"
  },
  "evaluation": {
    "suite": "evals/meeting-summary",
    "lastRun": "2026-11-02",
    "metrics": { "actionItemRecall": 0.91, "unsupportedClaimRate": 0.02 }
  },
  "humanOversight": "Summaries stay drafts until a project member publishes them.",
  "limitations": [
    "Misses some action items phrased as questions",
    "Lower quality for notes that mix several languages"
  ],
  "incidentContact": "ai-incidents@taskflow.example.com",
  "reviewedOn": "2026-10-15"
}

    The file lives at governance/system-cards/meeting-summary.json. The model field names the configuration key, not a model ID, so the card stays true when the model changes; the evaluation record says which suite gates a model change.

    Checking cards in CI¶

    // src/governance/check-system-cards.ts: fail CI when an AI feature's system card is missing, incomplete, or stale
import { readdir, readFile } from "node:fs/promises";
import { join } from "node:path";
import { z } from "zod";

const isoDate = z.string().regex(/^\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}$/, "use YYYY-MM-DD");

const SystemCard = z.object({
  id: z.string().min(1),
  name: z.string().min(1),
  owner: z.string().min(1),
  purpose: z.string().min(20),
  intendedUsers: z.string().min(1),
  notIntendedFor: z.array(z.string()).min(1, "list at least one use the feature must not be used for"),
  riskTier: z.enum(["prohibited", "high", "transparency", "minimal"]),
  transparency: z.string().min(1),
  model: z.object({ provider: z.string(), configKey: z.string(), changePolicy: z.string().min(1) }),
  data: z.object({
    inputs: z.array(z.string()).min(1),
    personalData: z.string(),
    providerRetention: z.string(),
    residency: z.string(),
  }),
  evaluation: z.object({ suite: z.string(), lastRun: isoDate, metrics: z.record(z.string(), z.number()) }),
  humanOversight: z.string().min(1).optional(),
  limitations: z.array(z.string()).min(1, "document at least one known limitation"),
  incidentContact: z.email(),
  reviewedOn: isoDate,
});
// The fields the policy rules need, checked even when other parts of the card are invalid
const PolicyView = SystemCard.pick({ riskTier: true, humanOversight: true, evaluation: true, reviewedOn: true });

const daysBetween = (from: string, to: Date) => Math.floor((to.getTime() - Date.parse(from)) / 86_400_000);

// Rules the schema cannot express: freshness, and duties that depend on the risk tier
export function policyProblems(card: z.infer<typeof PolicyView>, today: Date): string[] {
  const problems: string[] = [];
  if (card.riskTier === "prohibited") problems.push("risk tier 'prohibited': this feature must not ship");
  if (card.riskTier === "high" && !card.humanOversight) problems.push("high risk: humanOversight is required");
  if (card.riskTier === "high") problems.push("high risk: needs a conformity plan and legal sign-off before launch");
  if (Object.keys(card.evaluation.metrics).length === 0) problems.push("evaluation has no recorded metrics");
  if (daysBetween(card.evaluation.lastRun, today) > 90) problems.push(`evaluation is ${daysBetween(card.evaluation.lastRun, today)} days old (max 90)`);
  if (daysBetween(card.reviewedOn, today) > 180) problems.push(`review is ${daysBetween(card.reviewedOn, today)} days old (max 180)`);
  return problems;
}

export async function checkCards(dir: string, today: Date): Promise<number> {
  let failures = 0;
  for (const file of (await readdir(dir)).filter((f) => f.endsWith(".json")).sort()) {
    const raw: unknown = JSON.parse(await readFile(join(dir, file), "utf8"));
    const parsed = SystemCard.safeParse(raw);
    const problems = parsed.success ? [] : parsed.error.issues.map((i) => `${i.path.join(".")}: ${i.message}`);
    const view = PolicyView.safeParse(raw);
    if (view.success) problems.push(...policyProblems(view.data, today));
    console.log(`${problems.length === 0 ? "PASS" : "FAIL"} ${file}`);
    for (const p of problems) console.log(`  - ${p}`);
    if (problems.length > 0) failures += 1;
  }
  return failures;
}

// Usage: node dist/src/governance/check-system-cards.js governance/system-cards [YYYY-MM-DD]
if (process.argv[1]?.endsWith("check-system-cards.js")) {
  const [dir = "governance/system-cards", day] = process.argv.slice(2);
  const failures = await checkCards(dir, day ? new Date(`${day}T00:00:00Z`) : new Date());
  process.exitCode = failures > 0 ? 1 : 0;
}

    The schema (zod) checks shape and required content; policyProblems adds rules that depend on the risk tier and on dates, and runs even when the schema fails, so one run reports everything. Running it on the meeting-summary card and a proposed "workload insights" card, which would suggest who takes a task based on past performance, printed:

    PASS meeting-summary.json
FAIL workload-insights.json
  - notIntendedFor: list at least one use the feature must not be used for
  - limitations: document at least one known limitation
  - high risk: humanOversight is required
  - high risk: needs a conformity plan and legal sign-off before launch
  - evaluation has no recorded metrics
  - evaluation is 178 days old (max 90)
  - review is 258 days old (max 180)

    The process exited with code 1, which fails the build. The proposed feature is the Annex III 4(b) case from above: the checker cannot decide the legal question, but it forces the conversation before launch rather than after.

    Running the check on every change and every week¶

    # .github/workflows/ai-governance.yml: block merges when an AI feature's system card is missing or stale
name: ai-governance
on:
  pull_request:
    paths:
      - "governance/system-cards/**"
      - "src/**"
  schedule:
    - cron: "0 6 * * 1" # weekly, so cards go stale in CI even when nobody edits them
jobs:
  system-cards:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v7
      - uses: actions/setup-node@v7
        with:
          node-version: 24
          cache: npm
      - run: npm ci
      - run: npx tsc -p .
      - run: node dist/src/governance/check-system-cards.js governance/system-cards

    The schedule matters as much as the pull-request trigger: evaluation evidence and reviews age even when the code does not change. The workflow passed actionlint; CI setup in general is covered in security in the delivery pipeline.

    Disclosing AI output in the API¶

    Transparency is easier to keep consistent when the API carries it, so every client (web, mobile, email digest) can show the same label:

    {
  "summary": "Action items: ship the export feature (note-12); fix the login bug (note-14).",
  "ai": {
    "generated": true,
    "feature": "meeting-summary",
    "label": "AI-generated summary. Check it against the notes before sharing.",
    "card": "https://app.taskflow.example.com/ai/meeting-summary"
  }
}

    A UI test that fails when a component renders a response with ai.generated: true but without the label catches the most common transparency gap: a new surface, such as an email digest, that forgets it (see frontend patterns for AI features).

    Best practices¶

    • Keep an inventory of every AI feature, owned by a named team.
    • Classify each feature's risk tier at design time, and again when its use changes.
    • Write a system card per feature, including what it must not be used for, and check cards in CI on every change and on a schedule.
    • Treat any feature that ranks, scores, monitors, or allocates work to people as a legal-review trigger.
    • Show users that they are dealing with an AI, and carry an AI flag in API responses so every client can label output.
    • Keep evaluation and red-team results as dated evidence, linked from the card.
    • Track model providers' documentation, terms, and deprecations as part of vendor management.
    • Run an incident process for AI failures: record, contain, fix, and update the card.
    • Follow official sources (the Commission, the AI Act Service Desk, NIST, state legislatures) and date every claim in internal guidance.

    In production¶

    • An AI feature ships without anyone knowing. Detect: a CI job that searches for model SDK imports finds a call site with no matching system card. Cause: a team added an AI helper inside an existing feature. Fix: fail the build when model clients are used outside registered features, and add the card.
    • A customer uses a feature for something it was not designed for. Detect: support requests or usage analytics show workload suggestions being exported into performance reviews. Cause: the feature drifted into an Annex III 4(b) use. Fix: re-classify, restrict the export, update terms and the card's "not intended for" list, and get legal advice before continuing.
    • Evaluation evidence goes stale. Detect: the weekly governance job fails with "evaluation is 120 days old". Cause: model upgrades shipped without re-running the suite. Fix: make the evaluation run part of the model-change process, and record the result in the card automatically.
    • A new surface shows AI output without a label. Detect: a UI review or an automated test finds an email digest with AI summaries and no disclosure. Cause: the label lived only in the web component. Fix: carry the AI flag in the API response and test every client against it.
    • The model provider changes terms or retires a model. Detect: a provider deprecation notice, or a changed data-retention page. Cause: normal vendor change. Fix: treat provider changes as card review triggers, re-run evaluations on the replacement model, and update privacy documentation.
    • An AI summary states something false and damaging about a person. Detect: a user report through the incident contact on the card. Cause: hallucination or a prompt-injected note. Fix: remove the output, preserve logs for the investigation, add the case to the evaluation set, and review whether the feature needs stronger human review before publishing.

    Important terminology¶

    • Provider: Under the AI Act, whoever develops an AI system or model and places it on the market under its own name.
    • Deployer: An organization using an AI system under its authority in a professional context.
    • General-purpose AI model: A model that can serve many tasks and is integrated into many downstream systems.
    • High-risk AI system: A system in Annex I products or Annex III areas, subject to requirements before and after it is placed on the market.
    • Prohibited practice: A use banned outright under Article 5.
    • Transparency obligations: Article 50 duties to tell people they are interacting with AI and to mark or disclose generated content.
    • Digital Omnibus on AI: The 2026 amending regulation that moved the high-risk dates and added new prohibitions.
    • AI RMF: NIST's voluntary AI Risk Management Framework, organized into Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.
    • ISO/IEC 42001: The international standard for AI management systems, which organizations can be certified against.
    • Model card / system card: A short, structured document describing a model or an AI feature, its intended use, evaluation, and limits.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • "We only call an API, so the AI Act is the vendor's problem." The vendor covers its model; your feature and its use are yours.
    • "The high-risk rules were delayed, so nothing applies yet." Prohibitions, AI literacy, and transparency duties already apply.
    • "Our feature is fine because the model is general-purpose." Risk follows the use: allocating work by personal traits is high-risk whatever model does it.
    • Writing the card once at launch and never updating it.
    • Documenting what a feature does but not what it must not be used for.
    • Treating NIST or ISO frameworks as law, or treating them as optional extras when a customer contract requires them.
    • Relying on blog summaries of legal changes instead of the official sources, especially for dates.
