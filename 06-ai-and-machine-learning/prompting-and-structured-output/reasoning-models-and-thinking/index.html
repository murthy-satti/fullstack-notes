    Reasoning Models and Thinking¶

    A reasoning model is a language model trained to work through a problem before it answers: it generates a long internal chain of reasoning tokens, checks and revises its approach, and only then writes the reply. Spending more computation at answer time, rather than only at training time, is called test-time compute, and it is why the same request can take a fast model one second and a reasoning model thirty, at a very different cost, with a very different chance of being right on a hard problem.

    This lesson covers what reasoning models are and where they came from; the controls each provider offers for how much a model thinks (effort levels, thinking budgets, and output ceilings), verified against the official documentation as of November 2026; when to use a reasoning model instead of a fast one; how prompting changes for them; the latency and cost trade-offs; visible versus hidden reasoning; and how to evaluate reasoning models by their outcomes. The running example is a TaskFlow weekly planner that schedules a team's tasks under capacity, dependency, and due-date constraints. Choosing a model family, provider, and price tier is covered in choosing models and providers; this lesson is about prompting and configuring reasoning once you use it.

    Why it matters¶

    • Some problems need search, not recall. Scheduling twelve tasks for three people without breaking a dependency or a due date is a constraint problem. Fast models produce plausible plans that break rules; reasoning models check their own work and fail far less often.
    • Reasoning is paid for even when hidden. Every provider bills reasoning tokens as output tokens, and they count against the output limit, so a feature can become many times more expensive and slower without any visible change in the answer.
    • The controls changed recently and differ by provider. Token budgets are giving way to effort levels, defaults differ between models of the same family, and some settings that worked last year now return a 400 error.
    • Prompting habits can backfire. "Think step by step" helped older models; with reasoning models it is unnecessary and, according to OpenAI, can sometimes hurt.
    • The reasoning you see is not the reasoning that happened. Summaries are written for people, and research shows reasoning text often omits what actually drove the answer, which changes how you debug and evaluate.

    Real-world usage¶

    • Chain-of-thought prompting. Wei and colleagues, Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (2022), showed that asking a model to write intermediate steps improves arithmetic and logic results. Reasoning models internalize that idea through training instead of prompting.
    • Test-time compute scaling. Snell and colleagues, Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally (2024), found that allocating test-time compute adaptively per prompt is more than four times as efficient as a best-of-N baseline, and that on problems where a smaller model already has moderate success, extra test-time compute can let it outperform a model 14 times larger.
    • Reasoning through reinforcement learning. DeepSeek-R1 (2025, published in Nature) reports that reasoning abilities can be incentivized with pure reinforcement learning, without human-written reasoning traces, that behaviours such as self-reflection and verification emerge, and that the reasoning patterns can be distilled into smaller models. Its open weights made a reasoning model's raw reasoning visible to anyone running it.
    • Provider controls. OpenAI's reasoning guide documents hidden reasoning tokens billed as output, reasoning.effort values from none to max depending on the model, and a recommendation to reserve at least 25,000 tokens for reasoning and output when you start. Anthropic's thinking documentation documents adaptive thinking that is on by default on Claude 5 models, manual budget_tokens that returns a 400 error from Claude Opus 4.7 onwards, and effort as soft guidance with max_tokens as the hard ceiling.
    • Faithfulness. Anthropic's alignment science team, Reasoning models don't always say what they think (April 2025), planted hints in prompts and found that when models used a hint, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned it in its reasoning 25 percent of the time and DeepSeek R1 39 percent of the time.
    • Monitoring reasoning. OpenAI's Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior (2025) found that a weaker model reading the chain of thought caught reward hacking in coding tasks better than one reading only actions, but that strong training pressure on the chain of thought taught models to hide their intent. The multi-lab position paper Chain of Thought Monitorability (2025) calls this "a new and fragile opportunity" for safety.

    Popular tools and providers: OpenAI's reasoning models through the Responses API, Claude models with adaptive thinking and effort, Gemini thinking models through the Interactions API, open-weight reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 served on your own GPUs, and the Vercel AI SDK's provider-neutral reasoning setting. Choose model IDs from the providers' current lists: OpenAI models, Anthropic models, and Gemini models.

    How it works¶

    Test-time compute: what changes inside the request¶

    A fast model reads the prompt and starts producing answer tokens at once. A reasoning model first produces reasoning tokens: it restates the problem, tries an approach, checks it against the constraints, backs up, and tries again. Only when it is satisfied does it write the answer. The reasoning is trained with reinforcement learning on problems whose answers can be checked (math, code, logic), which is why the gains are largest on tasks of that kind.

    Three properties follow, and every design decision in this lesson comes back to them:

    1. Reasoning tokens are output tokens. They are billed at the output rate, they count against the output limit, and they take generation time, even when you never see them.
    2. The amount of reasoning varies by request. Current models decide how long to think based on difficulty, within the limits you set. Easy requests can get almost no reasoning; hard ones can use many thousands of tokens.
    3. Nothing visible happens while the model reasons. Time to first visible token grows with reasoning, so streaming alone does not fix the user experience.

    The controls, provider by provider¶

    Each provider exposes one control for how much the model thinks, one hard ceiling, one option for visible reasoning, and a mechanism for carrying reasoning across turns. As of November 2026:

    Control OpenAI Responses API Anthropic Messages API Google Interactions API Vercel AI SDK
    How much to think reasoning.effort: none, minimal, low, medium, high, xhigh, max (supported values depend on the model) output_config.effort: low, medium, high, xhigh, max, plus thinking.type: adaptive, disabled, between_tools, or enabled with budget_tokens on older models generation_config.thinking_level: minimal, low, medium, high, xhigh (depends on the model) reasoning: provider-default, none, minimal ... max, mapped to each provider
    Hard ceiling max_output_tokens (includes reasoning) max_tokens (includes thinking) generation_config.max_output_tokens maxOutputTokens
    Visible reasoning reasoning.summary: auto, concise, detailed thinking.display: summarized or omitted (the default on Claude 5 models) thinking_summaries: auto or none reasoningText, when the provider returns it
    Across turns reasoning items; encrypted_content when store is false; reasoning.context thinking blocks with a signature, returned unchanged thought blocks with signatures, resent unchanged in stateless mode kept in the message history by the SDK
    Usage field usage.output_tokens_details.reasoning_tokens usage.output_tokens_details.thinking_tokens total_thought_tokens usage on the result

    The direction across providers is the same: from token budgets to effort levels. Anthropic's manual budget_tokens (at least 1,024 tokens and below max_tokens) is deprecated on Claude Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 and rejected on later models, where effort is the recommended control and adaptive thinking decides how much of it to spend. Defaults also differ inside one family: Anthropic documents high as the default effort for most models but medium for some, so set the value explicitly rather than inheriting a default that changes with the model ID.

    Some sampling parameters no longer apply. OpenAI's reasoning guide documents reasoning.effort, not sampling parameters, as the control; Anthropic documents that on its newest models any non-default temperature, top_p, or top_k returns a 400 error, and that from API version 2026-10-21 any value of them does on every model; and Google marks temperature as deprecated in the Interactions API's generation config. Control variety with the prompt, and quality with effort.

    Effort is guidance; the output limit is the wall¶

    Effort tells the model how much work a request deserves. It is soft: Anthropic calls it "a behavioral signal, not a strict token budget", and at low effort a model still thinks on a hard problem, only less. The output limit is hard: when max_output_tokens or max_tokens is reached, generation stops, wherever it is. With a reasoning model that can be in the middle of reasoning, before a single visible token, and OpenAI's guide warns that you are still billed for the input and the reasoning in that case.

    Practical consequences:

    • Leave headroom. Start with a generous ceiling (OpenAI suggests at least 25,000 tokens while experimenting), then lower it once logs show how much the feature really uses.
    • Treat "incomplete" as its own outcome. OpenAI reports status: "incomplete" with reason max_output_tokens; Anthropic reports stop_reason: "max_tokens". Alert on its rate, because it means paid requests with no answer.
    • Stream long requests. Anthropic's TypeScript SDK refuses a non-streaming request whose max_tokens implies it could run longer than ten minutes (by its estimate, above roughly 21,000 output tokens), so generous ceilings go with messages.stream() and finalMessage().
    • Lower effort before raising the ceiling. Anthropic's guidance for cost and latency is to lower effort first; raising the ceiling only allows more spending.

    When to use a reasoning model¶

    OpenAI's reasoning best-practices guide describes reasoning models as "the planners" and faster models as "the workhorses", and expects most workflows to combine both. For TaskFlow:

    Feature Recommended setup Why
    Natural-language task drafts Fast model, reasoning off or minimal, structured output Short, frequent, latency-sensitive; correctness is checked by code
    Meeting-notes summaries Fast model, or low effort Mostly comprehension and compression
    Project Q&A over documents Fast model; low effort when sources conflict Retrieval supplies the facts; reasoning helps reconcile them
    Weekly planner Reasoning model, effort chosen by a sweep Constraint satisfaction with checkable rules
    Agent that updates tasks through tools Reasoning at low or medium effort, thinking between tool calls Multi-step decisions where each tool result changes the plan
    Nightly analysis of overdue work Reasoning model in a background job Accuracy matters, nobody is waiting for the answer

    Do not use a reasoning model to fix a missing fact (use retrieval), an unenforced format (use structured output), or a vague task (fix the prompt). And do not set the highest effort everywhere "to be safe": the cost and latency are real, and on simple tasks extra thinking can make results worse, a behaviour Anthropic's guide calls overthinking.

    How prompting differs¶

    The prompting advice from OpenAI's reasoning best-practices guide and Anthropic's prompting guide converges on a few changes:

    • State the goal, the constraints, and what success looks like; leave out the method. "Schedule as much high-priority work as possible; these three rules must hold; list what you could not schedule and why" works better than a numbered procedure. OpenAI recommends being very specific about the end goal and success criteria.
    • Do not ask for step-by-step reasoning. The model already reasons; OpenAI says prompts asking it to think step by step are unnecessary and can sometimes hinder performance.
    • Try zero-shot first. Reasoning models often need no examples; add them only when evaluation shows a gap, and make them consistent with the rules.
    • Use developer instructions and delimiters as before. OpenAI notes that developer messages replace system messages for its reasoning models; delimit untrusted input exactly as in prompt design and templates.
    • Keep reasoning out of the output schema. A structured answer should contain results, not a "reasoning" field. Anthropic warns that schema fields asking for step-by-step reasoning can trigger refusals; ask for a short explanation if users need one.
    • Calm the prompt down. Anthropic recommends replacing aggressive instructions written for older models ("CRITICAL: you MUST...") with plain ones, and steering overthinking with guidance such as "choose an approach and commit to it" or with lower effort.

    Latency and cost trade-offs¶

    All comparisons here are relative, because prices change and differ by model. Three effects dominate:

    • Output volume. A reasoning request can produce several times more billed output than the visible answer. Output tokens usually cost more than input tokens, so reasoning tends to dominate the bill of a feature that uses it.
    • Time to first visible token. It grows with the amount of reasoning. For interactive features, stream the reasoning summary or a progress indicator; for anything over a few seconds, consider a background job that notifies the user (see latency optimization).
    • Caching helps less. Prompt caching reduces the cost of repeated input; it does not reduce the reasoning a request generates. Anthropic also notes that changing the top-level effort between requests can invalidate cached prompt prefixes, so keep effort constant within a cached conversation.

    The way to choose is an effort sweep: run the same evaluation set at each effort level, record the pass rate, latency percentiles, and output tokens, and pick the lowest level that meets the bar. On the sample data in the practical examples, medium passes 90 percent of planning cases at a p95 of 15.1 seconds and about 4,400 output tokens per request, while high passes the same 90 percent at a p95 of 33 seconds and about 10,000 tokens: more than twice the tokens and time for no measurable gain. low is the fastest but passes only 70 percent.

    Visible and hidden reasoning¶

    Hosted providers do not return raw reasoning. OpenAI says it does not expose raw reasoning tokens; Anthropic says no display setting returns the raw chain of thought, that summaries are produced by a different model, and that you are billed for the full thinking, not the summary; Google returns summaries only when asked and prices the full thoughts. What you can get is:

    • A summary, for debugging and for user-facing progress ("checking capacity for Tuesday"). On Anthropic's Claude 5 models the default display is omitted, which Anthropic notes makes the first text token arrive sooner because no thinking text is streamed.
    • Opaque carry-over, so the model can continue its reasoning across tool calls. Send it back exactly as received: Anthropic rejects modified thinking blocks with a 400 error, and OpenAI's stateless mode (store: false) carries reasoning in encrypted_content that only works within the same model family.

    Do not show a reasoning summary to users as "why the assistant decided this". It is not an audit trail: the faithfulness research above shows reasoning text often omits the factor that actually changed the answer. If users need a reason, ask for a short, checkable explanation in the answer (which tasks were left out and the rule that excluded them), and verify it with code.

    Evaluating reasoning models¶

    Evaluate the outcome, not the reasoning:

    1. Grade with code wherever a rule exists. The planner's answer is checked by checkPlan: every task appears once, no member exceeds capacity, dependencies come first, due dates hold. Code gives an objective pass or fail that does not depend on how convincing the summary sounds.
    2. Run each case several times. Reasoning output varies between runs; report the pass rate over repeats, not a single run.
    3. Sweep effort and record cost and latency with quality. A plan that is 2 percent more likely to pass but takes twice as long may not be worth it for an interactive feature.
    4. Re-run on every model change. Effort levels are calibrated per model; Anthropic recommends a fresh sweep rather than carrying settings over from an earlier model.
    5. Watch production outcomes. Incomplete responses, plans the user rejects, and repairs needed per request are the production versions of the same metrics (see LLM observability and tracing).

    Grading with a model is useful for qualities code cannot check, such as the clarity of a plan's summary; the techniques are in LLM-as-judge and automated evals.

    When to use and when not to use¶

    Situation Use Avoid
    Planning, scheduling, constraint problems A reasoning model plus a code checker A fast model with "think carefully" in the prompt
    Code generation and debugging Reasoning at an effort chosen by evals The maximum effort by default
    Extraction, classification, short drafts A fast model with structured output Reasoning models (slower, costlier, rarely better)
    Chat that must feel instant A fast model; a reasoning model only behind a "think deeper" action Reasoning on every message
    Long background analysis A reasoning model in a queue worker Holding an HTTP request open for minutes
    Explaining a decision to users A short explanation field checked by code Showing the reasoning summary as the explanation

    Practical examples¶

    The weekly planner: schema and checker¶

    // src/ai/planner/week-plan.ts
import { z } from "zod";

export interface PlannerTask {
  id: string;
  title: string;
  estimateHours: number;
  priority: "low" | "medium" | "high";
  dueDate: string | null; // YYYY-MM-DD
  dependsOn: string[];    // task IDs that must be finished first
}

export interface PlannerInput {
  weekDays: string[];                       // the working days, YYYY-MM-DD
  members: { id: string; name: string; hoursPerDay: number }[];
  tasks: PlannerTask[];
}

/** The model's answer: a schema for the final result only, no reasoning fields. */
export const WeekPlan = z.object({
  scheduled: z.array(z.object({
    taskId: z.string(),
    date: z.string().describe("One of the working days, YYYY-MM-DD"),
    assigneeId: z.string(),
  })),
  unscheduled: z.array(z.object({
    taskId: z.string(),
    reason: z.string().describe("One short sentence"),
  })),
  summary: z.string().describe("Two or three sentences for the team lead"),
});
export type WeekPlan = z.infer<typeof WeekPlan>;

/** Grades a plan with code: every rule is checked, nothing is taken on trust. */
export function checkPlan(plan: WeekPlan, input: PlannerInput): string[] {
  const problems: string[] = [];
  const tasks = new Map(input.tasks.map((t) => [t.id, t]));
  const members = new Map(input.members.map((m) => [m.id, m]));
  const dateOf = new Map<string, string>();

  const seen = new Set<string>();
  for (const id of [...plan.scheduled.map((s) => s.taskId), ...plan.unscheduled.map((u) => u.taskId)]) {
    if (!tasks.has(id)) problems.push(`unknown task ${id}`);
    if (seen.has(id)) problems.push(`task ${id} appears more than once`);
    seen.add(id);
  }
  for (const id of tasks.keys()) if (!seen.has(id)) problems.push(`task ${id} is missing`);

  const load = new Map<string, number>(); // "memberId|date" -> hours
  for (const s of plan.scheduled) {
    const task = tasks.get(s.taskId);
    const member = members.get(s.assigneeId);
    if (!task) continue;
    if (!member) { problems.push(`task ${s.taskId}: unknown assignee ${s.assigneeId}`); continue; }
    if (!input.weekDays.includes(s.date)) problems.push(`task ${s.taskId}: ${s.date} is not a working day`);
    if (task.dueDate && s.date > task.dueDate) problems.push(`task ${s.taskId}: scheduled after its due date`);
    dateOf.set(s.taskId, s.date);
    const key = `${member.id}|${s.date}`;
    load.set(key, (load.get(key) ?? 0) + task.estimateHours);
  }

  for (const [key, hours] of load) {
    const [memberId, date] = key.split("|");
    const capacity = members.get(memberId)!.hoursPerDay;
    if (hours > capacity) problems.push(`${memberId} has ${hours} h on ${date} (capacity ${capacity} h)`);
  }

  for (const s of plan.scheduled) {
    for (const dep of tasks.get(s.taskId)?.dependsOn ?? []) {
      const depDate = dateOf.get(dep);
      if (!depDate) problems.push(`task ${s.taskId} depends on ${dep}, which is not scheduled`);
      else if (depDate >= s.date) problems.push(`task ${s.taskId} is not after its dependency ${dep}`);
    }
  }
  return problems;
}

    The schema describes the result only. The checker is the real grader: for a plan that put a dependent task on the same day as its dependency, overloaded a member, and scheduled a task after its due date, it returns (output of a local run on sample data):

    flawed plan: [
  'task t4: scheduled after its due date',
  'u1 has 9 h on 2026-11-16 (capacity 6 h)',
  'task t2 is not after its dependency t1'
]
fixed plan: []

    The same function serves three purposes: it grades evaluation runs, it gates what the API returns to users, and its messages can drive one repair attempt, as in structured output and validation.

    Calling a reasoning model¶

    // src/ai/planner/plan-week-openai.ts
import OpenAI from "openai";
import { zodTextFormat } from "openai/helpers/zod";
import { z } from "zod";
import { logger } from "../../logger.js";
import { WeekPlan, checkPlan, type PlannerInput } from "./week-plan.js";

const client = new OpenAI();
const Effort = z.enum(["none", "minimal", "low", "medium", "high", "xhigh", "max"]);

const INSTRUCTIONS = `Plan next week's work for a small team.
Goal: schedule as much high-priority work as possible.
Hard constraints, all of which must hold:
- A task is scheduled only after every task it depends on, on an earlier day.
- No member works more than their hours per day.
- No task is scheduled after its due date.
Put every task you cannot schedule under "unscheduled" with a one-sentence reason.`;

export async function planWeek(input: PlannerInput) {
  const model = process.env.PLANNER_MODEL; // a reasoning model from the provider's list
  if (!model) throw new Error("PLANNER_MODEL is not configured");
  const effort = Effort.parse(process.env.PLANNER_EFFORT ?? "medium"); // tuned with evals

  const started = Date.now();
  const response = await client.responses.parse({
    model,
    reasoning: { effort, summary: "auto" }, // supported values depend on the model
    instructions: INSTRUCTIONS,
    input: `<planning_input>\n${JSON.stringify(input)}\n</planning_input>`,
    text: { format: zodTextFormat(WeekPlan, "week_plan") },
    max_output_tokens: 25_000, // reasoning tokens count against this limit too
    store: false,
  });

  const reasoningTokens = response.usage?.output_tokens_details.reasoning_tokens ?? 0;
  logger.info({
    feature: "week-planner", model: response.model, effort, status: response.status,
    latencyMs: Date.now() - started,
    outputTokens: response.usage?.output_tokens, reasoningTokens,
  }, "planner call");

  if (response.status === "incomplete") {
    // The budget can run out during reasoning, before any visible answer.
    return { ok: false as const, reason: response.incomplete_details?.reason ?? "incomplete" };
  }
  const plan = response.output_parsed;
  if (!plan) return { ok: false as const, reason: "no parsed plan" };

  const reasoningSummary = response.output
    .flatMap((item) => (item.type === "reasoning" ? item.summary.map((s) => s.text) : []))
    .join("\n"); // a summary written for people, not the raw reasoning

  const problems = checkPlan(plan, input); // the grade comes from code, not from the summary
  return { ok: problems.length === 0, plan, problems, reasoningSummary, reasoningTokens };
}
    • The prompt states the goal and the rules, not a procedure, and says nothing about thinking step by step.
    • PLANNER_MODEL and PLANNER_EFFORT come from configuration. Pick a reasoning model from the provider's model list, check which effort values its model page supports, and set the effort from your sweep. Effort.parse rejects a typo at startup instead of sending it to the API.
    • Reasoning and structured output combine. The schema constrains the final answer only; the reasoning happens before it.
    • The log line records reasoning tokens separately, which is what shows a cost increase after a model or effort change.

    Run against a local stub of the Responses API with a fake key, the function sent "reasoning":{"effort":"low","summary":"auto"} with max_output_tokens: 25000, and returned the summary text and the reasoning-token count from the stub's response:

    {"ok":true,"problems":[],"reasoningSummary":"Checked capacity and dependencies.","reasoningTokens":2100}

    The same call with Anthropic¶

    // src/ai/planner/plan-week-anthropic.ts (the same call with Anthropic)
import Anthropic from "@anthropic-ai/sdk";
import { zodOutputFormat } from "@anthropic-ai/sdk/helpers/zod";
import { WeekPlan, type PlannerInput } from "./week-plan.js";

const anthropic = new Anthropic();

export async function planWeekAnthropic(input: PlannerInput, instructions: string) {
  // Large max_tokens values need streaming: the SDK refuses non-streaming
  // requests that could run longer than ten minutes.
  const stream = anthropic.messages.stream({
    model: process.env.PLANNER_MODEL ?? "",
    max_tokens: 32_000, // a hard ceiling; effort is only guidance
    thinking: { type: "adaptive", display: "summarized" }, // accepted values depend on the model
    output_config: { effort: "medium", format: zodOutputFormat(WeekPlan) },
    system: instructions,
    messages: [{ role: "user", content: `<planning_input>\n${JSON.stringify(input)}\n</planning_input>` }],
  });
  const message = await stream.finalMessage();

  const thinkingSummary = message.content
    .flatMap((block) => (block.type === "thinking" ? [block.thinking] : []))
    .join("\n");
  return {
    plan: message.parsed_output,
    stopReason: message.stop_reason,
    thinkingSummary,
    thinkingTokens: message.usage.output_tokens_details?.thinking_tokens ?? 0,
    // In a tool loop, send message.content back unchanged: thinking blocks carry
    // a signature, and edited or dropped blocks are rejected.
  };
}
    • thinking: { type: "adaptive" } is the default on Claude 5 models and must be set explicitly on Claude Opus 4.6 to 4.8; older models such as Claude Opus 4.5 reject it and use enabled with budget_tokens instead. Check the per-model table in Anthropic's thinking documentation for the model you configure.
    • display: "summarized" opts in to readable thinking; leave it out (the omitted default on Claude 5 models) when nobody reads it, so the answer starts streaming sooner.
    • effort and the JSON schema both live in output_config.

    Google and the Vercel AI SDK¶

    // src/ai/planner/equivalents.ts (Google Interactions API and Vercel AI SDK)
import { GoogleGenAI } from "@google/genai";
import { generateText } from "ai";
import { openai } from "@ai-sdk/openai";

export async function googleThinking(prompt: string) {
  const ai = new GoogleGenAI({});
  const interaction = await ai.interactions.create({
    model: process.env.PLANNER_MODEL ?? "",
    input: prompt,
    generation_config: { thinking_level: "medium", thinking_summaries: "auto" },
  });
  return interaction.output_text;
}

export async function aiSdkThinking(prompt: string) {
  const result = await generateText({
    model: openai(process.env.PLANNER_MODEL ?? ""),
    prompt,
    reasoning: "medium", // provider-neutral; mapped to each provider's own setting
  });
  return { text: result.text, reasoning: result.reasoningText };
}

    On Google, the supported thinking_level values differ by model (the thinking documentation lists them per model), and Gemini models think dynamically by default. The generateContent method of the same SDK uses a thinkingConfig object (thinkingLevel, thinkingBudget, includeThoughts) instead. All the examples in this lesson were type-checked against openai 7.57, @anthropic-ai/sdk 1.8, @google/genai 3.3, and ai 7.0.

    Choosing the effort level with a sweep¶

    // src/ai/planner/effort-sweep.ts
export interface RunResult {
  effort: string;
  caseId: string;
  passed: boolean;        // checkPlan returned no problems
  latencyMs: number;
  outputTokens: number;   // includes reasoning tokens
}

export interface EffortSummary {
  effort: string;
  passRate: number;
  p50Ms: number;
  p95Ms: number;
  meanOutputTokens: number;
}

function percentile(sorted: number[], p: number): number {
  const index = Math.min(sorted.length - 1, Math.ceil((p / 100) * sorted.length) - 1);
  return sorted[Math.max(0, index)];
}

export function summarize(results: RunResult[]): EffortSummary[] {
  const byEffort = new Map<string, RunResult[]>();
  for (const r of results) byEffort.set(r.effort, [...(byEffort.get(r.effort) ?? []), r]);
  return [...byEffort].map(([effort, runs]) => {
    const latencies = runs.map((r) => r.latencyMs).sort((a, b) => a - b);
    return {
      effort,
      passRate: runs.filter((r) => r.passed).length / runs.length,
      p50Ms: percentile(latencies, 50),
      p95Ms: percentile(latencies, 95),
      meanOutputTokens: Math.round(runs.reduce((s, r) => s + r.outputTokens, 0) / runs.length),
    };
  });
}

/** The cheapest effort that meets the quality bar and the latency budget. */
export function pickEffort(
  summaries: EffortSummary[],
  order: string[],
  minPassRate: number,
  maxP95Ms: number,
): EffortSummary | undefined {
  return order
    .map((e) => summaries.find((s) => s.effort === e))
    .find((s) => s !== undefined && s.passRate >= minPassRate && s.p95Ms <= maxP95Ms);
}

    The evaluation runner calls planWeek for every case at every effort level, grades each plan with checkPlan, and feeds the results to summarize. With made-up sample results for ten cases per level, a 90 percent pass-rate bar, and a 20-second p95 budget, the functions above print (output of a local run, with the table borders removed):

    effort   passRate  p50Ms  p95Ms  meanOutputTokens
low      0.7       4600   6600   1680
medium   0.9       10600  15100  4380
high     0.9       23000  33000  9980
pick: medium

    With real results the numbers will differ; the procedure is the point. Re-run the sweep whenever the model, the prompt, or the case set changes, and store the chosen effort in configuration next to the model ID.

    Best practices¶

    • Use reasoning models per feature, for problems with multi-step reasoning and checkable rules; keep fast models for drafts, extraction, and classification.
    • Set the effort explicitly from an effort sweep on your evaluation set, and store it in configuration with the model ID.
    • Set a generous hard output ceiling at first, then tune it from logged usage; alert on incomplete responses.
    • Prompt with the goal, the constraints, and the success criteria; do not ask for step-by-step reasoning; try zero-shot first.
    • Keep reasoning out of output schemas; ask for a short explanation only if users need one.
    • Grade outcomes with code and evaluation sets, over repeated runs; never grade the reasoning summary.
    • Pass reasoning items, signatures, and thought blocks back unchanged in multi-turn and tool loops.
    • Log reasoning tokens separately from visible output, and track cost and latency per feature.
    • Stream progress or run long reasoning in background jobs; do not hold users on a blank screen.
    • Re-run the sweep and the evaluation set on every model change.

    In production¶

    • The planner times out for some teams. Detect: p95 latency for large teams is several times the median, and some requests hit the gateway timeout. Cause: reasoning grows with the number of tasks and constraints, and the request ran synchronously at high effort. Fix: move planning to a background job with a notification, cap the number of tasks per request, and pick the effort from a sweep that includes large inputs.
    • Paid requests return no answer. Detect: a rising share of responses with status: "incomplete" (or stop_reason: "max_tokens") and empty output, while token spend keeps growing. Cause: the output ceiling was sized for the visible answer, and reasoning used it up first. Fix: raise the ceiling with headroom, lower the effort, and alert on the incomplete rate.
    • A model upgrade doubled the bill. Detect: reasoning tokens per request jump on the day the model ID changed, with no change in pass rate. Cause: the new model's default effort or its calibration differs, and the feature inherited the default. Fix: set effort explicitly, run a fresh sweep for the new model, and gate model changes on cost per passing case.
    • Requests fail with 400 errors after switching models. Detect: errors mention an invalid thinking type, budget_tokens, or temperature. Cause: code written for an older model sends a manual thinking budget or sampling parameters that newer models reject. Fix: move to effort and adaptive thinking, drop sampling parameters, and test each configured model in CI against a staging key.
    • A tool loop breaks on the second turn. Detect: the follow-up request after a tool call fails with an error about modified thinking blocks or missing reasoning items. Cause: the code rebuilt the assistant message from its text and dropped the thinking blocks or reasoning items. Fix: append the provider's returned content unchanged, including signatures and encrypted items.
    • Support relies on reasoning summaries to explain decisions. Detect: a user disputes a plan, and the summary's explanation does not match the rules that actually excluded the task. Cause: summaries were treated as an audit trail. Fix: return a structured, code-verified reason per unscheduled task, and use summaries only for debugging.

    Important terminology¶

    • Reasoning model: a model trained to generate and use internal reasoning before answering.
    • Test-time compute: computation spent while answering a request, as opposed to during training.
    • Reasoning (thinking) tokens: the tokens a model generates while reasoning; billed as output, usually not returned.
    • Effort: a soft setting for how much work a model puts into a response, including how much it thinks.
    • Thinking budget: an older, explicit token budget for reasoning, such as Anthropic's budget_tokens.
    • Adaptive thinking: a mode in which the model decides when and how much to think, guided by effort.
    • Output ceiling: the hard maximum of output tokens, reasoning included.
    • Reasoning summary: a human-readable summary of the reasoning, produced on request; not the raw reasoning.
    • Opaque carry-over: encrypted reasoning items or signed thinking blocks passed back so the model can continue its reasoning.
    • Faithfulness: how well visible reasoning reflects what actually produced the answer.
    • Effort sweep: running the same evaluation set at several effort levels to pick the cheapest one that meets the quality bar.

    Common mistakes and misconceptions¶

    • "A reasoning model is always better." On simple tasks it is slower, costlier, and sometimes worse.
    • "Effort is a token budget." It is guidance; the output ceiling is the only hard limit.
    • "Think step by step" still helps. Reasoning models reason anyway, and the instruction can hurt.
    • "Hidden reasoning is free." It is billed as output and counts against the output limit.
    • "The summary explains the decision." It is a summary written for people, and reasoning text is often unfaithful to the real cause.
    • "Settings carry over between models." Effort levels and defaults are calibrated per model; sweep again.
    • "Temperature tunes a reasoning model." Several providers ignore or reject sampling parameters for these models.
